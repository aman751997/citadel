<h2 id="prologue">Prologue · The stone you will never regret</h2>

<Scene chapter="Prologue" title="The apprentice on the big stone" mood="dawn">

Wendmouth has no bridge. The river is crossed on stepping stones, each carved with a number, and the Wayfinders’ Guild teaches the crossing to anyone who can pay in patience.

On your first morning an apprentice ahead of you leaps for the widest, flattest stone in sight. It looks like the best stone on the river. From it, the next stone is too far. He stands there, stranded, while the water rises around his boots.

The Keeper watches him without a word. Beside her, a thin man named Nix is already scratching three tiny rivers into the mud with a stick.

*"Every stone looks best from the bank,"* the Keeper says. *"Show me the stone you will never regret — and tell me why."*

</Scene>

A **greedy algorithm** builds its answer one choice at a time, makes each choice by a simple local rule (the farthest stone, the smallest card, the biggest gain), and **never takes a choice back**. That is what makes it fast: no backtracking, no table of alternatives. It is also what makes it dangerous. A greedy rule that sounds sensible is wrong surprisingly often, and wrong silently: it returns a plausible number.

So in Wendmouth nobody codes a greedy rule until they can say **why it is safe**. A greedy rule is correct when two things hold:

1. **The greedy-choice property.** Some best answer agrees with the greedy’s first choice. You never need to regret it.
2. **Optimal substructure.** After that choice, what remains is the same kind of problem, only smaller, so the same rule applies again.

The guild teaches three ways to prove the first property. Every chapter in this book names the one it uses.

- **Stays ahead.** After every step, the greedy is at least as far along as any other solution after the same number of steps — it reaches as far, has as much, has cut as soon. If it is never behind, it cannot finish behind.
- **The exchange argument.** Take any best answer that disagrees with the greedy at its first difference. Swap that choice for the greedy’s and show the answer gets no worse. Repeat until it *is* the greedy’s answer.
- **The forced move.** Sometimes there is only one choice that could possibly be right — the smallest card can only start a group. A forced choice needs no exchange: there is nothing to swap it with.

The Keeper asks her three questions here as everywhere in the realm:

1. **What is settled?** Every choice already made. Greedy never revisits one.
2. **Why is this move safe?** Name the proof: stays ahead, exchange, or forced. “It looks best” is not an answer.
3. **When do I stop?** When the input is used up — or as soon as the summary you carry already decides the answer.

> “I may take this stone, because I can prove it never costs me the crossing.” That is the promise. Every chapter keeps it with a different proof.

### Nix’s three stones: hunt for the counterexample first

Before anyone in the guild writes code, Nix tries to break the rule on **three tiny inputs**, each chosen to hurt:

1. **The degenerate stone.** One element. All values equal. All negative. Zeros.
2. **The trap.** A big, tempting early choice that blocks something better later — the wide flat stone with nothing beyond it.
3. **The tie.** Two choices look equally good. Does the rule’s tie-break matter?

If any of them breaks the rule, drop the rule — the fix is usually to try every choice and remember the results (dynamic programming). If none does, write the one-sentence proof. Two minutes of hunting either saves you from a wrong answer or tells you what the proof must say.

Run Nix’s trap: largest-first takes 4, then 1, then 1 — **3 coins**. But 3 + 3 makes 6 with **2**. The rule dies on its first tiny input. The fix is to try every last coin with memory (Coin Change, a DP problem). The epilogue, [When the river lies](#choose-the-tool), takes this apart.

</Reveal>

### When this pattern fires

- **An optimisation (max, min, fewest, “can you?”) where each step has a natural best-looking choice**: farthest reach, smallest remaining card, biggest gain, earliest end.
- **One pass that carries one or two summary values** — a running total, a farthest reach, a tank — instead of a table of possibilities.
- **Sorting by one key makes every decision obvious** (smallest cookie to the least hungry child; heaviest person first).
- **The brute force tries all subsets, all orders or all subarrays** — 2ⁿ, n! or n² — and the greedy runs in **O(n) or O(n log n)**.

And the warning signs that greedy will fail: **arbitrary coin values**, **whole (0/1) items with weights**, **weighted** choices where one big item is worth many small ones. Hunt hardest there.

One honest note before the crossings. Some of these chapters are filed under dynamic programming in other books — Kadane’s algorithm is the textbook example. That is not a contradiction. **A greedy algorithm is a DP whose proof lets it keep a single candidate per step** instead of a whole table.

<h2 id="max-subarray">Chapter 1 · The burden on your back</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 1" title="The burden on your back" mood="dawn">

The toll stones of the lower ford are older than the guild. Step on one and it pays you coins; step on another and it takes them. Every wayfinder walks a single unbroken stretch of them, and keeps whatever the stretch adds up to.

Run walks the stones with a satchel, adding each stone’s coins as he goes. Behind him, Best chalks the largest total the satchel has ever held. By the third stone the satchel is in debt — it holds −2.

*"Put it down,"* says the Keeper.

Run frowns. *"It might come back."*

*"It might,"* she agrees. *"But will anything ahead of you be better with that debt on your back than without it?"*

</Scene>

**Problem: Maximum Subarray · Kadane’s algorithm, a running total that resets**

Find the largest total of a contiguous, non-empty stretch. Checking every stretch is O(n²). Instead, carry one number, `run`: **the best total of a stretch that ends exactly here**. At each new value there are only two kinds of stretch ending here — the value alone, or the value added to a stretch that ended one step earlier. So:

```text
run  = max(x, run + x)        restart here, or extend
best = max(best, run)
```

And `run + x` loses to `x` alone **exactly when `run` is negative**. That is the whole rule: **a running total that resets when it becomes a burden.** Not when the next value is negative — a stretch often crosses a negative value and is still worth keeping.

**Hunt first.** Nix scratches three rivers:

- `[5, −2, 5]` — the trap for “restart whenever the next value is negative”. That rule restarts at −2 and never sees 5 − 2 + 5 = **8**; it answers 5.
- `[−3, −1, −2]` — every value negative. The answer is **−1**, the largest single value; the stretch must be non-empty, so 0 is not allowed.
- `[0, 0]` — a tie: with `run = 0`, extending and restarting give the same total, so either is fine.

<Trace title="Carry it or put it down?" steps={kadane} takeaway="Remember: restart when the RUN is negative, not when the next value is. Start run and best at nums[0], never at 0." />

**Why it works — the exchange argument.** Take any stretch that ends at index `i` and starts before it. Split it into “everything before `i`” and `x`. If the part before `i` has a negative total, **exchange** the stretch for `x` alone: strictly better. If it has a non-negative total, the best such part is the best stretch ending at `i − 1`, which is `run`. So `max(x, run + x)` really is the best stretch ending at `i`. Every stretch ends somewhere, so the largest `run` ever seen is the answer.

**What stays true:** after handling index `i`, `run` is the best total of a non-empty stretch ending exactly at `i`, and `best` is the best total of any non-empty stretch inside `0..i`.

**At the loop exit:** `run` is the best stretch ending at the **last** index — not the answer. `best` is the answer. Say both out loud; mixing them up is the bug that returns 5 instead of 6 above.

<Reveal q="Why not start run and best at 0, the way a sum usually starts?">

Because 0 is the total of the **empty** stretch, and the problem forbids it. On `[−3, −1, −2]` a version that starts at 0 and resets the run to 0 never sees anything above 0 and returns **0**, while the right answer is −1. Starting both at `nums[0]` and restarting at the value itself (not at 0) keeps every candidate non-empty.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="Isn’t this dynamic programming?">

Yes, and that is fine. Write `dp[i]` for the best stretch ending at `i`: `dp[i] = max(nums[i], dp[i − 1] + nums[i])`. Kadane keeps only the latest `dp` value. The greedy reading is the reset rule — the proof that a negative prefix is never worth keeping. The <a href={`${BASE}dsa/lessons/prefix-sum/`}>Prefix Sum</a> book has a third view: the best stretch ending at `j` is the ledger line at `j` minus the smallest line above it.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.kadane}>

```java id=maxSubArray
public int maxSubArray(int[] nums) {
    int run = nums[0], best = nums[0];             // non-empty: start from the first value, never from 0
    for (int i = 1; i < nums.length; i++) {
        run = Math.max(nums[i], run + nums[i]);    // restart when the old run is a burden (negative)
        best = Math.max(best, run);
    }
    return best;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) extra space.** LeetCode’s bounds (at most 10⁵ values within ±10⁴) keep every total within ±10⁹, inside `int`.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** the empty-stretch version.

```java fragment
int run = 0, best = 0;                             // BUG: 0 is the empty stretch
for (int x : nums) { run = Math.max(0, run + x); best = Math.max(best, run); }
return best;                                       // [-3, -1, -2] -> 0 instead of -1
```

<h2 id="max-product">Chapter 2 · The mirror pools</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 2" title="The mirror pools" mood="dusk">

Upstream, between the stones, lie the mirror pools. Step over one and your lantern’s glow is multiplied by the number painted on its rim. Some rims say 3. Some say −4, and the glow turns inside out, dark becoming bright. One rim says 0, and the lantern goes out.

Hi carries the brightest glow a stretch of pools could give you so far. His twin, Lo, carries the darkest — the most negative — and everyone wonders why the guild bothers to pay her.

Then the next rim reads −4. Hi’s glow of 3 becomes −12. Lo’s −6 becomes 24.

*"Now,"* says the Keeper, *"tell me again which twin was useless."*

</Scene>

**Problem: Maximum Product Subarray · Kadane’s variant, carry both extremes**

Same shape as Chapter 1, one rule changed: multiply instead of add. The product of a stretch ending here is `x` times the product of a stretch ending one step earlier — or `x` alone. Which earlier product makes it largest?

- If `x > 0`, the **largest** earlier product.
- If `x < 0`, the **smallest** (most negative) earlier product — a negative times a negative flips to a large positive.
- If `x = 0`, it doesn’t matter: everything through the zero becomes 0, and the next value starts fresh.

So carry two numbers: `hi`, the largest product of a stretch ending here, and `lo`, the smallest.

**Hunt first.**

- `[−2, 3, −4]` — tracking only `hi` answers **3**; the whole array gives **24**.
- `[2, 3, 0, 4]` — a zero resets everything; the answer is **6**, from `[2, 3]`.
- `[−2]` — a single negative value; the answer is **−2**.

**Why it works — keep every survivor.** For a fixed `x`, the map “earlier product `P` ↦ `x × P`” is increasing when `x > 0` and decreasing when `x < 0`. Either way its maximum and minimum over all earlier products are reached at the **extremes**, `hi` and `lo`. So the only candidates that can ever win are `x`, `hi × x` and `lo × x`, and throwing every other earlier product away loses nothing. It is stays-ahead with two runners: no stretch ending at `i` has a product above `hi` or below `lo`.

**What stays true:** after index `i`, `hi` and `lo` are the largest and smallest products of non-empty stretches ending exactly at `i`; `best` is the largest product of any stretch in `0..i`.

<Reveal q="Some solutions swap hi and lo when x is negative. Is that the same thing?">

Yes. Multiplying by a negative turns the largest product into the smallest and vice versa, so `if (x < 0) swap(hi, lo)` followed by `hi = max(x, hi × x)` and `lo = min(x, lo × x)` gives exactly the same values. Use whichever you can explain.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.product}>

```java id=maxProduct
public int maxProduct(int[] nums) {
    int hi = nums[0], lo = nums[0], best = nums[0];   // largest / smallest product of a stretch ending here
    for (int i = 1; i < nums.length; i++) {
        int x = nums[i];
        int a = hi * x, b = lo * x;                   // both from the OLD hi and lo
        hi = Math.max(x, Math.max(a, b));
        lo = Math.min(x, Math.min(a, b));
        best = Math.max(best, hi);
    }
    return best;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) extra space.** LeetCode states that every subarray’s product fits in a 32-bit integer; `hi × x` and `lo × x` are products of real subarrays, so they fit too. (If you ever meet a judge whose tests break that promise, carry `hi` and `lo` as `long`.)

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** overwriting `hi` before `lo` reads it.

```java fragment
hi = Math.max(x, Math.max(hi * x, lo * x));
lo = Math.min(x, Math.min(hi * x, lo * x));    // BUG: uses the NEW hi
// [-2, 3, -4, -1] -> 96, a product no stretch has (the answer is 24)
```

And the classic one: dropping `lo` entirely answers 3 on `[−2, 3, −4]`. [Solve Maximum Product Subarray](https://leetcode.com/problems/maximum-product-subarray/).

<h2 id="gas-station">Chapter 3 · The lamp-oil ring</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 3" title="The lamp-oil ring" mood="night">

Around the lake above the falls runs a ring path with lamp posts, and every night someone must walk it once, lighting each post. Each post has a can of oil to collect; each stretch of path to the next post burns some.

Tank sets out from post 0 with an empty flask and runs dry before post 1. She starts again from post 1, and runs dry again. The junior wayfinders groan: at this rate she will try every post, and walk the ring a hundred times.

The Keeper stops Tank at the foot of post 3 and asks two questions, not one.

*"Is there oil enough on this ring for anyone to finish? And if there is — why should you never go back to the posts you already failed from?"*

</Scene>

**Problem: Gas Station · start after the last deficit**

`gas[i]` is the oil collected at station `i`, `cost[i]` the oil burned driving to station `i + 1` (wrapping around). Call `gas[i] − cost[i]` the station’s **gain**. Return the start that completes the ring with an empty tank, or −1. Simulating from every start is O(n²). The Keeper’s two questions split the problem in two, and **each needs its own proof**.

**Claim 1 — whether.** If total gas &lt; total cost, no start works: a full lap changes the tank by the total gain, which is negative. If total gas ≥ total cost, **some start works**.

*Proof (the lowest point).* Drive from station 0 without ever resetting, and write down the running total of gains, allowing it to go negative. Let station `m` be where that running total is **lowest** (just before `m`’s own gain is added). Start at `m`. On the way to any later station, your tank equals the running total there minus the lowest value — never negative. After you wrap past the end, your tank is that same difference plus the whole lap’s total, which is ≥ 0. You never run dry.

**Claim 2 — where.** One pass finds that start. Carry `tank`, the oil since the current start. **If `tank` goes negative after station `i`, no station from `start` to `i` can be a start**, so jump straight to `i + 1`.

*Proof (stays ahead).* You reached every station `k` between `start` and `i` with a tank of at least 0, and still ran dry after `i`. Starting at `k` with an *empty* tank is never better than arriving there with something, so you would run dry by `i` too. A restart happens exactly when the running total sets a new low — which is why the final `start` sits right after the lowest point of Claim 1.

<Trace title="Restart, and trust the total" steps={gas} takeaway="Remember: tank decides WHERE (restart after a deficit); total decides WHETHER (−1 if negative). Both proofs, both checks." />

**Hunt first.** One station (`[5]`, `[4]`: start 0). A ring that can’t be finished (LeetCode’s second example: total gas 9, total cost 10). Every gain 0 (every start works; this code returns 0, the first).

**What stays true:** at the top of iteration `i`, `tank` is the oil gained from `start` to `i − 1`, it is ≥ 0, and no station before `start` can be a valid start. `total` is the gain of stations `0..i − 1`.

**At the loop exit:** `total` answers *whether*, `start` answers *where*. Neither alone is enough.

<Reveal q="The scan never drives the wrap-around from start back to start. Why trust it?">

Because Claim 1 already did. The final `start` sits right after the lowest point of the running total, and the lowest-point proof covers the whole lap, wrap-around included, whenever the total is ≥ 0. The loop does not need to simulate what the proof guarantees.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.gas}>

```java id=canCompleteCircuit
public int canCompleteCircuit(int[] gas, int[] cost) {
    int total = 0, tank = 0, start = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < gas.length; i++) {
        int gain = gas[i] - cost[i];
        total += gain;                     // decides WHETHER: the whole lap
        tank += gain;                      // decides WHERE: oil since the current start
        if (tank < 0) {                    // can't reach i + 1: no station in start..i can start
            start = i + 1;
            tank = 0;
        }
    }
    return total >= 0 ? start : -1;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) extra space.** With at most 10⁵ stations and amounts up to 10⁴, totals stay within ±10⁹, inside `int`. LeetCode promises the answer is unique when it exists; in general several starts may work, and this returns the one just after the first lowest point.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** returning `start` without the total check. On gas `[2, 3, 4]`, cost `[3, 4, 3]` it returns **2**, but the lap loses 1 unit of oil and the answer is −1. [Solve Gas Station](https://leetcode.com/problems/gas-station/).

<h2 id="jump-game">Chapter 4 · Can you cross at all?</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 4" title="Can you cross at all?">

The great crossing is a line of stones with a number carved into each: the longest leap you may take from it. You may always leap shorter.

Nix proposes searching every path — every stone, every leap length. Far, the guild’s scout, doesn’t move. She stands on the first stone, reads its number, and shouts a single stone index across the water. At the next stone she shouts again, sometimes a bigger number, never a smaller one.

*"She hasn’t picked a path,"* Nix complains.

*"She doesn’t need one,"* says the Keeper. *"If you can reach a stone, which stones before it can you reach?"*

</Scene>

**Problem: Jump Game · track the farthest reachable stone**

From stone `i` you may jump forward 1 to `nums[i]` stones. Can you reach the last stone? Searching paths can blow up; one number is enough.

The key fact: **the reachable stones always form one unbroken block, `0..far`.** If you can land on stone `j`, then every stone between the take-off stone and `j` is reachable by a shorter leap from the same take-off stone. So walk left to right, carrying `far`: the farthest stone reachable from any stone seen so far. If you ever stand at an index beyond `far`, there is a gap nobody can cross.

**Hunt first.** `[0]` — you are already on the last stone: **true**. `[3, 2, 1, 0, 4]` — every stone reaches stone 3 at most: **false**. `[2, 0, 1, 0]` — a zero you leap over: **true**.

<Figure caption="[3, 2, 1, 0, 4]. Each stone’s reach i + nums[i] is 3, 3, 3, 3. far never passes 3, so stone 4 — with its tempting 4 — is never stood on. The answer is false.">
  <ArrayStrip name="nums" values={[3, 2, 1, 0, 4]} tones={{ 4: 'out' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 3, label: 'reachable block 0..far = 0..3', tone: 'green' }]} pointers={[{ at: 3, label: 'far', tone: 'gold' }, { at: 4, label: 'gap', tone: 'red' }]} />
  <ArrayStrip name="i + nums[i]" values={[3, 3, 3, 3, '·']} tones={{ 4: 'ghost' }} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="How far can anyone get?" steps={jump} takeaway="Remember: the reachable stones form one block 0..far. A gap (i > far) is the only way to fail." />

**Why it works — stays ahead.** Claim: when the loop reaches stone `i` without failing, every stone `0..far` is reachable and no reachable stone lies beyond `far`. It holds at the start (`far = 0`). Stone `i` is inside the block, so it is reachable, and it extends the block to `i + nums[i]`. If `i > far`, no reachable stone reaches `i`, so `i` — and everything after it — is unreachable.

**What stays true:** at the top of iteration `i`, `far` is the farthest stone reachable using take-off stones `0..i − 1`.

**At the loop exit:** returning normally means no stone was ever beyond `far`, so the last stone is reachable.

<Reveal q="Some solutions walk backwards with a ‘goal’ stone. Same thing?">

Yes, mirrored. Start with `goal = n − 1`. Walking from the right, if `i + nums[i] >= goal`, stone `i` can reach the goal, so it becomes the new goal. The answer is `goal == 0`. Both are O(n); pick the one whose invariant you can say out loud.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.jump}>

```java id=canJump
public boolean canJump(int[] nums) {
    int far = 0;                                   // every stone 0..far is reachable
    for (int i = 0; i < nums.length; i++) {
        if (i > far) return false;                 // a gap: no reachable stone lands on i
        far = Math.max(far, i + nums[i]);
    }
    return true;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) extra space.** You may also stop early once `far >= nums.length − 1`.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** “any zero before the end means stuck”. On `[2, 0, 1, 0]` that returns false, but stone 0 leaps straight over the zero to stone 2, and stone 2 reaches the end. A zero only matters when `far` stops on it. [Solve Jump Game](https://leetcode.com/problems/jump-game/).

<h2 id="jump-game-ii">Chapter 5 · The fewest leaps</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 5" title="The fewest leaps" mood="storm">

The spring flood is coming, and the guild wants the crossing done in the fewest leaps. The last stone is always within reach; the question is how quickly.

A young wayfinder has a rule: *always leap as far as you can.* From the first stone he leaps two, lands on a stone carved with 1, and has to hop, hop to the end — three leaps. Nix, behind him, took a shorter first leap onto a stone carved with 3, and was across in two.

End, the old post-keeper, plants a post at the edge of what one leap can reach. Far stands beside him, calling out how far the stones inside that edge could carry you next.

*"Don’t choose where to land,"* says the Keeper. *"Count the edges."*

</Scene>

**Problem: Jump Game II · implicit breadth-first search by levels**

Same stones, new question: the fewest jumps to the last stone (always reachable). The tempting greedy — “jump as far as you can” — is wrong, and Nix’s trap shows it: on `[2, 3, 1, 1, 4]` it goes 0 → 2 → 3 → 4, **three** jumps, while 0 → 1 → 4 takes **two**. The landing spot is the wrong thing to be greedy about.

Think in rounds instead. The stones you can stand on with **at most k jumps** form one block `0..end`, by Chapter 4’s argument. While you scan the stones of that block, track `far`, the farthest any of them reaches: **that is where the block for k + 1 jumps ends.** So:

- Scan stones left to right, updating `far`.
- When the scan reaches `end`, the edge of the current block, one more jump is forced: `jumps++`, `end = far`.
- Stop before the last stone — you never jump *from* it.

You never decide which stone to land on. You only count how many times you cross an edge.

<Figure caption="[2, 3, 1, 1, 4] in levels. Level 0 is stone 0; it reaches 2, so level 1 is stones 1..2. Scanning them, stone 1 reaches 1 + 3 = 4, so level 2 is stones 3..4 and contains the last stone: 2 jumps.">
  <ArrayStrip name="nums" values={[2, 3, 1, 1, 4]} tones={{ 0: 'done', 1: 'hot', 2: 'hot', 3: 'blue', 4: 'blue' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 0, label: '0 jumps', tone: 'green' }, { from: 1, to: 2, label: '1 jump', tone: 'gold' }, { from: 3, to: 4, label: '2 jumps', tone: 'blue' }]} pointers={[{ at: 2, label: 'end after jump 1', tone: 'gold' }, { at: 4, label: 'end after jump 2', tone: 'blue' }]} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="Count the edges, not the stones" steps={jumpII} takeaway="Remember: far is updated on every stone; a jump is counted only when i reaches end. Loop to n − 2." />

**Why it works — stays ahead.** Let `E(k)` be the end of the block after `k` jumps. Claim: for any path at all, the stone it stands on after `k` jumps is at most `E(k)`. True for `k = 0`. If a path stands on `p ≤ E(k)` after `k` jumps, its next landing is at most `p + nums[p]`, and `p` was scanned before the edge `E(k)`, so `p + nums[p] ≤ far = E(k + 1)`. **The greedy’s block is never behind any path.** And every stone inside the block really is reachable in that many jumps. So the first `k` whose block contains the last stone is the minimum.

**What stays true:** whenever `i == end`, `far` is the farthest stone reachable from any stone in `0..end` — exactly the end of the next block.

**At the loop exit:** `jumps` is the number of edges crossed; `end ≥ n − 1`.

<Reveal q="Why is this breadth-first search, and why is it O(n) instead of O(n × maxJump)?">

A real BFS from stone 0 would put each stone in a queue with its distance. But the stones at distance `k` are always one contiguous run, `E(k − 1) + 1 .. E(k)`, so the queue is just a window, and two integers can hold it. Each stone is scanned once: O(n) time, O(1) space. A plain BFS that examines every jump edge costs O(n × maxJump).

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="What if the last stone might be unreachable?">

Then a block can stop growing: at `i == end`, `far` is still `end`. Add `if (far <= i) return -1;` inside the `i == end` branch, before `end = far`. LeetCode guarantees reachability here; Chapter 4 is the check if it doesn’t.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.jump2}>

```java id=jump
public int jump(int[] nums) {
    int jumps = 0, end = 0, far = 0;               // end: last stone reachable with `jumps` jumps
    for (int i = 0; i < nums.length - 1; i++) {    // never jump FROM the last stone
        far = Math.max(far, i + nums[i]);          // the next block ends at the best reach so far
        if (i == end) {                            // edge of this block: one more jump is forced
            jumps++;
            end = far;
        }
    }
    return jumps;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) extra space.** On `[0]` the loop never runs and the answer is 0.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** looping to `i < nums.length`. When the last stone is the edge of a block, the loop counts a jump *from* it: `[2, 3, 1, 1, 4]` returns 3, and `[0]` returns 1. [Solve Jump Game II](https://leetcode.com/problems/jump-game-ii/).

**Problem: Partition Labels · extend the cut to every letter’s last appearance**

A piece that contains letter `c` must reach `last[c]`, the last index of `c`. So walk the string carrying `end`: **the farthest last appearance of any letter seen in the current piece**. When `i == end`, no letter in the piece appears again: cut, record the size, and start a new piece.

**Hunt first.** `"abba"` — the shortcut “cut where the current letter ends” cuts after the second b and splits a across two pieces. `"abc"` — every letter once: `[1, 1, 1]`. `"aaaa"` — one piece: `[4]`.

<Figure caption="LeetCode’s example. The first piece must reach the last a (8); c’s last copy at 7 is already inside. The second piece starts with d (last at 14) and stretches to the last e at 15. The third runs to the end. Sizes 9, 7, 8.">

**Why it works — stays ahead, with forced cuts.** A cut after index `i` is **legal** exactly when no letter appears on both sides of it. Every valid split cuts only at legal points, and cutting at *every* legal point is itself a valid split. So the most pieces you can get is “cut at every legal point”. The scan’s test `i == end` is exactly “`i` is legal”: `end` is the farthest last appearance of any letter in the current piece, and letters of earlier pieces all ended before it began. The greedy takes each legal cut the moment it appears, so its k-th cut is never later than anyone else’s.

**What stays true:** `end` is the farthest last appearance of every letter in `s[start..i]`, and every piece already recorded contains all copies of its letters.

```java id=partitionLabels
public List<Integer> partitionLabels(String s) {
    int[] last = new int[26];
    for (int i = 0; i < s.length(); i++) last[s.charAt(i) - 'a'] = i;
    List<Integer> sizes = new ArrayList<>();
    int start = 0, end = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < s.length(); i++) {
        end = Math.max(end, last[s.charAt(i) - 'a']);   // the piece must reach this letter's last copy
        if (i == end) {                                  // nothing inside goes further: a safe cut
            sizes.add(end - start + 1);
            start = i + 1;
        }
    }
    return sizes;
}
```

**O(n) time** (two passes), **O(1) extra space** beyond the output: 26 integers.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** cutting when the *current* letter makes its last appearance (`last[c] == i`) instead of when `i == end`. On `"abba"` it cuts after index 2 and returns `[3, 1]` — an illegal split, since a hangs in both banners. The answer is `[4]`. <h2 id="hand-of-straights">Chapter 7 · The tally sticks</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 7" title="The tally sticks" mood="dusk">

Every crossing is recorded on a numbered tally stick, and at the end of the season the sticks must be tied into bundles of three consecutive numbers — 4, 5, 6 — with none left over.

Nix grabs the most common stick, a 2, and starts a bundle 2, 3, 4. It works this time. On the next pile, it strands a lonely 1 with nothing to bundle it with.

**Problem: Hand of Straights · smallest card first (TreeMap)**

Split the cards into groups of `groupSize` consecutive values, using every card. The rule: **the smallest remaining card must start a group**. No smaller card exists, so its group cannot begin below it, and a group of consecutive values that begins at `x` is exactly `x, x + 1, …, x + groupSize − 1`. Remove one of each; if any value is missing, answer false. Repeat until the cards run out.

**Hunt first.** `[1, 2, 3, 6, 2, 3, 4, 7, 8]` with groups of 3 — true (1 2 3, 2 3 4, 6 7 8). `[1, 2, 3, 4, 5]` with groups of 4 — false before you start: 5 cards don’t split into 4s. `[2, 1, 3, 4, 5, 6]` with groups of 3 — the trap for “start from the first card you see”: starting at 2 strands the 1.

<Figure caption="LeetCode’s first example as counts. Each round, the smallest value left is forced to start a group of three: 1, 2, 3, then 2, 3, 4, then 6, 7, 8. Every card is used.">
  <ArrayStrip name="round 1" values={['1 ×1', '2 ×2', '3 ×2', '4 ×1', '6 ×1', '7 ×1', '8 ×1']} indices={false} tones={{ 0: 'hot', 1: 'hot', 2: 'hot' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 2, label: 'forced: 1, 2, 3', tone: 'gold' }]} />
  <ArrayStrip name="round 2" values={['2 ×1', '3 ×1', '4 ×1', '6 ×1', '7 ×1', '8 ×1']} indices={false} tones={{ 0: 'hot', 1: 'hot', 2: 'hot' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 2, label: 'forced: 2, 3, 4', tone: 'gold' }]} />
  <ArrayStrip name="round 3" values={['6 ×1', '7 ×1', '8 ×1']} indices={false} tones={{ 0: 'hot', 1: 'hot', 2: 'hot' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 2, label: 'forced: 6, 7, 8', tone: 'gold' }]} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="Which bundle must it join?" steps={straights} takeaway="Remember: the smallest remaining card has exactly one possible group. A hole in that group means no arrangement exists at all." />

**Why it works — the forced move.** Every valid arrangement puts the smallest card `x` in some group, and that group can only be `x .. x + groupSize − 1`. So every valid arrangement contains this group; removing it leaves a smaller hand that must be arrangeable on its own (optimal substructure). There is no exchange to make, because there was never a choice. And if a value in the forced group is missing, **no arrangement exists** — not merely “this attempt failed”, which is why the code can return false at once with no backtracking.

**What stays true:** the map holds exactly the cards not yet grouped, and every group formed so far is part of every valid arrangement.

<Reveal q="Would starting from the LARGEST card work too?">

Yes, by the mirror argument: nothing is larger, so the largest card must **end** its group. Use `lastKey()` and count downward. What fails is starting anywhere else — the first card in input order, or the most common value — because those cards still have a choice, and a wrong choice strands someone.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.straights}>

```java id=isNStraightHand
public boolean isNStraightHand(int[] hand, int groupSize) {
    if (hand.length % groupSize != 0) return false;
    TreeMap<Integer, Integer> count = new TreeMap<>();
    for (int card : hand) count.merge(card, 1, Integer::sum);
    while (!count.isEmpty()) {
        int first = count.firstKey();                  // the smallest card MUST start a group
        for (int v = first; v < first + groupSize; v++) {
            Integer have = count.get(v);
            if (have == null) return false;            // a hole in the forced group: no arrangement exists
            if (have == 1) count.remove(v); else count.put(v, have - 1);
        }
    }
    return true;
}
```

**O(n log n) time, O(n) extra space**: every card is removed exactly once, each with an O(log n) map operation. Values reach 10⁹ and `groupSize` 10⁴, so `first + groupSize` stays inside `int`. Divide Array in Sets of K Consecutive Numbers is the same problem.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** walking the cards in input order with a `HashMap` and starting a group from each card that is still there. On `[2, 1, 3, 4, 5, 6]` with groups of 3 it starts 2, 3, 4, strands the 1, and returns false — but 1, 2, 3 and 4, 5, 6 work. [Solve Hand of Straights](https://leetcode.com/problems/hand-of-straights/).

<h2 id="stock-ii">Side quest 1 · Every rising step</h2>

**The tile says:** a share’s price is known for every day. You may hold at most one share, but you may buy and sell as often as you like (selling and buying again on the same day is allowed). Maximise the total profit.

**The reasoning:** a trade that buys on day `a` and sells on day `b` earns `p[b] − p[a]`, which is the sum of the day-to-day changes from `a` to `b`. **Every rising step can be earned on its own** (buy yesterday, sell today), and **no falling step ever has to be taken**. Exchange argument: replace any trade with the rising steps inside it; the profit never goes down. And no plan can beat the sum of all rising steps, because each plan’s profit is a sum of daily changes over days it holds, and dropping the falling ones only helps.

<Figure caption="[7, 1, 5, 3, 6, 4]. Daily changes −6, +4, −2, +3, −2. Take the +4 (buy at 1, sell at 5) and the +3 (buy at 3, sell at 6): profit 7.">

  <ArrayStrip name="change" values={['·', -6, 4, -2, 3, -2]} tones={{ 2: 'done', 4: 'done', 1: 'out', 3: 'out', 5: 'out', 0: 'ghost' }} />
</Figure>

<HintLadder hints={hints.stock}>

```java id=maxProfit
public int maxProfit(int[] prices) {
    int profit = 0;
    for (int i = 1; i < prices.length; i++)
        profit += Math.max(0, prices[i] - prices[i - 1]);   // take every rising step, skip every fall
    return profit;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) extra space.**

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** now every sale costs a fee. Why does the step-by-step trick break? (Each separate step would pay the fee; merging steps into one long trade pays it once. The choice now depends on the future, so carry two states — holding and not holding — which is a small DP.) <h2 id="assign-cookies">Side quest 2 · Bread for the ferry children</h2>

**The tile says:** each child waiting for the ferry needs a bread roll of at least size `g[i]`; the rolls have sizes `s[j]`. Each child gets at most one roll. Satisfy as many children as possible.

**The reasoning:** sort both lists. Give the **least hungry** child the **smallest roll that satisfies them**. Exchange argument: suppose a best plan gives that child a roll `y` instead of the smallest fitting roll `x`. Then `y ≥ x`. If `x` is unused, swap `y` for `x`. If another child has `x`, swap their rolls: that child needed at most `x`, and `y ≥ x`, so both are still satisfied. Either way the count is unchanged. And a roll too small for the least hungry child is too small for everyone: throw it away.

<Reveal q="Hunger [1, 2, 3], rolls [1, 1]. And hunger [1, 2], rolls [1, 2, 3]?">

First: the roll of 1 satisfies the child who needs 1; the second roll of 1 is too small for the child who needs 2, and therefore for everyone left. **1.** Second: roll 1 to the child needing 1, roll 2 to the child needing 2, and the roll of 3 is spare. **2.**

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.cookies}>

```java id=findContentChildren
public int findContentChildren(int[] g, int[] s) {
    Arrays.sort(g);
    Arrays.sort(s);
    int child = 0;                                     // least hungry child still waiting
    for (int roll = 0; roll < s.length && child < g.length; roll++)
        if (s[roll] >= g[child]) child++;              // smallest roll that fits: never a waste
    return child;                                      // a roll too small here is too small for everyone
}
```

**O(n log n + m log m)** for the two sorts, then one linear walk.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** what if a child may take several rolls whose sizes add up to their need? (The swap argument breaks — two small rolls might serve one child or two — and the problem becomes a much harder packing problem.) [Solve Assign Cookies](https://leetcode.com/problems/assign-cookies/).

<h2 id="boats">Side quest 3 · Two to a coracle</h2>

**The tile says:** the guild’s coracles carry at most two people and at most `limit` in total weight; nobody is heavier than `limit`. Carry everyone in the fewest coracles.

**The reasoning:** look at the **heaviest** person. They must leave in some coracle. If even the lightest person can’t ride with them, nobody can: they go alone. Otherwise, pair them with the lightest. Exchange argument: in a best plan, say the heaviest `h` rides with `p` (or alone) and the lightest `l` rides with `q` (or alone). Swap `p` and `l`: `h + l` fits by assumption, and `p + q ≤ h + p` (since `q ≤ h`) also fits. No extra coracle. Sort, then two indices close in from both ends.

<Reveal q="Weights [3, 2, 2, 1], limit 3. How many coracles?">

Sorted: 1, 2, 2, 3. The 3 can’t ride with the 1 (4 > 3): alone. The 2 rides with the 1 (3 ≤ 3). The last 2 rides alone. **3 coracles.**

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.boats}>

```java id=numRescueBoats
public int numRescueBoats(int[] people, int limit) {
    Arrays.sort(people);
    int boats = 0, light = 0, heavy = people.length - 1;
    while (light <= heavy) {
        if (people[light] + people[heavy] <= limit) light++;   // the lightest rides along if anyone can
        heavy--;                                               // the heaviest leaves in this boat regardless
        boats++;
    }
    return boats;
}
```

**O(n log n)** for the sort; the loop is O(n). When `light == heavy`, one person is left and takes a coracle alone.

</HintLadder>

<h2 id="candy">Side quest 4 · Sweets for the stone-counters</h2>

**The tile says:** children stand in a line with ratings. Each gets at least one sweet, and a child rated higher than an **immediate neighbour** must get more than that neighbour. Use the fewest sweets.

**The reasoning:** every child has two rules, one per side. Satisfy them **one direction at a time**. A left-to-right pass gives `L[i]`: 1, or `L[i − 1] + 1` when rated above the left neighbour. A right-to-left pass gives `R[i]` the same way. **Stays ahead in both directions:** by induction along each rising run, any valid assignment gives child `i` at least `L[i]` and at least `R[i]`. And `max(L[i], R[i])` satisfies every rule, so it is the minimum. The second pass computes that max in place.

```java id=candy
public int candy(int[] ratings) {
    int n = ratings.length;
    int[] c = new int[n];
    Arrays.fill(c, 1);
    for (int i = 1; i < n; i++)                        // left neighbours
        if (ratings[i] > ratings[i - 1]) c[i] = c[i - 1] + 1;
    for (int i = n - 2; i >= 0; i--)                   // right neighbours, keeping the stricter need
        if (ratings[i] > ratings[i + 1]) c[i] = Math.max(c[i], c[i + 1] + 1);
    int total = 0;
    for (int x : c) total += x;
    return total;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(n) extra space.**

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** writing `c[i] = c[i + 1] + 1` in the second pass, without the max. On `[1, 3, 4, 5, 2]` it overwrites the 5’s left-pass 4 with 2, so the 5 gets fewer sweets than the 4 beside it: total 9, invalid. The answer is 11.

**Transfer test:** why does a single left-to-right pass fail on `[5, 4, 3, 2, 1]`? (It gives everyone 1 sweet, total 5. Every rule here compares a child with the neighbour on their right, and only the right-to-left pass looks that way: the answer is 15.) [Solve Candy](https://leetcode.com/problems/candy/).

<h2 id="wildcard-parens">Side quest 5 · Jokers in the brackets</h2>

**The tile says:** a string of `(`, `)` and `*`, where each `*` may be read as `(`, as `)`, or as nothing. Can it be read as correctly matched brackets?

**The reasoning:** don’t guess what each `*` is. Track **every possible count of open brackets at once**. Those counts always form a range `lo..hi`: `(` moves both ends up, `)` moves both down, and `*` lowers `lo` and raises `hi` (it could be either, or nothing). Two rules keep the range honest:

- If `hi < 0`, there are too many `)` even with every `*` read as `(`: **false**.
- If `lo < 0`, clamp it to 0. A negative count isn’t a real option; it means some `*` must be read differently.

At the end, a count of 0 must be possible: `lo == 0`. This is greedy in the gentlest sense: not one guess, but a summary of every guess, kept in two numbers. (Every step changes a count by −1, 0 or +1, so the possible counts can never develop a gap.)

<Figure caption="“(*))”. After each character the possible open counts are 1..1, 0..2, 0..1 (clamped from −1), 0..0 (clamped). hi never drops below 0 and lo ends at 0: valid, reading the * as (.">
  <ArrayStrip name="s" values={['(', '*', ')', ')']} />
  <ArrayStrip name="lo" values={[1, 0, 0, 0]} tones={{ 3: 'done' }} />
  <ArrayStrip name="hi" values={[1, 2, 1, 0]} />
</Figure>

<HintLadder hints={hints.wildcard}>

```java id=checkValidString
public boolean checkValidString(String s) {
    int lo = 0, hi = 0;                    // fewest and most '(' that could be open right now
    for (char ch : s.toCharArray()) {
        if (ch == '(') { lo++; hi++; }
        else if (ch == ')') { lo--; hi--; }
        else { lo--; hi++; }               // '*' as ')' lowers, as '(' raises, as nothing stays
        if (hi < 0) return false;          // too many ')' even if every '*' opens
        if (lo < 0) lo = 0;                // a count below zero is not a real option
    }
    return lo == 0;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) extra space.**

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** forgetting the clamp. On `"(*)"`, `lo` goes 1, 0, −1 and the function returns false — but reading the `*` as nothing gives `"()"`.

**Transfer test:** a two-stack solution pushes the indices of `(` and `*`, matches each `)` against an open `(` first (else a `*`), then pairs leftover `(` with leftover `*`. Why must each leftover `(` be paired with a `*` **to its right**? (A `*` before an opener cannot close it.) [Solve Valid Parenthesis String](https://leetcode.com/problems/valid-parenthesis-string/).

<h2 id="merge-triplets">Side quest 6 · Three dyes, one colour</h2>

**The tile says:** the dyers have colour recipes as triples. Merging two triples replaces one with their position-by-position maximum. Can some triple become exactly the target?

**The reasoning:** maxima never go down. So **a triple with any value above the target in the same position can never be used** — it would overshoot forever. Every other triple is **safe**: merging it never pushes anything past the target. So merge all the safe ones, and check that each of the three positions is matched exactly by at least one of them. Filter, then combine: no order matters, because max doesn’t care about order.

<Reveal q="Triples (2, 9, 5) and (1, 7, 1), target (2, 7, 5). Yes or no?">

(2, 9, 5) has 9 > 7 in the middle: unsafe, never used. The only safe triple is (1, 7, 1), which matches the middle but not 2 or 5. **No.** A version that skips the filter sees a 2, a 7 and a 5 somewhere and wrongly says yes.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.triplets}>

```java id=mergeTriplets
public boolean mergeTriplets(int[][] triplets, int[] target) {
    boolean[] hit = new boolean[3];
    for (int[] t : triplets) {
        if (t[0] > target[0] || t[1] > target[1] || t[2] > target[2]) continue;   // overshoots forever
        for (int k = 0; k < 3; k++) if (t[k] == target[k]) hit[k] = true;      // safe: merge it in
    }
    return hit[0] && hit[1] && hit[2];
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) extra space.**

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** if merging took the position-by-position **minimum**, what changes? (Mirror everything: discard triples with any value below the target, then look for exact matches.) [Solve Merge Triplets to Form Target Triplet](https://leetcode.com/problems/merge-triplets-to-form-target-triplet/).

<h2 id="choose-the-tool">When the river lies</h2>

<Scene title="The ferry toll" mood="storm">

The ferry on the lower river charges 6 coppers, and its toll-keeper gives change in coins of 1, 3 and 4. She always hands over the biggest coin that fits — 4, then 1, then 1 — and she is proud of it. Three coins.

Nix doesn’t argue. He lays two 3s on the counter.

The toll-keeper stares at them for a long time. *"But I took the biggest,"* she says.

*"You did,"* says the Keeper. *"And the biggest coin left you with the worst remainder. When the best-looking choice can spoil what comes after, you can’t choose — you have to remember every choice."*

</Scene>

Greedy is the wrong tool whenever the **greedy-choice property fails**: no best answer agrees with the local rule. Nix’s hunt usually finds out in one tiny input.

- **Coin change with arbitrary coins.** For coins {1, 3, 4} and amount 6, largest-first uses 3 coins (4 + 1 + 1); the best is 2 (3 + 3). No best answer for 6 even uses a 4, so there is no exchange to make. Real currencies like {1, 5, 10, 25} are designed so largest-first works; arbitrary coin sets are not.
- **0/1 knapsack.** Capacity 50, items (weight 10, value 60), (20, 100), (30, 120). Best value per kilo first takes 60 and 100, then 120 no longer fits: **160**. Taking the 20 and the 30 gives **220**. Whole items leave empty space the ratio argument can’t account for. (With fractions allowed — fractional knapsack — the ratio rule *is* optimal: there is never empty space.)
- **Weighted interval scheduling.** Earliest-end-first is optimal when every booking is worth the same; give them weights and it fails. See <a href={`${BASE}dsa/lessons/intervals/#choose-the-tool`}>the Intervals book</a>.

The fix in all three is the same: **try every choice, and remember the answer for each smaller problem.** That is dynamic programming; the monastery staircase of <a href={`${BASE}dsa/lessons/dp-1d/`}>1-D Dynamic Programming</a> is where it lives.

<HintLadder hints={hints.coins} labels={['Hunt first', 'What to remember', 'The recurrence', 'Compare with the Java solution']}>

```java id=coinChange
public int coinChange(int[] coins, int amount) {
    int[] best = new int[amount + 1];                  // best[a] = fewest coins for amount a
    Arrays.fill(best, Integer.MAX_VALUE);
    best[0] = 0;
    for (int a = 1; a <= amount; a++)
        for (int c : coins)
            if (c <= a && best[a - c] != Integer.MAX_VALUE)
                best[a] = Math.min(best[a], best[a - c] + 1);   // try EVERY last coin
    return best[amount] == Integer.MAX_VALUE ? -1 : best[amount];
}
```

**O(amount × coins) time, O(amount) space.** On {1, 3, 4} and 6, `best` is 0, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 2: the last coin is a 3, after `best[3] = 1`.

</HintLadder>

<Reveal q="How would I know, in an interview, whether largest-first is safe for a given coin set?">

You usually can’t prove it on the spot, and the interviewer rarely wants you to. If the coins are part of the input, assume they are arbitrary and use DP. If they are fixed and familiar (1, 5, 10, 25), say that this particular set is “canonical” — largest-first is known to be optimal for it — and that arbitrary sets are not. The word to look up is *canonical coin systems*.

</Reveal>

<Scene title="No stones are marked" mood="dawn">

On the morning of the exam the Keeper hands you a slate with three problems and no chapter names. Nix sits beside her with his stick, ready to scratch tiny rivers in the mud.

*"Pick one,"* she says. *"Before you write a line, give me your rule, Nix’s three stones, the name of your proof, and the time and space it costs."*

Choose one. Keep the tutorial closed. Write down the rule, three counterexample attempts, the proof’s name, the invariant, and the complexity **before** coding.

1. **The film editor.** Clips `[start, end]` of a match recording are given, possibly overlapping. Use the fewest clips to cover `[0, T]`, or return −1. Test: a gap in the middle, one clip that covers everything, clips that only touch at their ends.
2. **Change at the lemonade stand.** Customers pay in order with a 5, 10 or 20; a lemonade costs 5 and you start with no change. Can you give everyone correct change? Test: the first customer pays 10; a 20 when you hold one 10 and one 5; a 20 when you hold three 5s.
3. **Flip k signs.** An array of integers and a number `k`: flip the sign of exactly `k` elements (the same element may be flipped more than once) to make the sum as large as possible. Test: no negatives and odd `k`; more negatives than `k`; a zero in the array.

<Reveal q="I’ve written and tested it. What should my reasoning contain?">

**The film editor** is Chapter 5 in disguise. Sort the clips by start. The clips that start at or before your current covered point are “the current window”; scanning them, track the farthest end — that is where the next window ends. Count a clip each time you must move past the current end; return −1 if the farthest end doesn’t grow. Proof: stays ahead, exactly as in Jump Game II. O(n log n).

**Lemonade change** is an exchange argument. For a 20, give a 10 + 5 when you can, rather than 5 + 5 + 5: a 10 can only ever help with a 20, while a 5 helps with both 10s and 20s, so keeping 5s never hurts. Two counters, O(n).

**Flip k signs** is a forced move, then a cleanup. Flipping the most negative value gains the most, so sort and flip negatives from smallest upward while `k` lasts. If `k` is still odd afterwards, flip the element with the smallest absolute value once (a zero absorbs any leftover for free). O(n log n).

Now explain why a slightly wrong version fails one of your tests — forgetting the gap check, paying a 20 with three 5s first, flipping the wrong element at the end. That counterexample is stronger evidence of understanding than a memorised rule.

</Reveal>

Passing this once is a useful checkpoint. Rebuild one of these on another day, with a changed input and without the headings choosing the pattern for you.

<h2 id="cheat-sheet">The Keeper’s crossing notes</h2>

| When you see… | Start with… | The proof that makes it safe |
|---|---|---|
| Largest sum of a contiguous stretch | `run = max(x, run + x)`, `best = max(best, run)`, both from `nums[0]` | Exchange: a negative prefix only lowers what follows |
| Largest product of a contiguous stretch | Carry `hi` and `lo`; candidates `x`, `hi·x`, `lo·x` from the old values | Keep every survivor: extremes stay extreme under multiplication |
| Start a circular trip with an empty tank | `total` decides whether; restart after each deficit decides where | Lowest point of the running total; failed stretches can’t contain a start |
| Can you reach the end? | `far = max(far, i + nums[i])`; fail when `i > far` | Stays ahead: reachable stones form one block `0..far` |
| Fewest jumps to the end | Windows: scan, update `far`, jump when `i == end`; loop to n − 2 | Stays ahead: no k-jump path passes the k-th window’s end |
| Most pieces, each letter in one piece | `last[c]`; `end = max(end, last[c])`; cut when `i == end` | Every legal cut is taken the moment it appears |
| Groups of consecutive cards | Sorted counts; the smallest card starts a group | Forced move: the smallest card has one possible group |
| Unlimited trades, one share at a time | Sum of positive day-to-day changes | Exchange: any trade splits into its rising steps |
| Match items to needs, maximise matches | Sort both; smallest item that fits the smallest need | Exchange: swap rolls, nobody loses |
| Pairs under a weight limit | Sort; heaviest leaves, with the lightest if they fit | Exchange: swap partners, both boats still fit |
| Neighbour rules in a line | Left pass, right pass, keep the max | Each pass is a lower bound; the max meets both |
| Wildcards that may be (, ) or nothing | Range `lo..hi` of open counts; clamp `lo`; fail on `hi < 0` | The range is exactly the set of possible counts |
| Build a target from position-wise maxima | Discard triples that overshoot; check each position is hit | Maxima never go down; safe triples never hurt |
| Arbitrary coins, 0/1 items, weights | **Not greedy:** DP over amounts or capacities | The hunt finds a counterexample |

**Greedy does not mean O(1) space or no sorting.** Kadane, the jump games and Gas Station run in O(n) with O(1) extra space; the sorting side quests cost O(n log n); Hand of Straights needs an O(n) map; Candy needs an O(n) array. Count what you build.

## Five bugs the Keeper keeps in her pocket

1. **The empty stretch.** Starting Kadane’s `run` and `best` at 0 returns 0 on an all-negative array. Start at `nums[0]`.
2. **Overwriting before reading.** In Maximum Product, `lo` must use the *old* `hi`. Compute `hi × x` and `lo × x` first.
3. **Half a proof.** Gas Station without the total check returns a start for a ring that can’t be finished.
4. **Jumping from the last stone.** Jump Game II’s loop runs to `n − 2`; looping to `n − 1` counts an extra jump.
5. **Trusting a rule nobody tried to break.** Largest-coin-first, best-ratio-first, longest-leap-first. Three tiny inputs before any code.
