<h2 id="prologue">Prologue · The rope you must never drop</h2>

<Scene chapter="Prologue" title="Six islands in the fog" mood="dawn">

The Tethered Isles appear on no map that shows them all at once. Each island keeps a single rope, tied to exactly one other island, and its ferry runs along that rope in one direction only. The harbour office records one fact: where the first island is.

On your first morning, the Keeper walks you to the end of the pier and points into the fog. *"Six islands are out there,"* she says. *"Good islands. Nobody can reach them any more. An apprentice untied a rope to re-tie it somewhere better, and forgot to hold the other end."*

She hands you a coil of rope. Three lines are carved into the pier post: **What is settled? Why is this move safe? When do I stop?**

*"In these isles,"* she adds, *"safe means one thing. Before you touch a rope, someone must be holding every island you still need."*

</Scene>

A **linked list** is a chain of nodes. Each node holds a value and one reference, `next`, to another node — or `null`, meaning the chain ends there. You never see the whole chain. You hold references, usually just `head`, and you reach everything else by following `next`.

The isles map onto this exactly. An island is a node. Its rope is `next`. The harbour's record is `head`. And the fog is real: in Java, a node that no variable and no other node refers to is unreachable, and the garbage collector will reclaim it. **Re-tying a `next` pointer is the only dangerous move in this whole book**, because it can cut off everything that rope used to lead to.

```java fragment
// The LeetCode shape used throughout. One value, one rope.
class ListNode {
    int val;
    ListNode next;
    ListNode(int val) { this.val = val; }
    ListNode(int val, ListNode next) { this.val = val; this.next = next; }
}
```

The Keeper's three questions, for chains:

1. **What is settled?** The reversed prefix, the merged output so far, the islands the slow courier has passed.
2. **Why is this move safe?** Before re-tying any `next`, name the variable that still holds the island it points to now.
3. **When do I stop?** At `null`, at a meeting, or when a gap reaches the end.

**When this pattern fires.** The input is a linked list and the question involves **cycles, middles, ends, reversals, splicing, or reordering**. It also fires when an array secretly behaves like a list (values that are valid indexes, as in Find the Duplicate), and when you need O(1) lookups *plus* an order (a cache). Arrays index; lists *point* — so every technique here moves pointers cleverly instead of counting.

The brute force it replaces: copy every node into an `ArrayList`, index into it, and rebuild. That is O(n) extra memory and usually a second pass. The techniques in this book do the same jobs in **one pass with O(1) extra memory**, and interviewers ask for exactly that.

**Two tools you will use in almost every problem:**

- **The dummy head.** `ListNode dummy = new ListNode(0); dummy.next = head;` It makes "delete the head" and "insert before the head" stop being special cases: the head becomes an ordinary island that somebody's rope points at. Return `dummy.next` at the end. When in doubt, dummy.
- **Draw it.** Four boxes and arrows on paper before you code. Pointer bugs are invisible in code and obvious in a picture. You have called this your decomposition-under-pressure rep in miniature, and it is: one picture, then one line per arrow you change.

<h2 id="find-the-middle">Chapter 1 · The halfway lantern</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 1" title="The halfway lantern" mood="dawn">

The night ferries keep losing their way, and the Guild decides to hang a lantern on the middle island of the chain. Nobody knows how long the chain is. Counting means sailing to the end and all the way back, and there is only time for one trip.

Two couriers volunteer. Slow is a tortoise, steady and unhurried: one ferry per turn. Fast is a hare: two ferries per turn, every turn.

*"Leave together,"* says the Keeper. *"Stop the moment the hare runs out of ferries. Then tell me where the tortoise is standing."*

</Scene>

**Problem: Middle of the Linked List · Fast & slow**

Fast covers two ferries for each one of Slow's. So **when Fast has covered the whole chain, Slow has covered half of it**. No counting, no second trip, two references of memory.

<Figure caption="Where the couriers stand when the loop stops. Five islands: Fast rests on 5 after 4 hops and Slow on 3 after 2 — the exact middle. Six islands: Fast falls off to null after 6 hops and Slow stands on 4 after 3 — the second of the two middle islands.">
  <ArrayStrip name="odd" values={[1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 'null']} join="→" indices={false} pointers={[{ at: 2, label: 'SLOW', tone: 'green' }, { at: 4, label: 'FAST' }]} tones={{ 2: 'done', 5: 'ghost' }} />
  <ArrayStrip name="even" values={[1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 'null']} join="→" indices={false} pointers={[{ at: 3, label: 'SLOW', tone: 'green' }, { at: 6, label: 'FAST' }]} tones={{ 3: 'done', 6: 'ghost' }} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="Where is the tortoise?" steps={middle} takeaway="Remember: Fast has always made exactly twice Slow's hops. Both on head gives the second middle; fast = head.next gives the first." />

**Why it works:** after t turns, Slow has made t hops and Fast has made 2t. The loop stops when Fast cannot make two more hops. On an odd chain of 2m + 1 islands, Fast lands on the last island after m turns, so Slow is on island m (counting from 0): the centre. On an even chain of 2m islands, Fast reaches `null` after m turns, so Slow is on island m: the second of the two centre islands.

**What stays true:** Fast has made exactly twice as many hops as Slow, and every island Slow has passed lies in the first half.

<Reveal q="What if the problem wants the FIRST of two middle islands?">

Give Fast a one-island head start: `fast = head.next`. On even chains it runs out one turn earlier, and Slow stops on the first middle (3 in 1 → … → 6). On odd chains nothing changes.

An equivalent spelling keeps both on `head` and tightens the guard to `fast.next != null && fast.next.next != null`. You will meet that guard again in Reorder List, where it marks the end of the first half.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.middle}>

```java id=middleNode
public ListNode middleNode(ListNode head) {
    ListNode slow = head, fast = head;
    while (fast != null && fast.next != null) {
        slow = slow.next;
        fast = fast.next.next;
    }
    return slow; // even length: the SECOND middle
}

public ListNode firstMiddle(ListNode head) {
    if (head == null) return null;
    ListNode slow = head, fast = head.next; // one-island head start
    while (fast != null && fast.next != null) {
        slow = slow.next;
        fast = fast.next.next;
    }
    return slow; // even length: the FIRST middle
}
```

Fast visits about n islands and Slow about n / 2: **O(n) time, O(1) extra space**.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** the guard's order matters. `while (fast.next != null && fast != null)` reads `fast.next` before checking `fast`, so it throws a NullPointerException as soon as Fast falls off an even chain. Check `fast != null` first; Java's `&&` stops at the first false. [Solve Middle of the Linked List](https://leetcode.com/problems/middle-of-the-linked-list/).

<h2 id="detect-a-cycle">Chapter 2 · The ferry that never ends</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 2" title="The ferry that never ends" mood="night">

An old ferryman comes to the Guild hall, soaked and furious. He has been sailing his chain for three days and has not reached the end. He swears that somewhere out there, the last island's rope leads back to an island he has already visited.

You could mark every island you pass with chalk — but the islanders hate chalk, and the Guild's rules forbid writing anything down. You have two couriers and nothing else.

*"On a chain with an end,"* the Keeper says, *"the hare finds the end. What does the hare find on a chain without one?"*

</Scene>

**Problem: Linked List Cycle · Fast & slow, comparing nodes**

If the chain ends, Fast reaches `null` first: it is always ahead. If the chain loops, Fast never reaches `null`; it circles, and eventually Slow enters the loop too. From then on, **Fast gains exactly one island on Slow every turn**, so it must land on Slow's island.

<Figure caption="3 → 2 → 0 → −4, and the last rope leads back to 2. Race: Slow visits 2, 0, −4; Fast visits 0, 2, −4. After three turns both stand on −4: the same node, so there is a loop.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    A(("3")) --> B(("2")) --> C(("0")) --> D(("-4"))
    D -->|"loops back"| B`} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="Same island, or same flag?" steps={cycle} takeaway="Remember: move first, then compare the nodes themselves. A meeting proves a loop; Fast reaching null proves an end." />

**Why it works:** with no loop, Fast hits `null` and you return false. With a loop of length L, look at the moment Slow enters it. Measure how many ferries Fast is *behind* Slow along the loop: some d with 0 ≤ d &lt; L. Each turn Slow moves one and Fast two, so d drops by exactly 1 (counting round the loop). It cannot skip from 1 to −1, so it reaches 0 within at most L turns: Slow never needs more than one lap.

**What stays true:** Fast has made twice Slow's hops. If Fast found `null`, the chain has an end.

<Reveal q="Why compare slow == fast instead of slow.val == fast.val?">

Because two islands can fly the same flag. In `1 → 2 → 2 → 3 → null`, after one turn Slow is on the first 2 and Fast on the second 2. Equal values, different nodes, and no loop at all. `==` on references asks "is this the same node?", which is the only question that proves anything.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="How long can the race take?">

Slow needs a hops to reach the loop (a = the length of the tail before it), then at most L more turns until Fast catches it. a + L is at most n, so the whole race is **O(n) time**, with **O(1) extra space**. A `HashSet` of visited nodes also works, at O(n) extra space — say it first in an interview, then offer the couriers.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.cycle}>

```java id=hasCycle
public boolean hasCycle(ListNode head) {
    ListNode slow = head, fast = head;
    while (fast != null && fast.next != null) {
        slow = slow.next;
        fast = fast.next.next;
        if (slow == fast) return true; // compare NODES (==), never values
    }
    return false; // fast found the end
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) extra space.** The empty chain and a single island are both handled by the guard.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** comparing *before* moving.

```java fragment
ListNode slow = head, fast = head;
while (fast != null && fast.next != null) {
    if (slow == fast) return true; // true on the very first check, for every chain
    slow = slow.next;
    fast = fast.next.next;
}
```

Both couriers start on the head, so this reports a loop in `1 → 2 → null`. Move first, then compare. [Solve Linked List Cycle](https://leetcode.com/problems/linked-list-cycle/).

<h2 id="nth-from-end">Chapter 3 · Two pilots, one gap</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 3" title="Two pilots, one gap" mood="storm">

A storm warning arrives at noon: the island second from the end of the eastern chain is sinking, and its rope must be cut out before it drags the others down. Nobody knows how long the eastern chain is. There is time for one trip.

Two pilots, Lead and Trail, take their boats out together from the buoy before the first island. *"Lead goes ahead alone,"* says the Keeper, *"and then you sail in step, never closer, never further apart. When Lead runs out of chain, where will Trail be?"*

Trail does the arithmetic and frowns. "Next to the sinking island," she says. "Which side of it?"

</Scene>

**Problem: Remove Nth Node From End of List · A fixed gap + a dummy**

"The nth from the end" is a distance from `null`. If Lead is k ferries ahead of Trail, then when Lead reaches `null`, Trail is k ferries from `null`. You don't want to stand *on* the target — **a rope is cut from the island before it**. So choose a gap of **n + 1**.

<Figure caption="Remove the 2nd from the end of 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5. Lead opened a gap of n + 1 = 3 ferries, then both sailed in step. Lead is on null, so Trail is on 3, right before the target 4: trail.next = trail.next.next cuts it out.">
  <ArrayStrip values={['D', 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 'null']} join="→" indices={false} pointers={[{ at: 3, label: 'TRAIL', tone: 'blue' }, { at: 6, label: 'LEAD' }]} tones={{ 0: 'ghost', 4: 'hot', 6: 'ghost' }} regions={[{ from: 3, to: 6, label: 'gap: n + 1 = 3 ferries', tone: 'gold' }]} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="Which side of the sinking island?" steps={nth} takeaway="Remember: start both on a dummy, open a gap of n + 1, walk until Lead is null, then re-tie Trail's rope past its next island." />

**Why it works:** number the dummy 0, the islands 1 to len, and `null` len + 1. Lead ends at len + 1, so Trail ends at len + 1 − (n + 1) = len − n. The nth island from the end is at position len − n + 1: the one right after Trail.

**What stays true:** once the gap is open, Lead is exactly n + 1 ferries ahead of Trail.

**Why the dummy:** when n equals the length, the target is the head itself, and the island "before" it is the dummy. Trail never leaves it, the cut re-ties `dummy.next`, and returning `dummy.next` gives the right answer — possibly the empty chain — with no special case.

<Reveal q="Why not count the length first, then walk to position len − n?">

That is correct, also O(n) time, and a fine first answer. It takes two passes. The gap does the same job in one, which is the follow-up the problem itself asks for. Both use O(1) extra space.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.nth}>

```java id=removeNthFromEnd
public ListNode removeNthFromEnd(ListNode head, int n) {
    ListNode dummy = new ListNode(0, head);
    ListNode lead = dummy, trail = dummy;
    for (int i = 0; i <= n; i++) lead = lead.next; // open a gap of n + 1
    while (lead != null) {
        lead = lead.next;
        trail = trail.next;
    }
    trail.next = trail.next.next; // trail stands just before the target
    return dummy.next;
}
```

One pass: **O(n) time, O(1) extra space.** LeetCode guarantees 1 ≤ n ≤ length, so Lead never steps past `null` while opening the gap.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** a gap of n instead of n + 1 lands Trail *on* the target. On `1 → 2` with n = 1, Trail finishes on 2, the last island, and `trail.next.next` throws a NullPointerException — with n = 1 that happens on every chain. With a larger n it is quieter and worse: on `1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5` with n = 2, Trail stands on 4 and the cut removes 5, the island after the target. [Solve Remove Nth Node From End of List](https://leetcode.com/problems/remove-nth-node-from-end-of-list/).

<h2 id="find-the-duplicate">Chapter 4 · The pigeonhole census</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 4" title="The pigeonhole census" mood="dusk">

The Guild archive has a cabinet of n + 1 pigeonholes, numbered 0 to n. In each one is a card naming a pigeonhole between 1 and n. The archivist is certain that some number is written on two cards, and wants to know which.

The rules are strict. Don't move a card. Don't write anything down. You may remember a couple of numbers in your head.

You stare at the cabinet for a long time. The Keeper leans over and taps a card. *"Stop reading it as a number,"* she says. *"Read it as a ferry."*

</Scene>

**Problem: Find the Duplicate Number · A hidden linked list + Floyd's two phases**

Make pigeonhole i an island whose ferry goes to island `nums[i]`. Every island has exactly one ferry out, and every value 1..n is a real island, so **no ferry leads off the map**. Start at island 0 and keep sailing: with only n + 1 islands, you must eventually revisit one. The walk ends in a loop.

Two facts make that loop useful. **No card says 0**, so no ferry arrives at island 0: it sits on a tail before the loop. And the loop's **entrance** has two ferries arriving: one from the tail, one from the island before it inside the loop. Two cards naming the same island — that island's number is the duplicate.

**Phase 1 is Chapter 2's race**: Slow does `slow = nums[slow]`, Fast does `fast = nums[nums[fast]]`, until they stand on the same island. That meeting is *somewhere* in the loop — not necessarily at the entrance. **Phase 2** sends one courier back to island 0, and both take one ferry per turn. They meet exactly at the entrance. Here is why.

**The distance argument, one line at a time:**

1. At the meeting, Slow has sailed some number of ferries, s, and Fast has sailed **2s**. They stand on the same island, so Fast's extra s ferries were whole laps: **s is a multiple of L**.
2. Slow's s ferries were the a ferries of the tail, then b ferries past E, plus possibly some whole laps: s = a + b + (whole laps). Since s is a whole number of laps, **so is a + b: a + b = kL** for some whole number k.
3. Rearrange: **a = kL − b = (k − 1)·L + (L − b)**.
4. Read it aloud. A courier at M that sails a ferries goes round (k − 1) whole laps and then L − b more — which is exactly the way from M back to E. A courier at the start that sails a ferries also reaches E. **Same number of ferries, same island.**
5. They cannot meet earlier: until step a, the restarted courier is still on the tail, and no tail island is part of the loop.

Check it on the figure's numbers, `[1, 3, 4, 2, 2]`: the tail 0 → 1 → 3 → 2 gives a = 3; the loop 2 → 4 → 2 gives L = 2; the couriers met on 4 after Slow sailed s = 4 ferries, so b = 1. Then a + b = 4 = 2 · L, so k = 2, and (k − 1)·L + (L − b) = 2 + 1 = 3 = a.

You have named this fear before: **Recognition Fear**, freezing on a problem whose real structure is hidden. Here the constraints do the recognising for you. Values 1..n in n + 1 slots, read-only, O(1) space: that rules out sorting and hashing and points straight at Floyd's. Recognising "array as hidden linked list" cold is a win waiting to happen.

<Reveal q="Why must the walk start at index 0, not at index 1 or n?">

Because 0 is the one island no ferry arrives at, so it is guaranteed to be on the tail (a ≥ 1), and the entrance then has two distinct arrows in. Start on an island that is already inside the loop and a = 0: phase 2 returns your starting island, duplicate or not. On `[1, 3, 4, 2, 2]`, starting at index 4 returns 4 — which appears only once.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.duplicate}>

```java id=findDuplicate
public int findDuplicate(int[] nums) {
    int slow = 0, fast = 0;
    do {                                  // phase 1: race to a meeting
        slow = nums[slow];
        fast = nums[nums[fast]];
    } while (slow != fast);
    slow = 0;                             // phase 2: restart one courier
    while (slow != fast) {
        slow = nums[slow];
        fast = nums[fast];
    }
    return slow;                          // the loop's entrance = the duplicate
}
```

The `do … while` moves before comparing, just like Chapter 2. **O(n) time, O(1) extra space**, and the array is never modified. The duplicate may appear more than twice; the entrance still has at least two arrows in.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** restarting phase 2 at `nums[0]` instead of 0. On `[1, 3, 4, 2, 2]` the two couriers then chase each other round the 2–4 loop forever, always one ferry apart. Returning the phase 1 meeting point (4) is the other classic. [Solve Find the Duplicate Number](https://leetcode.com/problems/find-the-duplicate-number/).

<h2 id="reverse-the-chain">Chapter 5 · Turning the ferries</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 5" title="Turning the ferries" mood="dawn">

The tide is turning for the season, and by sunset every ferry in the western chain must run the other way. The last island must become the first.

**Problem: Reverse Linked List · In-place reversal, the four-line dance**

Walk the chain once. At each island, re-tie its rope to point at the island behind it. The trouble is that the rope you are re-tying is your only way to the islands ahead. So **save the rope before you cut it**.

<Figure caption="Mid-reversal, safe. 1 → null is finished and held by prev. Cur stands on 2, and next already holds the rope to 3 → 4 → 5. Now 2 can be re-tied to point at 1.">

  <ArrayStrip name="cur" values={[2, 3, 4, 5, 'null']} join="→" indices={false} pointers={[{ at: 0, label: 'CUR' }, { at: 1, label: 'NEXT (saved)', tone: 'blue' }]} tones={{ 0: 'hot', 1: 'blue', 4: 'ghost' }} />

  <ArrayStrip name="the fog" values={[3, 4, 5, 'null']} join="→" indices={false} tones={{ 0: 'out', 1: 'out', 2: 'out', 3: 'out' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 3, label: 'nobody holds these', tone: 'red' }]} />

<Trace title="Save the rope before you cut it" steps={reverse} takeaway="Remember: save next, flip, advance prev, advance cur. Return prev. An empty chain needs no special case." />

This is **the** fundamental of the whole book, and it needs to live in your fingers, not your notes. The four lines run in that exact order. Line 1 before line 2: grab the other side of the rope, then cut. Return `prev`, not `cur` — cur has walked off the end.

**What stays true:** at the top of every loop, `prev` is the head of the reversed part (the islands already turned, in reverse order), `cur` is the head of the untouched part, and the two parts together hold every island. Each turn moves exactly one island from the front of the untouched part to the front of the reversed part. When `cur` is `null`, the untouched part is empty and `prev` is the whole reversed chain.

<HintLadder hints={hints.reverse}>

```java id=reverseList
public ListNode reverseList(ListNode head) {
    ListNode prev = null, cur = head;
    while (cur != null) {
        ListNode next = cur.next;   // 1. save the rope before cutting it
        cur.next = prev;            // 2. flip the arrow
        prev = cur;                 // 3. advance prev
        cur = next;                 // 4. advance cur
    }
    return prev;                    // prev is the new head (cur is null!)
}
```

Each island is re-tied exactly once: **O(n) time, O(1) extra space.**

</HintLadder>

<Reveal q="Can you reverse it recursively? What does that cost?">

Yes. Reverse everything after the head, then hang the head on the end.

```java id=reverseRecursive
public ListNode reverseRecursive(ListNode head) {
    if (head == null || head.next == null) return head;
    ListNode newHead = reverseRecursive(head.next);
    head.next.next = head; // the island after me now points back at me
    head.next = null;      // and I point at nothing, for now
    return newHead;
}
```

The trick line is `head.next.next = head`: after the recursive call, `head.next` is the *last* island of the reversed rest, and you tie it back to `head`. It is still O(n) time, but the call stack holds n frames: **O(n) space**. On a very long chain, Java can throw a StackOverflowError. Interviewers often ask for both versions; lead with the loop.

</Reveal>

**Catch the bug:** flip before save, and the rest of the chain is gone; return `cur` and you return `null`; return `head` and you return the old first island, which is now the last: a one-node chain.

```java fragment
while (cur != null) {
    cur.next = prev;          // the rope to the rest is gone...
    ListNode next = cur.next; // ...so this "saves" prev instead
    prev = cur;
    cur = next;               // cur walks backwards
}
```

<h2 id="reverse-a-stretch">Chapter 6 · Only the middle reach</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 6" title="Only the middle reach">

**Problem: Reverse Linked List II · The four-line dance, plus two seams**

Four moves: put a dummy in front; walk to `before`, the island just before position `left`; remember `first = before.next`, which will become the stretch's *last* island; run the four-line dance exactly `right − left + 1` times. Then **re-tie both seams**: `before.next = prev` (into the new front of the stretch) and `first.next = cur` (out to the island after `right`).

<Figure caption="Reverse positions 2 through 4. Before: before stands on 1, first on 2. After: seam 1 ties 1 to 4, the stretch's new front; seam 2 ties 2, its old front and new end, to 5.">
  <ArrayStrip name="before" values={['D', 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 'null']} join="→" indices={false} pointers={[{ at: 1, label: 'BEFORE', tone: 'purple' }, { at: 2, label: 'FIRST' }]} tones={{ 0: 'ghost', 2: 'hot', 3: 'hot', 4: 'hot', 6: 'ghost' }} regions={[{ from: 2, to: 4, label: 'flip this stretch', tone: 'gold' }]} />

<Trace title="Mind the seams" steps={reverseII} takeaway="Remember: before stands at left − 1 (the dummy when left = 1). After the flips, before.next = prev and first.next = cur." />

**What stays true:** during the flips, `before` still points at `first`, the stretch's flipped part is held by `prev`, and everything after it is held by `cur`. Nothing is lost; it is just disconnected until the seams are re-tied. After exactly right − left + 1 flips, `prev` is the island at position `right` and `cur` is the island after it (possibly `null`).

The seams are where all the bugs live. Draw them before you code them.

<Reveal q="Why does first end up as the stretch's last island — and what does it point at before seam 2?">

The dance turns the stretch around, so its old front ends up at the back. On the first flip, `prev` was `null`, so `first.next` was set to `null`. Until seam 2 re-ties it to `cur`, the stretch ends in `null` and the rest of the chain is held only by `cur`.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.reverseII}>

```java id=reverseBetween
public ListNode reverseBetween(ListNode head, int left, int right) {
    ListNode dummy = new ListNode(0, head);
    ListNode before = dummy;
    for (int i = 1; i < left; i++) before = before.next; // stand at left − 1
    ListNode first = before.next;          // becomes the stretch's last island
    ListNode prev = null, cur = first;
    for (int i = left; i <= right; i++) {  // the four-line dance, right − left + 1 times
        ListNode next = cur.next;
        cur.next = prev;
        prev = cur;
        cur = next;
    }
    before.next = prev;  // seam 1: into the new front of the stretch
    first.next = cur;    // seam 2: out to the island after right
    return dummy.next;
}
```

One walk to `left`, one pass over the stretch: **O(n) time, O(1) extra space.** Positions are 1-based, with 1 ≤ left ≤ right ≤ n.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** seam amnesia. Forget `first.next = cur` and you get `1 → 4 → 3 → 2 → null`: island 5 is dropped. Return `head` instead of `dummy.next` when `left = 1` and you return the old first island, now in the middle. [Solve Reverse Linked List II](https://leetcode.com/problems/reverse-linked-list-ii/).

<Scene chapter="Chapter 7" title="Convoys of k" mood="night">

**Problem: Reverse Nodes in k-Group · Stretch reversal in a loop (Hard)**

Otherwise reverse the group exactly as in Chapter 6 (remember `first` and `after = probe.next`, flip until `cur == after`), re-tie both seams, and **move `before` to `first`** — the group's old front is now its last island, right in front of the next group.

**What stays true:** everything up to and including `before` is finished — groups reversed and correctly linked — and `before.next` is the first island not yet processed.

<HintLadder hints={hints.kGroup}>

```java id=reverseKGroup
public ListNode reverseKGroup(ListNode head, int k) {
    ListNode dummy = new ListNode(0, head);
    ListNode before = dummy;
    while (true) {
        ListNode probe = before;
        for (int i = 0; i < k && probe != null; i++) probe = probe.next;
        if (probe == null) break;              // fewer than k remain: leave them
        ListNode first = before.next, after = probe.next;
        ListNode prev = null, cur = first;
        while (cur != after) {                 // the four-line dance on this group
            ListNode next = cur.next;
            cur.next = prev;
            prev = cur;
            cur = next;
        }
        before.next = prev;   // seam 1 (prev is the old probe)
        first.next = after;   // seam 2
        before = first;       // stand in front of the next group
    }
    return dummy.next;
}
```

Every island is probed once and flipped at most once: **O(n) time, O(1) extra space.** With k = 1 every group is a single island and nothing changes.

</HintLadder>

<Reveal q="Recursion or iteration?">

A recursive version reverses the first group and ties its old front to `reverseKGroup(after, k)`. It reads nicely, but it holds one stack frame per group: O(n / k) space. The loop above is O(1). Either is accepted on LeetCode; know which one you wrote and say its space cost.

</Reveal>

**Catch the bug:** setting `before = prev` (the group's *new* front) instead of `first`. The next probe then counts from the wrong island, and the old first island is dragged into every later group: on `1 → 2 → 3 → 4` with k = 2 you get `2 → 3 → 4 → 1` instead of `2 → 1 → 4 → 3`. Also: flipping before counting reverses the short tail. [Solve Reverse Nodes in k-Group](https://leetcode.com/problems/reverse-nodes-in-k-group/).

**Problem: Merge Two Sorted Lists · Dummy + tail splice**

Dummy head plus tail pointer. Each turn, **splice the smaller front node after `tail`**, advance that list, and advance `tail`. When one list runs out, **attach the leftover list in one re-tie**: it is already linked and already sorted, so no leftover loop is needed.

**What stays true:** `dummy.next … tail` is sorted and contains exactly the nodes taken so far, and every node still waiting in either list is at least `tail.val`.

<Reveal q="Why <= rather than <?">

Both produce a sorted result. With `<=`, a tie takes list1's node first, so equal values keep their original relative order: the merge is **stable**. That matters when nodes carry more than a value, and it is what makes merge sort stable (side quest 6).

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.merge}>

```java id=mergeTwoLists
public ListNode mergeTwoLists(ListNode list1, ListNode list2) {
    ListNode dummy = new ListNode(0), tail = dummy;
    while (list1 != null && list2 != null) {
        if (list1.val <= list2.val) { tail.next = list1; list1 = list1.next; }
        else { tail.next = list2; list2 = list2.next; }
        tail = tail.next;
    }
    tail.next = (list1 != null) ? list1 : list2; // the leftover is already linked
    return dummy.next;
}
```

**O(m + n) time, O(1) extra space**: every node is re-tied, none is created. Either list may be empty.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** forgetting `tail = tail.next`. Tail stays on the dummy, every splice overwrites `dummy.next`, and the final re-tie leaves only the leftover: merging `1 → 2 → 4` with `1 → 3 → 4` returns just `4`. Returning `dummy` instead of `dummy.next` puts a fake 0 at the front. [Solve Merge Two Sorted Lists](https://leetcode.com/problems/merge-two-sorted-lists/).

<Scene chapter="Chapter 9" title="The festival weave" mood="dawn">

On festival morning the ferry must visit the islands in a strange order: the first, then the last, then the second, then the second-to-last, weaving inward until it meets itself in the middle.

No new ropes. No walking backwards — the ferries only run one way. You cannot even see the last island from the first.

The Keeper does not explain. She holds up three fingers. *"You already own three moves,"* she says. *"This chapter only asks you to chain them."*

</Scene>

**Problem: Reorder List · Middle + reverse + weave (composite)**

L0 → Ln → L1 → Ln−1 → … is the pattern showcase: **find the middle** (fast/slow) → **reverse the second half** → **weave** the two halves together. If you can narrate this cold, you own the pattern.

<Figure caption="Reorder 1 → … → 6. Slow stops on 3, the end of the first half. Cut there and reverse the second half into 6 → 5 → 4. Weave one from each: 1 → 6 → 2 → 5 → 3 → 4.">
  <ArrayStrip name="first half" values={[1, 2, 3, 'null']} join="→" indices={false} pointers={[{ at: 2, label: 'SLOW', tone: 'green' }]} tones={{ 3: 'ghost' }} />
  <ArrayStrip name="second, reversed" values={[6, 5, 4, 'null']} join="→" indices={false} tones={{ 0: 'blue', 1: 'blue', 2: 'blue', 3: 'ghost' }} />
  <ArrayStrip name="woven" values={[1, 6, 2, 5, 3, 4, 'null']} join="→" indices={false} tones={{ 1: 'blue', 3: 'blue', 5: 'blue', 6: 'ghost' }} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="Three moves, chained" steps={reorder} takeaway="Remember: middle, cut, reverse, weave. Save both ropes before every weave re-tie." />

**Why it works:** with the guard `fast.next != null && fast.next.next != null`, Slow stops on the last island of the first half, which has ⌈n / 2⌉ islands; the second half has ⌊n / 2⌋. After reversing, the second half's front is the original last island. The weave alternates one from each half. When the first half is longer by one (odd n), its last island is the true middle — it should come last, and after the cut it already points at `null`.

<Reveal q="Would Chapter 1's guard (fast != null && fast.next != null) work here too?">

Surprisingly, yes. On even lengths Slow then stops on the second middle, so the first half keeps two more islands than the second. Those two islands are consecutive in the answer anyway (try 1 → 2 → 3 → 4: either guard gives 1 → 4 → 2 → 3). This book uses the balanced guard because it is the habit that keeps working when the halves really must be balanced, as in Sort List (side quest 6).

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.reorder}>

```java id=reorderList
public void reorderList(ListNode head) {
    if (head == null || head.next == null) return;
    // 1. find the end of the first half
    ListNode slow = head, fast = head;
    while (fast.next != null && fast.next.next != null) {
        slow = slow.next;
        fast = fast.next.next;
    }
    // 2. cut, then reverse the second half
    ListNode second = slow.next, prev = null;
    slow.next = null;
    while (second != null) {
        ListNode next = second.next;
        second.next = prev;
        prev = second;
        second = next;
    }
    // 3. weave: save both ropes, then re-tie
    ListNode first = head;
    second = prev;
    while (second != null) {
        ListNode n1 = first.next, n2 = second.next;
        first.next = second;
        second.next = n1;
        first = n1;
        second = n2;
    }
}
```

Three linear passes: **O(n) time, O(1) extra space.** The method returns nothing; it re-ties the chain in place.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** forgetting the cut, `slow.next = null`. The first half still points into the second, and the weave ties an island to itself: on `1 → 2 → 3 → 4`, island 3 ends up pointing at 3, a one-island loop that hangs whoever prints the list. [Solve Reorder List](https://leetcode.com/problems/reorder-list/).

<h2 id="copy-random">Chapter 10 · The mirror isles</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 10" title="The mirror isles" mood="night">

The Guild is commissioning a twin archipelago: an exact copy of the eastern chain. But the eastern islands have a second rope each, a signal line that can lead to *any* island in the chain, or to none at all.

The twin must be fully independent. Not one rope — ferry or signal — may lead back into the old chain. When the inspectors cut the old chain loose, the twin must not notice.

You start building twins in order and tying signal lines as you go. On the very first island, the signal line points forward, to an island whose twin you haven't built yet. You stop, rope in hand.

</Scene>

**Problem: Copy List with Random Pointer · Clone with a map, or weave the clones in**

Two answers, and you should be able to give both. **The map**: old node → clone. Two passes, O(n) extra space; say it first. **The weave**: put each clone right after its original (`A → A' → B → B'`), so the clone of any node X is simply `X.next`; then `clone.random = orig.random.next`, then unweave. O(1) extra space beyond the clones themselves — the senior flex.

<Figure caption="Woven. Every clone sits right after its original, so the clone of X is X.next. A's signal line points at C, so A′'s must point at C.next, which is C′ — never at C itself.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    A["A"] --> A2["A′"] --> B["B"] --> B2["B′"] --> C["C"] --> C2["C′"]
    A -.->|"random"| C
    A2 -.->|"random = A.random.next"| C2`} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="A twin with no ropes home" steps={copyRandom} takeaway="Remember: a clone's pointers must land on clones. Map: create all clones, then wire. Weave: clone of X is X.next." />

On LeetCode the `Node` class (with `val`, `next`, `random`) is given to you. It is written out here as a nested class so the code is complete and tested.

**Why the map works:** pass 1 creates a clone for every original, so every target exists before any pointer is wired. Pass 2 wires both pointers through the map. `map.get(null)` is `null`, which handles the chain's end and missing random pointers for free. The map compares nodes by identity (`Node` does not override `equals`), so two nodes with the same value stay separate.

**Why the weave works:** while woven, the chain itself *is* the map: clone(X) = X.next. Unweaving re-ties every original to the original after it and every clone to the clone after it, restoring the input exactly.

<HintLadder hints={hints.copyRandom}>

```java group=copy
static class Node {
    int val;
    Node next, random;
    Node(int val) { this.val = val; }
}

// Answer 1 — the map. O(n) time, O(n) extra space.
public Node copyRandomList(Node head) {
    Map<Node, Node> clone = new HashMap<>();
    for (Node cur = head; cur != null; cur = cur.next) clone.put(cur, new Node(cur.val));
    for (Node cur = head; cur != null; cur = cur.next) {
        clone.get(cur).next = clone.get(cur.next);     // get(null) is null
        clone.get(cur).random = clone.get(cur.random);
    }
    return clone.get(head);
}
```

```java group=copy
// Answer 2 — the weave. O(n) time, O(1) extra space beyond the clones.
public Node copyRandomListWoven(Node head) {
    if (head == null) return null;
    for (Node cur = head; cur != null; cur = cur.next.next) {   // 1. weave A → A' → B → B'
        Node copy = new Node(cur.val);
        copy.next = cur.next;
        cur.next = copy;
    }
    for (Node cur = head; cur != null; cur = cur.next.next)     // 2. clone of X is X.next
        if (cur.random != null) cur.next.random = cur.random.next;
    Node copyHead = head.next;
    for (Node cur = head; cur != null; cur = cur.next) {        // 3. unweave, restoring the original
        Node copy = cur.next;
        cur.next = copy.next;
        copy.next = (copy.next != null) ? copy.next.next : null;
    }
    return copyHead;
}
```

Both are **O(n) time**. The map costs O(n) extra space; the weave costs O(1) beyond the n clones the answer needs anyway.

</HintLadder>

<Reveal q="Why must the weave restore the original chain?">

Because the caller still owns it. A deep copy that leaves the original tangled with clones is a broken original. LeetCode checks the input list too, and an interviewer will ask. Step 3 restores every original `next` as it peels the clones off.

</Reveal>

**Catch the bug:** `copy.random = orig.random` — a shallow copy whose signal lines lead home into the old chain. Its values print correctly, but LeetCode's checker rejects it, and any later change to the original leaks into the copy. [Solve Copy List with Random Pointer](https://leetcode.com/problems/copy-list-with-random-pointer/).

<h2 id="lru-cache">Chapter 11 · The crowded pier</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 11" title="The crowded pier" mood="storm">

The Guild's pier has berths for only a few boats. All day, captains shout for boats by name, or bring a new one in. When the pier is full and a new boat arrives, the boat nobody has called for the longest gets towed out to sea.

Every call must be answered instantly. Nobody has time to walk the length of the pier asking which boat is oldest, and nobody has time to search for a boat by name.

**Problem: LRU Cache · HashMap + doubly linked list**

The structural boss. A **HashMap** gives O(1) lookup from key to node. A **doubly linked list** keeps the nodes in recency order: most recent at the front, least recent at the back. `get` moves a node to the front; `put` either updates and moves it, or adds a new node at the front and, if over capacity, evicts the back. **Every operation updates the map and the list together.**

<Figure caption="Two sentinels, head and tail, mean every real node always has two real neighbours, so unlinking never needs a null check. The map points straight at nodes. Eviction removes tail.prev — from the list and, using the key stored in the node, from the map.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    H["head sentinel"] <--> N3["key 3 · most recent"] <--> N1["key 1"] <--> T["tail sentinel"]
    M[("HashMap: key → node")] -.-> N3
    M -.-> N1`} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="Who gets towed?" steps={lru} takeaway="Remember: get and put both refresh recency; eviction takes tail.prev and deletes its key from the map." />

<Reveal q="Why a doubly linked list, not a singly linked one?">

`get` must move an arbitrary node from the middle to the front in O(1). Unlinking a node means re-tying its **predecessor's** rope, and only a `prev` pointer gives you the predecessor instantly. With a singly linked list you would walk from the head to find it: O(n), and the whole contract breaks. This "why doubly" follow-up is asked nearly every time.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.lru}>

```java id=lru
class LRUCache {
    private class Node {
        int key, val;
        Node prev, next;
        Node(int key, int val) { this.key = key; this.val = val; }
    }
    private final int capacity;
    private final Map<Integer, Node> map = new HashMap<>();
    private final Node head = new Node(0, 0), tail = new Node(0, 0); // sentinels

    public LRUCache(int capacity) {
        this.capacity = capacity;
        head.next = tail;
        tail.prev = head;
    }

    public int get(int key) {
        Node node = map.get(key);
        if (node == null) return -1;
        unlink(node);
        addFront(node);          // used: now the most recent
        return node.val;
    }

    public void put(int key, int value) {
        Node node = map.get(key);
        if (node != null) {      // update: never evicts
            node.val = value;
            unlink(node);
            addFront(node);
            return;
        }
        if (map.size() == capacity) {
            Node oldest = tail.prev;
            unlink(oldest);
            map.remove(oldest.key);  // why each node stores its key
        }
        node = new Node(key, value);
        map.put(key, node);
        addFront(node);
    }

    private void unlink(Node node) {
        node.prev.next = node.next;
        node.next.prev = node.prev;
    }

    private void addFront(Node node) {
        node.prev = head;
        node.next = head.next;
        head.next.prev = node;
        head.next = node;
    }
}
```

**O(1) per `get` and `put`** (expected, for the hash map), and **O(capacity) space**. LeetCode guarantees capacity ≥ 1.

</HintLadder>

<Reveal q="Doesn't Java already have this?">

It does. A `LinkedHashMap` built with `accessOrder = true` keeps entries in access order, and overriding `removeEldestEntry` turns it into an LRU cache. Mention it — then build the manual version anyway, because that is the actual test.

```java id=lruLinked
class LRUCache extends LinkedHashMap<Integer, Integer> {
    private final int capacity;
    LRUCache(int capacity) {
        super(16, 0.75f, true);              // true = order by access, not insertion
        this.capacity = capacity;
    }
    public int get(int key) { return getOrDefault(key, -1); }   // a hit counts as access
    public void put(int key, int value) { super.put(key, value); }
    @Override protected boolean removeEldestEntry(Map.Entry<Integer, Integer> eldest) {
        return size() > capacity;            // evict after inserting past capacity
    }
}
```

</Reveal>

LRU Cache deserves its own practice session: it is a small system design, not a trick.

**Catch the bug:** the map updated and the list not, or the other way round. The two structures drift, and a later `get` finds a node that was evicted, or eviction removes a key that is still in the list. Every operation touches both. The quietest version: forgetting `map.remove(oldest.key)`, so the map grows forever and `get` returns evicted values. [Solve LRU Cache](https://leetcode.com/problems/lru-cache/).

<h2 id="cycle-entrance">Side quest 1 · Where the loop begins</h2>

**The card says:** don't just tell me there is a loop. Name the island where it begins — or `null` if there is none. Linked List Cycle II.

**The reasoning:** this is Chapter 4's phase 2, on a real chain. Race the couriers (Chapter 2). If Fast finds `null`, there is no loop. If they meet, start a walker at the head and step it and Slow one island at a time: by the distance argument, a = (k − 1)·L + (L − b), they meet exactly at the entrance.

<Reveal q="What if the last island loops all the way back to the head?">

Then the tail is empty: a = 0. The couriers start on the entrance but must move before comparing, so Slow sails exactly one lap and they meet back on the head (b = 0). Phase 2's walker starts on the head, already equal to Slow, so the loop body never runs and the head is returned. Correct, with no special case.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.cycleEntrance}>

```java id=detectCycle
public ListNode detectCycle(ListNode head) {
    ListNode slow = head, fast = head;
    while (fast != null && fast.next != null) {
        slow = slow.next;
        fast = fast.next.next;
        if (slow == fast) {                 // phase 1 done: we are in the loop
            ListNode walker = head;
            while (walker != slow) {        // phase 2: equal steps
                walker = walker.next;
                slow = slow.next;
            }
            return walker;                  // the entrance
        }
    }
    return null;                            // fast found the end: no loop
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) extra space.**

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** in Find the Duplicate you started at index 0 and never checked for `null`. Name the two facts about that array that made both shortcuts safe.

<h2 id="palindrome-list">Side quest 2 · A chain that reads both ways</h2>

**The card says:** does the chain read the same from both ends? O(1) extra memory — no copying values into an array.

**The reasoning:** you cannot walk backwards, so make the back half walk forwards. Find the middle (Chapter 1), reverse the second half (Chapter 5), then walk both halves together comparing values. Then **reverse it back**: the caller's chain should look the way they gave it to you.

<Reveal q="With an odd length, the middle island ends up in both halves. Is that a problem?">

No. With Chapter 1's guard, Slow stops on the true middle and the reversed half starts there. The island before the middle still points at it, so the front walker also reaches it — and compares the middle with itself. The comparison stops when the reversed half runs out.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.palindrome}>

```java id=isPalindrome
public boolean isPalindrome(ListNode head) {
    ListNode slow = head, fast = head;
    while (fast != null && fast.next != null) {
        slow = slow.next;
        fast = fast.next.next;
    }
    ListNode tail = reverse(slow);               // the back half, readable forwards
    boolean same = true;
    for (ListNode l = head, r = tail; r != null; l = l.next, r = r.next) {
        if (l.val != r.val) { same = false; break; }
    }
    reverse(tail);                               // put the islands back as you found them
    return same;
}

private ListNode reverse(ListNode cur) {
    ListNode prev = null;
    while (cur != null) {
        ListNode next = cur.next;
        cur.next = prev;
        prev = cur;
        cur = next;
    }
    return prev;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) extra space.** The restore works because the island before the middle never stopped pointing at the middle.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** a recursive solution can compare front and back without reversing anything. What does it cost, and which constraint does it break?

<h2 id="intersection">Side quest 3 · Where two chains join</h2>

**The card says:** two chains may merge into one shared tail. Return the first island they share, or `null`. O(m + n) time, O(1) memory.

**The reasoning:** call the first chain's own part a, the second chain's own part b, and the shared tail c. Walking side by side from the two heads, the walkers reach the join at different times whenever a ≠ b. So **let each walker, when it runs off its chain, restart on the other chain's head**. Walker A covers a + c, then b. Walker B covers b + c, then a. **a + c + b = b + c + a**: they step onto the join on the same turn. With no shared tail, both reach `null` on the same turn instead, and the loop ends with `null`.

<Figure caption="A: 4 → 1 → [8 → 4 → 5]. B: 5 → 6 → 1 → [8 → 4 → 5]. The purple islands are the same nodes. The two 1s are different nodes with equal values — so the answer is the 8, not the 1. Here a = 2, b = 3, c = 3: both walkers pass 2 + 3 + 3 = 8 nodes, then step onto the 8 together.">
  <ArrayStrip name="A" values={[4, 1, 8, 4, 5, 'null']} join="→" indices={false} tones={{ 2: 'purple', 3: 'purple', 4: 'purple', 5: 'ghost' }} />
  <ArrayStrip name="B" values={[5, 6, 1, 8, 4, 5, 'null']} join="→" indices={false} tones={{ 3: 'purple', 4: 'purple', 5: 'purple', 6: 'ghost' }} />
</Figure>

<Reveal q="Why does the loop always end, even when the chains never meet?">

Each walker visits at most a + c + b islands, plus one `null` step when it switches. If the chains share no tail (c = 0), walker A is on `null` after a + b + 1 steps (a, then null, then b) and so is walker B (b, null, a). `null == null`, the loop ends, and the method returns `null`. If a = b, they meet on their first pass, at the join or at `null`.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.intersection}>

```java id=getIntersectionNode
public ListNode getIntersectionNode(ListNode headA, ListNode headB) {
    ListNode a = headA, b = headB;
    while (a != b) {
        a = (a == null) ? headB : a.next;   // ran off A? continue on B
        b = (b == null) ? headA : b.next;   // ran off B? continue on A
    }
    return a;                               // the shared island, or null
}
```

**O(m + n) time, O(1) extra space.** The equivalent "measure both lengths, advance the longer one by the difference" also works; this version needs no arithmetic.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** why must the comparison be `a != b` on nodes? Build two chains whose values make a value comparison stop one island early.

<h2 id="add-two-numbers">Side quest 4 · Add two numbers</h2>

**The card says:** each chain is a non-negative number, one digit per island, **least significant digit first**. Return their sum in the same form. `2 → 4 → 3` plus `5 → 6 → 4` is 342 + 465 = 807, so return `7 → 0 → 8`.

**The reasoning:** least significant first is exactly the order you add by hand. Chapter 8's tools build the answer: a dummy and a tail, plus a carry that survives between columns. Keep going **while either chain or the carry remains**.

<Reveal q="Why does the loop condition include carry != 0?">

Because the sum can be one digit longer than both inputs. `5` plus `5` is 10: after the only column, both chains are used up but the carry is 1, and it needs its own island. Stop on the chains alone and you return `0` instead of `0 → 1`.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.addTwo}>

```java id=addTwoNumbers
public ListNode addTwoNumbers(ListNode l1, ListNode l2) {
    ListNode dummy = new ListNode(0), tail = dummy;
    int carry = 0;
    while (l1 != null || l2 != null || carry != 0) {
        int sum = carry;
        if (l1 != null) { sum += l1.val; l1 = l1.next; }
        if (l2 != null) { sum += l2.val; l2 = l2.next; }
        tail.next = new ListNode(sum % 10);
        tail = tail.next;
        carry = sum / 10;
    }
    return dummy.next;
}
```

**O(max(m, n)) time**, and the output has at most max(m, n) + 1 nodes. Never convert to `int` or `long`: the chains can be 100 digits long.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** Add Two Numbers II stores the **most** significant digit first. Which chapter turns it back into this problem, and what does that cost the inputs?

<h2 id="rotate-list">Side quest 5 · Rotate the chain</h2>

**The card says:** rotate the chain to the right by k places: each place moves the last island to the front. `1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5` rotated by 2 is `4 → 5 → 1 → 2 → 3`. k can be far larger than the chain.

**The reasoning:** rotating by the length changes nothing, so first **measure the length n** (the same walk finds the last island) and reduce `k %= n`. The last k islands move to the front, so the new tail is island n − k. Then **close the ring and cut it**: tie the old last island to the old head, and cut after the new tail.

<Reveal q="k = 2,000,000,000 on a three-island chain?">

2,000,000,000 mod 3 = 2, so you rotate by 2. Never walk k steps; walk at most 2n. And if k mod n is 0, return the head untouched.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.rotate}>

```java id=rotateRight
public ListNode rotateRight(ListNode head, int k) {
    if (head == null || head.next == null) return head;
    int n = 1;
    ListNode last = head;
    while (last.next != null) { last = last.next; n++; }  // length and last island
    k %= n;
    if (k == 0) return head;
    ListNode newTail = head;
    for (int i = 1; i < n - k; i++) newTail = newTail.next; // island n − k
    ListNode newHead = newTail.next;
    newTail.next = null;   // cut
    last.next = head;      // close the old ends together
    return newHead;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) extra space.**

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** the new head is the kth island from the end — Chapter 3's gap could find it in one pass. Why does this problem still need the length?

<h2 id="sort-list">Side quest 6 · Sort a chain</h2>

**The card says:** sort the chain in O(n log n) time. No copying values into an array.

**The reasoning:** quicksort wants random access; a chain has none. **Merge sort** only needs two things a chain does well: **split** it at the middle (Chapter 1, then cut the rope between the halves) and **merge** two sorted chains (Chapter 8). Sort each half recursively, then merge.

<Reveal q="Which middle do you split at, and does it matter?">

It matters. Split at the **first** middle: start `fast = head.next`. On a two-island chain, Slow stays on the first island and the halves are 1 + 1. With Chapter 1's second-middle start, a two-island chain splits into 2 + 0, the left half is the whole chain again, and the recursion never ends.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.sortList}>

```java id=sortList
public ListNode sortList(ListNode head) {
    if (head == null || head.next == null) return head;
    ListNode slow = head, fast = head.next;        // first middle: 2 islands split 1 + 1
    while (fast != null && fast.next != null) {
        slow = slow.next;
        fast = fast.next.next;
    }
    ListNode right = slow.next;
    slow.next = null;                              // cut the rope between the halves
    return merge(sortList(head), sortList(right));
}

private ListNode merge(ListNode a, ListNode b) {
    ListNode dummy = new ListNode(0), tail = dummy;
    while (a != null && b != null) {
        if (a.val <= b.val) { tail.next = a; a = a.next; }
        else { tail.next = b; b = b.next; }
        tail = tail.next;
    }
    tail.next = (a != null) ? a : b;
    return dummy.next;
}
```

**O(n log n) time**: log n levels of splitting, O(n) merging per level. The recursion uses **O(log n) stack space**; LeetCode's follow-up asks for O(1), which needs the bottom-up version (see Beyond this page). The `<=` keeps it stable.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** Merge k Sorted Lists can be done by merging pairs, level by level, exactly like this merge sort's upward half. What is its running time with N total nodes and k lists?

<h2 id="choose-the-tool">When to leave the ferries</h2>

<Scene title="The merchant's shortcut" mood="storm">

A merchant arrives with a sorted chain of a hundred thousand islands and a clever plan. "Middles are cheap now," he says. "The couriers find one in a single trip. So I'll binary search: find the middle, go left or right, find the middle again. Log n trips!"

He sends the couriers out for the first question of the day. They come back at dusk.

The Keeper does the sum on the pier post. *"Each middle costs a walk,"* she says. *"Half the chain, then a quarter, then an eighth. That adds up to the whole chain — for one question. You have a hundred thousand questions."*

</Scene>

Finding a middle is O(length), not O(1). Each round of binary search walks the remaining range to find its middle: Slow alone takes n/2 + n/4 + … ≈ n hops per query, and Fast twice that. That is **O(n) per search**, no better than a straight scan. When a chain will face many random-access questions, **copy it into an array once** (O(n) time and space), then binary search or index freely. For membership questions, a `HashSet` is simpler still.

The chain techniques shine for **one pass with O(1) memory**. Reach for something else when:

- You need **random access** (by index, by binary search) many times: copy to an array.
- Memory is allowed and clarity matters: an `ArrayList<ListNode>` makes Reorder List a two-pointer problem. It is correct — say it first, then offer the O(1) version.
- The input is an array to begin with: don't build a list. (Find the Duplicate *imagines* one; it never allocates a node.)
- You need ordering by key with fast inserts and lookups: a `TreeMap` or a heap, not a hand-kept sorted chain.

<Reveal q="Is copying into an ArrayList ever the right interview answer?">

Yes, as a first answer, when you name its cost. "Copy to a list, two pointers from the ends, O(n) space" is a working Reorder List. Then: "the follow-up is O(1) space: middle, reverse, weave." Interviewers reward the path from correct to optimal. They don't reward freezing while you search for the clever version.

</Reveal>

1. **Keep only the loners.** A sorted chain: delete every island whose value appears more than once, keeping only values that appear exactly once. `1 → 2 → 3 → 3 → 4 → 4 → 5` becomes `1 → 2 → 5`. Test a chain where the head's value repeats, and one where every value repeats.
2. **Swap neighbours.** Swap every two adjacent islands, by re-tying ropes, not by swapping values. `1 → 2 → 3 → 4` becomes `2 → 1 → 4 → 3`. Test an odd length and a single island.
3. **Split by a threshold.** Given x, re-tie the chain so every island with a value below x comes before every other island, keeping the original order **within** both groups. Test x below every value, above every value, and a chain that alternates.

<Reveal q="I've written and tested it. What should my reasoning contain?">

**Loners:** a dummy (the head itself may be deleted) and `prev`, the last island known to be kept. At each step, if the island after `prev` starts a run of equal values, skip the whole run: `prev.next = first island after the run`. Otherwise advance `prev`. Return `dummy.next`.

**Swap neighbours:** Chapter 7 with k = 2 works. The direct version: a dummy, `before`, and per pair `a = before.next`, `b = a.next`; re-tie `a.next = b.next`, `b.next = a`, `before.next = b`, then `before = a`. Three re-ties, and every island stays held.

**Split by a threshold:** two dummies and two tails, one chain for "below x" and one for the rest. Splice each island onto the right tail, tie the first chain's tail to the second chain's head, and **set the second tail's `next` to `null`**. Forget that last line and the old rope can tie the end back into the middle: a loop.

## Six bugs the Keeper keeps in her pocket

1. **Losing the rope.** Writing `cur.next = prev` before saving `next` — the rest of the chain is gone.
2. **Returning `cur`** (which is `null`) instead of `prev` from a reversal.
3. **The `fast.next.next` NullPointerException.** The guard is `fast != null && fast.next != null`, in that order.
4. **Seam amnesia** in stretch reversals. The reversed chunk floats disconnected; always re-tie both ends.
5. **Comparing values, not nodes** in cycle detection. Duplicate values give false positives.
6. **LRU: map updated, list not** (or the other way round). The two structures drift; every operation touches both.
