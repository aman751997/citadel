<h2 id="prologue">Prologue · The newest goes first</h2>

<Scene chapter="Prologue" title="The plate tower and the gallery" mood="dusk">

The Observatory sits on top of Cairn Hill, and on your first night the Keeper doesn't show you the telescope. She shows you the kitchen.

Against the wall stands the plate tower: forty white plates, stacked. Push, the porter, sets a clean one on top. Pop lifts the top one off for the soup. *"Which plate does Pop take next?"* The top one. Always the newest.

Then she leads you to the waiting gallery: a narrow corridor with one door and no other way out. The eyepiece hangs high on the wall, and nobody can reach it alone. You wait until someone taller than you comes in after you. They lift you up, and you leave.

A tall woman walks in. The two boys nearest the door are lifted out, one after the other. The man behind them is taller than she is, so she stops and takes her place by the door.

*"She never looked past him,"* the Keeper says. *"Why didn't she need to?"*

</Scene>

A **stack** is a pile you touch only at the top. `push` adds an item on top, `pop` removes the top item, and `peek` looks at it without removing it. All three are O(1). Nothing below the top can be reached until everything above it has gone: **last in, first out**.

That sounds like a restriction. It is the whole point. When the answer always involves **the most recent unfinished thing**, a stack hands you exactly that thing in O(1) and keeps everything else out of the way.

Before every push or pop, ask the Keeper's three questions:

1. **What is still waiting, and what do the waiting items have in common?** An open bracket with no closer yet. A day that hasn't seen a warmer day yet. And the shape they form on the stack.
2. **Why is this pop safe?** Which waiting item has just been answered, and why will it never be needed again?
3. **When do I stop popping, and what happens to the leftovers?** You stop at the first item the arrival can't answer. Leftovers get a default answer, or a sentinel flushes them out.

### When this pattern fires

There are two triggers, and each one leads to its own sub-pattern:

1. **Nesting, matching, undo.** Brackets, expressions, "the most recently opened thing must close first", undo history. Here, last-in-first-out *is* the problem statement. That's Part One.
2. **The nearest greater or smaller element.** For each item, find the nearest item to one side that beats it. In disguise, this is the next warmer day, the first taller bar, a car catching the car ahead, the widest rectangle under some bars, or a stock price's span. That's Part Two: the **monotonic stack**, and it is the sub-pattern interviews actually test.

The brute force for the second trigger scans forward from every item. For a month that only gets colder, that's n(n − 1)/2 comparisons: **O(n²)**. A monotonic stack does the same job in **O(n)**, because each item is pushed once and popped at most once.

**Java note:** use `ArrayDeque` as your stack, not the legacy `Stack` class. `Stack` extends `Vector`, synchronizes every call, and its own Javadoc recommends `Deque` instead. Saying this in an interview costs nothing and shows care.

```java fragment
Deque<Integer> stack = new ArrayDeque<>();
stack.push(4);        // top is 4
stack.push(9);        // top is 9
stack.peek();         // 9, still there
stack.pop();          // 9, removed
stack.isEmpty();      // false: 4 is left
```

Two behaviours to know by heart: `pop()` on an empty `ArrayDeque` throws `NoSuchElementException`, and `peek()` returns `null` (which then throws a `NullPointerException` if you unbox it into an `int`). Printing an `ArrayDeque` lists the **top first**.

<h2 id="valid-parentheses">Chapter 1 · The nested cases</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 1" title="The nested cases" mood="dawn">

In the instrument room, the lenses travel in nested cases: round ones, square ones, and the domed ones the old astronomers drew as `{` and `}`. A case may be shut only after every case inside it has been shut.

The sister observatory sends a packing slip with every crate, one symbol per line, in the order the cases were opened and shut. Tonight's slip reads `( [ ) ]`.

You read it aloud. A round case opens. A square case opens inside it. Then someone shuts… the round one, with the square case still open inside.

*"Was it packed properly?"* the Keeper asks. *"And which case were you allowed to shut at that moment?"*

</Scene>

**Problem: Valid Parentheses · Match on close**

At any moment, the only case you may shut is **the one opened most recently that is still open**. That is the top of a stack. Push on open. On close, the top must be its partner.

Here is a trick worth stealing from your old notes: **push the closer you expect**, not the opener you saw. Then checking a closer is one comparison against `pop()`.

<Figure caption="Reading ( [ ) ]. Two cases are open, and the top expects ]. The arriving ) would shut the outer case while the inner one is still open, so the pairs cross. Reject.">
  <ArrayStrip name="input" values={['(', '[', ')', ']']} pointers={[{ at: 2, label: 'NEXT' }]} tones={{ 0: 'done', 1: 'done', 2: 'hot' }} />
  <ArrayStrip name="expected closers · bottom → top" values={[')', ']']} indices={false} pointers={[{ at: 1, label: 'TOP', tone: 'blue' }]} tones={{ 1: 'blue' }} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="Which case may close?" steps={brackets} takeaway="Remember: an opener pushes its closer; a closer must match the top; an empty stack at the end means valid. Guard every pop." />

**Why it works:** the stack holds exactly the open cases, oldest at the bottom and innermost on top. A correct closer must close the innermost open case. If it closes anything else, two pairs cross. Every closer is checked against the one case it is allowed to close.

**What stays true:** the stack lists every opener still waiting for its closer, in the order they were opened.

<Reveal q="With only one kind of bracket, ( and ), do you still need a stack?">

No. A counter is enough: add 1 on `(`, subtract 1 on `)`, fail if the counter ever goes below 0, and require 0 at the end. With one kind, all openers are the same, so you only need to know *how many* are open, not *which*.

With three kinds, you need to know which kind is innermost. Three separate counters can't do that: `([)]` brings both counters back to 0 and would be accepted.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.parentheses}>

```java id=isValid
public boolean isValid(String s) {
    Deque<Character> st = new ArrayDeque<>();
    for (char c : s.toCharArray()) {
        switch (c) {
            case '(' -> st.push(')');
            case '[' -> st.push(']');
            case '{' -> st.push('}');
            default -> { if (st.isEmpty() || st.pop() != c) return false; }
        }
    }
    return st.isEmpty();          // leftovers = unclosed opens
}
```

`st.pop() != c` compares a `Character` with a `char`, so Java unboxes and compares the characters themselves. **O(n) time, O(n) space** in the worst case (all openers). An odd-length string can return `false` at once; that's a cheap early exit, not a fix.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** you've tripped on both of the classic misses before. One is closing on an **empty stack**: `")("` must return `false` before `pop()` throws. The other is forgetting the **end check**: `"(("` never fails during the loop, so returning `true` there is wrong. Leftovers mean unclosed openers. [Solve Valid Parentheses](https://leetcode.com/problems/valid-parentheses/).

<h2 id="min-stack">Chapter 2 · The lightest plate</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 2" title="The lightest plate">

The cook has a new demand. The soup bowls are delicate, and each one must go on the lightest plate in the tower. She wants that plate's weight *instantly*, at any moment, while Push and Pop keep changing the tower.

Pop suggests keeping one number, "the lightest so far". It works until he lifts off the lightest plate. Then the number is wrong, and nobody knows what the next-lightest was.

Push fetches a stick of chalk. On the rim of every plate he sets down, he writes one number: the weight of the lightest plate at or below this one.

*"When Pop takes the top plate away,"* the Keeper asks, *"is the chalk on the new top plate still true?"*

</Scene>

**Problem: Min Stack · Store extra information at every level**

Design a stack with `push`, `pop`, `top` and `getMin`, **all in O(1)**.

A single `min` variable handles pushes and fails on pops: after `push 5, push 3, pop`, it still says 3. The minimum from *before* the last push has to be remembered somewhere, and it has to come back exactly when that push is undone.

So keep a second stack, `mins`, the same height as `values`. Each level of `mins` records **the minimum of everything at or below that level**. Push both together, pop both together.

<Figure caption="The chalk on every plate. After push 5, 3, 7, 2, each mins level is the minimum at or below it. Pop the 2 and the new top of mins says 3, which was written when 7 was the top and is still true.">
  <ArrayStrip name="values" values={[5, 3, 7, 2]} pointers={[{ at: 3, label: 'TOP' }]} tones={{ 3: 'hot' }} />
  <ArrayStrip name="mins" values={[5, 3, 3, 2]} pointers={[{ at: 3, label: 'getMin()', tone: 'green' }]} tones={{ 3: 'done' }} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="Chalk on every plate" steps={minStack} takeaway="Remember: every level stores the minimum at or below it. Push both stacks together; pop both together; getMin is the top of mins." />

**Why it works:** a level's chalk is computed from the values at or below it, and those never change while that level exists. Only the top can change. So when a level becomes the top again, its chalk is still exactly right.

**What stays true:** for every level k, `mins[k]` is the minimum of `values[0..k]`.

The general lesson, from your old notes: **give each stack level its own extra information** instead of recomputing it. The same idea gives you a max stack, a stack that knows its sum, or Stock Span's (price, span) pairs later in this book.

<HintLadder hints={hints.minStack}>

```java id=minStack
class MinStack {
    private final Deque<Integer> values = new ArrayDeque<>();
    private final Deque<Integer> mins = new ArrayDeque<>();   // mins.peek() = minimum at or below the top

    public void push(int val) {
        values.push(val);
        mins.push(mins.isEmpty() ? val : Math.min(val, mins.peek()));
    }
    public void pop() {
        values.pop();
        mins.pop();
    }
    public int top() { return values.peek(); }
    public int getMin() { return mins.peek(); }
}
```

**O(1) per operation, O(n) extra space.** The problem promises that `pop`, `top` and `getMin` are only called on a non-empty stack.

</HintLadder>

<Reveal q="Can you save space by pushing onto mins only when a new record arrives?">

Yes, and it's a favourite follow-up. Push onto `mins` only when the new value is **less than or equal to** the current minimum. Pop from `mins` only when the value leaving `values` equals the current minimum.

```java id=minStackLean
class MinStackLean {
    private final Deque<Integer> values = new ArrayDeque<>();
    private final Deque<Integer> mins = new ArrayDeque<>();   // only record-setting values

    public void push(int val) {
        values.push(val);
        if (mins.isEmpty() || val <= mins.peek()) mins.push(val);   // <=, not <
    }
    public void pop() {
        int removed = values.pop();             // unbox first: compare ints, not Integer objects
        if (removed == mins.peek()) mins.pop();
    }
    public int top() { return values.peek(); }
    public int getMin() { return mins.peek(); }
}
```

Why `<=`: after `push 2, push 2`, a strict `<` records only one 2. The first `pop` removes that record while another 2 is still on the stack, and `getMin()` finds `mins` empty. The worst case is still O(n) extra space (a stack that only ever gets smaller), but typical inputs use much less.

</Reveal>

**Catch the bug:** the lean version has a Java trap that small tests never catch.

```java fragment
if (values.pop() == mins.peek()) mins.pop();   // compares two Integer objects
```

`==` on two `Integer` objects compares references. Java caches the boxes for -128 to 127, so `push 10, push 9, pop` works by luck. With `push 1000, push 999, pop`, the two 999s are different objects, the record is never popped, and `getMin()` answers 999 though only 1000 remains. Unbox to `int` first, as above, or use `equals`. [Solve Min Stack](https://leetcode.com/problems/min-stack/).

<h2 id="reverse-polish">Chapter 3 · The reckoning engine</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 3" title="The reckoning engine" mood="night">

In the cellar stands the reckoning engine: brass levers and a hopper for punched cards. Its builders hated brackets, so the engine reads cards with no brackets at all. Tonight's cards are `4`, `13`, `5`, `/`, `+`.

Numbers drop onto a spike. Each operator card grabs the two newest numbers off the spike, works on them, and spikes the result.

Last week someone serviced the engine, and now it prints nonsense: for `5 3 -` it prints minus two. The levers are fine. Something about *which number goes where* is not.

*"When the minus card arrives,"* the Keeper asks, *"which number is on the left of the sign?"*

</Scene>

**Problem: Evaluate Reverse Polish Notation · Operators consume the most recent results**

In Reverse Polish Notation (postfix), every operator comes **after** its operands: `3 + 4` is written `3 4 +`. No brackets are needed, because the order of the tokens is already the order of the work.

Read the tokens left to right. Push numbers. When an operator arrives, its two operands are the **two most recent results**: pop them, combine them, and push the answer. One value is left at the end.

<Figure caption="The tree for 4 + 13 / 5. Read it in post-order (left subtree, right subtree, then the operator) and you get exactly the cards: 4 13 5 / +. The right subtree finishes last, so its value is on top when the operator arrives.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart TD\n  P["+"] --> A["4"]\n  P --> D["/"]\n  D --> B["13"]\n  D --> C["5"]`} />
</Figure>

The one trap is **operand order**. The first pop is the value written *last*, which is the **right-hand operand**. For `5 3 -`, the first pop is 3 and the second is 5: compute 5 − 3.

<Trace title="Which number is on the left?" steps={rpn} takeaway="Remember: right = pop(), left = pop(), push(left op right). Java's int division truncates toward zero, as the problem asks." />

**Why it works:** each operator's right operand is the expression that finished most recently, so its value is on top. The left operand finished just before it, so its value is directly underneath. Replacing two values with one keeps that true for whatever comes next.

**What stays true:** the stack holds the values of the complete sub-expressions read so far, in order, none of them used yet.

<Reveal q="Why not test token.charAt(0) to tell numbers from operators?">

Because `-11` is a number that starts with `-`. Compare the **whole token** with `"+"`, `"-"`, `"*"` and `"/"`, and treat anything else as a number.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.rpn}>

```java id=evalRPN
public int evalRPN(String[] tokens) {
    Deque<Integer> stack = new ArrayDeque<>();
    for (String token : tokens) {
        switch (token) {
            case "+", "-", "*", "/" -> {
                int right = stack.pop();   // popped first = written second
                int left = stack.pop();
                stack.push(switch (token) {
                    case "+" -> left + right;
                    case "-" -> left - right;
                    case "*" -> left * right;
                    default -> left / right;   // Java truncates toward zero, as required
                });
            }
            default -> stack.push(Integer.parseInt(token));
        }
    }
    return stack.pop();
}
```

**O(n) time, O(n) space.** The problem guarantees a valid expression, no division by zero, and results that fit in 32 bits.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** this one-liner is the engine's broken lever.

```java fragment
case "-" -> stack.push(stack.pop() - stack.pop());   // first pop is the RIGHT operand
```

It computes right − left, so `5 3 -` gives −2. It passes every test that uses only `+` and `*`, which is why it survives until the interview. [Solve Evaluate Reverse Polish Notation](https://leetcode.com/problems/evaluate-reverse-polish-notation/).

<h2 id="daily-temperatures">Chapter 4 · The weather ledger</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 4" title="The weather ledger" mood="night">

Every evening the Keeper writes the temperature in the weather ledger: 73, 74, 75, 71, 69, 72, 76, 73. On cold nights the lens fogs, and the astronomers want to know, for every night, how many nights they would have waited for a warmer one.

You start the obvious way: for each night, read forward until something warmer appears. Last winter's ledger had five cold weeks in a row, and you read the same nights again and again.

The Keeper closes the ledger and walks you to the gallery. *"Pretend every night is a visitor. They come in one at a time, and they wait. When tonight walks in,"* she says, *"who does it answer?"*

</Scene>

**Problem: Daily Temperatures · Next greater, with a waiting stack**

This is your Stack kickoff. It has been queued on your hub since July 1st, and you already recognise the trigger. Time to collect.

The brute force reads forward from every day: O(n²) for a ledger that only cools. Turn the question around:

> A day sits on the stack because it is **still waiting for its answer**. When today arrives, it answers every waiting day it beats. Pop them and record `today - day`. Then today waits too.

Today can only answer days that are **colder** than it. Which waiting days are those? Here is the part worth understanding, not memorising.

**What stays true (the invariant):** reading the stack from bottom to top, **temperatures never increase**: each waiting day is at most as warm as the day beneath it. When no two waiting days share a temperature, that means strictly decreasing. Equal temperatures can sit together, because 70° does not answer 70°.

Why does it hold? Before today is pushed, it pops every waiting day colder than itself. So whatever is left directly underneath it is at least as warm as today.

**Why stopping early is safe:** if today can't beat the top, it can't beat anyone below the top either, because they are all at least as warm. That's why the woman in the prologue never looked past the taller man.

<Figure caption="Today is day 5 (72°). Days 0 and 1 already have their answers. Days 2, 3 and 4 are waiting, and their temperatures never increase toward the top: 75, 71, 69. 72 will answer day 4 (1 day) and day 3 (2 days), stop at 75, and wait on top of it.">
  <ArrayStrip name="temps" values={[73, 74, 75, 71, 69, 72, 76, 73]} pointers={[{ at: 5, label: 'TODAY' }]} tones={{ 0: 'done', 1: 'done', 2: 'hot', 3: 'hot', 4: 'hot' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 1, label: 'answered', tone: 'green' }, { from: 2, to: 4, label: 'waiting', tone: 'gold' }, { from: 6, to: 7, label: 'not read yet', tone: 'dim' }]} />
  <ArrayStrip name="waiting · bottom → top" values={['d2 · 75', 'd3 · 71', 'd4 · 69']} indices={false} pointers={[{ at: 2, label: 'TOP', tone: 'blue' }]} tones={{ 0: 'hot', 1: 'hot', 2: 'hot' }} />
  <ArrayStrip name="answer" values={[1, 1, '?', '?', '?', '·', '·', '·']} tones={{ 0: 'done', 1: 'done' }} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="Who does today answer?" steps={dailyTemperatures} takeaway="Remember: pop while today is strictly warmer than the top, recording today − day. Then push today. Leftovers keep 0." />

**Which way does the stack slope?** Don't memorise "decreasing stack for next greater". Work it out from who gets answered. A warmer arrival pops colder days, so the days left underneath are warmer: temperatures fall (never rise) as you go up. For the next *smaller* element, a colder arrival pops warmer values, so the values rise as you go up.

**Push indexes, not temperatures.** The answer is a distance, `today - day`, and you can always read a temperature from an index (`temperatures[day]`), but never the index from a temperature.

<HintLadder hints={hints.temps}>

```java id=dailyTemperatures
public int[] dailyTemperatures(int[] temperatures) {
    int n = temperatures.length;
    int[] answer = new int[n];                    // days nobody answers stay 0
    Deque<Integer> waiting = new ArrayDeque<>();  // indexes; temperatures never increase bottom to top
    for (int today = 0; today < n; today++) {
        while (!waiting.isEmpty() && temperatures[today] > temperatures[waiting.peek()]) {
            int answered = waiting.pop();         // today answers this day
            answer[answered] = today - answered;
        }
        waiting.push(today);                      // today now waits
    }
    return answer;
}
```

**O(n) time.** The interviewer will point at the `while` inside the `for` and ask, "Isn't that O(n²)?" Have the answer ready: every index is pushed exactly once and popped at most once, so all the `while` loops *together* pop at most n times. The total is O(n), even though a single day can pop many. **O(n) space**: a ledger that only cools keeps every day waiting.

</HintLadder>

<Reveal q="Now the astronomers want the PREVIOUS warmer day for each night instead. What changes?">

You look left instead of right, and the answer appears at push time instead of pop time. When today arrives, pop every waiting day that is **no warmer** than today (`temperatures[top] <= temperatures[today]`). Whatever is left on top is the nearest earlier day that is strictly warmer, or nothing. Then push today.

Popping those days is safe because today is at least as warm and closer, so no later night will ever need them. Stock Span (a side quest below) is this exact shape.

</Reveal>

**Catch the bug:** using `>=` in the pop condition makes equal days answer each other. On `[70, 70, 71]`, day 1 would "answer" day 0 with 1, though 70° is not warmer than 70°. The correct answer is `[2, 1, 0]`. The other classic is pushing temperatures instead of indexes, which leaves you with no way to compute the distance. [Solve Daily Temperatures](https://leetcode.com/problems/daily-temperatures/).

<h2 id="circular-next-greater">Chapter 5 · The ring of lamps</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 5" title="The ring of lamps" mood="dusk">

Around the rim of the dome hangs a ring of oil lamps, each a different brightness. The lamplighter wants a list: for each lamp, going clockwise, the first lamp that burns brighter.

The ring has no end. After the last lamp comes the first one again.

You try the gallery trick on a small ring, brightness `[1, 2, 1]`. One pass gives `[2, -1, -1]`. But the last lamp is still waiting, and going clockwise past the end, the lamp of brightness 2 is right there.

*"Your visitors only looked at who came after them,"* the Keeper says. *"In a ring, who else comes after them?"*

</Scene>

**Problem: Next Greater Element II · Two laps, push only in the first**

In a circular array, the elements after index `i` are `i+1, …, n-1`, and then `0, …, i-1`. So sweep **twice**: `i` runs from 0 to 2n − 1, and you read `nums[i % n]`. Every waiting index gets to see all of its circular successors.

Push only during the first lap (`i < n`). In the second lap, **answer but don't push**: every index has already waited once, and nobody new needs to.

<Figure caption="The doubled sweep over [1, 2, 1]. In lap one, 2 answers index 0. Index 1 (value 2) and index 2 (value 1) are left waiting. In lap two, i = 4 reads nums[1] = 2, which answers index 2. Nothing is strictly greater than 2, so index 1 keeps −1. Final: [2, −1, 2].">
  <ArrayStrip name="i % n reads" values={[1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 1]} pointers={[{ at: 4, label: 'answers index 2' }]} tones={{ 3: 'ghost', 4: 'ghost', 5: 'ghost' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 2, label: 'lap one: answer and push', tone: 'gold' }, { from: 3, to: 5, label: 'lap two: answer only', tone: 'blue' }]} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="The ring has no end" steps={circular} takeaway="Remember: loop to 2n, read nums[i % n], push only while i < n. Leftovers keep −1; only a maximum value can be left over." />

**Why two laps are enough:** an element's circular next greater, if it exists, is somewhere in the next n − 1 positions after it. Index `i` is pushed at step `i`, and the sweep keeps reading until step 2n − 1, which is at least `i + n - 1`. The pop logic is the same as in Chapter 4, so the first greater value it meets is the one that answers it.

**What stays true:** the waiting values never increase from bottom to top, and every index on the stack has not yet met a strictly greater value going clockwise.

<HintLadder hints={hints.circular}>

```java id=nextGreaterElements
public int[] nextGreaterElements(int[] nums) {
    int n = nums.length;
    int[] answer = new int[n];
    Arrays.fill(answer, -1);                       // leftovers: nothing greater anywhere
    Deque<Integer> waiting = new ArrayDeque<>();   // indexes still waiting
    for (int i = 0; i < 2 * n; i++) {
        int value = nums[i % n];
        while (!waiting.isEmpty() && value > nums[waiting.peek()]) {
            answer[waiting.pop()] = value;
        }
        if (i < n) waiting.push(i);                // the second lap only answers
    }
    return answer;
}
```

**O(n) time**: 2n reads, n pushes, at most n pops. **O(n) space.** No copy of the array is needed; `i % n` does the wrapping.

</HintLadder>

<Reveal q="Many solutions push in both laps. Is that wrong?">

It's wasted work, not a wrong answer. A second-lap copy of index `j` can only be answered by a value from `j + 1` to `n - 1`, and if such a value exists, the first lap already found that same first greater value. If it doesn't exist there, the copy is never popped and changes nothing. Pushing only in the first lap keeps each index waiting once, which makes the "pushed once, popped at most once" argument easy to say.

</Reveal>

**Catch the bug:** stopping after one lap gives `[2, -1, -1]` on `[1, 2, 1]`. The correct answer is `[2, -1, 2]`. Using `>=` lets equal values answer each other: `[7, 7]` must stay `[-1, -1]`. [Solve Next Greater Element II](https://leetcode.com/problems/next-greater-element-ii/).

**Problem: Car Fleet · Sort, then merge into the fleet ahead**

The coach closest to the gate can't be slowed: nobody is in front of it. That's the first thing that's settled. So **process cars from the front to the back**, and give each one its **solo arrival time**, `(target - position) / speed`: when it would arrive if the road were empty.

A car can never arrive before the car directly in front of it. So its real arrival time is **the larger of its own solo time and the arrival time of the fleet directly ahead**:

- **Solo time ≤ fleet ahead:** it catches up at or before the target and joins that fleet. Arriving together exactly at the target counts as joining.
- **Solo time > fleet ahead:** it never catches them. It leads a **new fleet**, and that fleet is now "the fleet directly ahead" for every car behind it.

That's stack thinking: a stack of fleet arrival times, with the fleet directly ahead on top. A slower car pushes a new fleet. A faster car is absorbed and pushes nothing. The answer is the stack's size.

<Figure caption="Front to back, with each coach's solo time. 8 (1 h) catches 10 (1 h) exactly at the gate. 3 (3 h) catches 5 (7 h). 0 (12 h) is slower than everything ahead and arrives alone. That makes three fleets.">

**What stays true:** after each car, the stack holds the arrival time of every fleet formed so far, front fleet at the bottom. Times **strictly increase** from bottom to top, and the top is the fleet directly ahead of the next car.

```java id=carFleet
public int carFleet(int target, int[] position, int[] speed) {
    int n = position.length;
    Integer[] order = new Integer[n];
    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) order[i] = i;
    Arrays.sort(order, (a, b) -> Integer.compare(position[b], position[a]));   // closest to target first
    Deque<Double> fleets = new ArrayDeque<>();     // arrival times; the fleet directly ahead is on top
    for (int car : order) {
        double time = (double) (target - position[car]) / speed[car];
        if (fleets.isEmpty() || time > fleets.peek()) {
            fleets.push(time);                     // can't catch the fleet ahead: a new fleet
        }                                          // otherwise it catches up and joins
    }
    return fleets.size();
}
```

**O(n log n) time** for the sort; the sweep itself is O(n). **O(n) space.** Positions are distinct in this problem, so the sort order is unambiguous.

At this problem's bounds (up to 10⁶), `double` times are precise enough: equal fractions always give exactly the same `double`. If you'd rather remove all doubt, compare `(long) (target - p1) * s2` with `(long) (target - p2) * s1` instead of dividing.

</HintLadder>

<Reveal q="You only ever read the top of that stack. Do you need the stack at all?">

**Problem: Largest Rectangle in Histogram · Nearest shorter bar on both sides**

The boss of this book. In your old notes you wrote that it is a Hard because of its **seams**, not its idea, and that it deserves a guided walk first and a solo re-fight two days later. Take it slowly.

**The idea.** Every rectangle has a shortest bar, and that bar sets its height. So for each bar, imagine the widest rectangle in which *it* is the shortest bar. That rectangle stretches until the **nearest strictly shorter bar on each side**. The answer is the best of those n rectangles.

That's a "nearest smaller element" question in both directions at once, and one monotonic stack answers both:

- Keep bar indexes with **heights strictly increasing from bottom to top**.
- When a bar arrives that is **no taller** than the top (`heights[top] >= current`), the top's rectangle can't stretch past it. **Finalize** the top: pop it.
- After the pop, the **new top** is the nearest strictly shorter bar on its left, and `i` is where it stops on the right. Width = `i - newTop - 1`.

<Figure caption="The best map: bars 2 and 3, height 5, width 2, area 10. It is finalized when the height-2 bar arrives at i = 4: popping bar 2 (height 5) leaves bar 1 (height 1) on top, so the width is 4 − 1 − 1 = 2.">
  <Bars heights={[2, 1, 5, 6, 2, 3]} rect={{ from: 2, to: 3, height: 5 }} pointers={[{ at: 1, label: 'new top' }, { at: 4, label: 'i', tone: 'blue' }]} />
</Figure>

**The two seams**, which your old notes called the two implementation saves:

1. **The sentinel flush.** Bars still on the stack when the input ends were never finalized. So run `i` one step past the end and treat that position as a bar of **height 0**: the Sentinel. He's shorter than everything, so he finalizes everyone still waiting.
2. **The empty-stack width.** If the stack is empty after a pop, nothing shorter exists on the left, and the rectangle reaches all the way back to index 0. Width = `i`.

<Trace title="How wide is the map?" steps={histogram} takeaway="Remember: pop while the top is at least as tall as the arrival. Width is i − newTop − 1, or i on an empty stack. A height-0 sentinel at i = n flushes the rest." />

**Why it works:** every popped rectangle is real. The bars between the new top and `i` are all at least as tall as the popped bar: the ones that once stood between them were popped by something no taller than themselves. Now take the best rectangle, and let `b` be the *rightmost* of its shortest bars. When `b` is popped, the new top is the nearest strictly shorter bar on its left, and `i` is the nearest bar to its right that is no taller. Because `b` is the rightmost shortest bar, that is exactly the first strictly shorter one. So the pop measures the best rectangle at full width.

**What stays true:** heights on the stack strictly increase from bottom to top, and every bar between two neighbouring stack entries is at least as tall as the upper one.

<Reveal q="What do equal heights do here? Try [3, 3, 3].">

With `>=`, an equal arrival finalizes the earlier equal bar *too early*. Bar 0 is popped at i = 1 with width 1 (area 3), and bar 1 at i = 2 with width 2 (area 6). That's harmless, because the last bar of the run is finalized by the Sentinel with the full width: bar 2 at i = 3, width 3, area 9.

Popping on strict `>` is also correct, the other way round. Equal bars stay stacked, and the *earliest* one of the run gets the full width when it is finally popped. Either works. Know which one you wrote, and why.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.histogram}>

```java id=largestRectangleArea
public int largestRectangleArea(int[] heights) {
    int n = heights.length, best = 0;
    Deque<Integer> stack = new ArrayDeque<>();     // indexes; heights strictly increase bottom to top
    for (int i = 0; i <= n; i++) {
        int current = (i == n) ? 0 : heights[i];   // the sentinel: height 0 flushes the stack
        while (!stack.isEmpty() && heights[stack.peek()] >= current) {
            int height = heights[stack.pop()];
            int left = stack.isEmpty() ? -1 : stack.peek();   // nearest strictly shorter bar on the left
            int width = i - left - 1;              // left == -1 gives width i
            best = Math.max(best, height * width);
        }
        stack.push(i);                             // pushing the sentinel index is harmless
    }
    return best;
}
```

**O(n) time**: each index is pushed once and popped at most once. **O(n) space.** With heights up to 10⁴ and up to 10⁵ bars, the largest area is 10⁹, which fits in an `int`.

</HintLadder>

<Reveal q="Is there a version that's easier to reason about, at the cost of a second pass?">

Yes. Compute `left[i]`, the nearest strictly shorter bar to the left, with one stack pass left to right (or −1). Compute `right[i]`, the nearest strictly shorter bar to the right, with a second pass right to left (or n). The answer is the maximum of `heights[i] × (right[i] - left[i] - 1)`. It's still O(n), with more code. Some people find it easier to prove under pressure; the one-pass version is what most interviewers expect to see.

</Reveal>

**Catch the bug:** without the Sentinel, `[2, 4]` returns 0, because both bars are pushed and never finalized. The answer is 4. Measuring width from the popped bar's own index (`i - popped`) forgets taller bars already popped on its left: at the Sentinel, bar 4 (height 2) would get width 2 instead of 4. <h2 id="next-greater-map">Side quest 1 · Only some visitors ask</h2>

**The card says:** you get two lists of distinct values. `nums1` is a subset of `nums2`. For each value in `nums1`, find the first larger value to its **right in nums2**, or −1.

**The reasoning:** the answers depend only on `nums2`. So compute the next greater value for *every* element of `nums2` in one waiting-stack pass, store it in a `HashMap` from value to answer, and then answer each query with one lookup. Because the values are distinct, they can be both the stack entries and the map keys.

<Reveal q="Why does the word 'distinct' matter so much here?">

The map is keyed by value. If `nums2` had two 4s, they could have different next greater values, and one would overwrite the other. With duplicates, you'd have to key by index, and the query would have to say *which* 4 it means.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.nextGreaterMap}>

```java id=nextGreaterElement
public int[] nextGreaterElement(int[] nums1, int[] nums2) {
    Map<Integer, Integer> nextGreater = new HashMap<>();
    Deque<Integer> waiting = new ArrayDeque<>();   // values from nums2 (all distinct)
    for (int value : nums2) {
        while (!waiting.isEmpty() && value > waiting.peek()) {
            nextGreater.put(waiting.pop(), value);
        }
        waiting.push(value);
    }
    int[] answer = new int[nums1.length];
    for (int i = 0; i < nums1.length; i++) answer[i] = nextGreater.getOrDefault(nums1[i], -1);
    return answer;
}
```

**O(n + m) time, O(n) space**, compared with O(n · m) for scanning `nums2` once per query.

</HintLadder>

class StockSpanner {
    private final Deque<int[]> stack = new ArrayDeque<>();   // {price, span}; prices strictly decrease bottom to top

<h2 id="asteroids">Side quest 3 · Collisions</h2>

**The card says:** rocks sit on a line. The sign is the direction (positive moves right, negative moves left) and the absolute value is the size. All move at the same speed. When two meet, the smaller explodes; equal sizes both explode. What's left?

**The reasoning:** the only rocks that can ever meet are a right-mover followed, somewhere later, by a left-mover. Keep a stack of **survivors so far**. A left-mover that arrives fights the top survivor, as long as the top moves right: the newest right-mover is the one it reaches first. If it wins, it keeps fighting the next survivor down. This time the arrival doesn't answer the waiting items. It **fights** them.

<Reveal q="[−2, −1, 1, 2]: what collides?">

Nothing. The left-movers are already to the left of every right-mover, and they all move apart. A left-mover only fights when the top survivor moves **right**. The `peek() > 0` check is what keeps this input untouched.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.asteroids}>

```java id=asteroidCollision
public int[] asteroidCollision(int[] asteroids) {
    Deque<Integer> survivors = new ArrayDeque<>();
    for (int rock : asteroids) {
        boolean alive = true;
        while (alive && rock < 0 && !survivors.isEmpty() && survivors.peek() > 0) {
            int top = survivors.peek();
            if (top < -rock) survivors.pop();          // the right-mover explodes; keep fighting
            else {
                if (top == -rock) survivors.pop();     // equal sizes: both explode
                alive = false;                         // the incoming rock is gone
            }
        }
        if (alive) survivors.push(rock);
    }
    int[] result = new int[survivors.size()];
    for (int i = result.length - 1; i >= 0; i--) result[i] = survivors.pop();   // top is the rightmost
    return result;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(n) space.** Each rock is pushed at most once and popped at most once. The answer comes out of the stack top-first, so fill the result from the back.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** the rule changes so that on a tie, the left-mover survives and only the right-mover explodes. Which single line changes?

<h2 id="remove-k-digits">Side quest 4 · The smallest number</h2>

**The card says:** you get a number as a string and must remove exactly `k` digits. The rest keep their order. Make the result as small as possible: no leading zeros, and `"0"` if nothing is left.

If removals are left over at the end, take them off the tail: the kept digits never decrease, so the largest ones are at the end.

```java id=removeKdigits
public String removeKdigits(String num, int k) {
    StringBuilder kept = new StringBuilder();      // used as a stack: digits never decrease
    for (char digit : num.toCharArray()) {
        while (k > 0 && kept.length() > 0 && kept.charAt(kept.length() - 1) > digit) {
            kept.deleteCharAt(kept.length() - 1);  // a bigger digit before a smaller one: remove it
            k--;
        }
        kept.append(digit);
    }
    kept.setLength(kept.length() - k);             // removals still owed come off the tail
    int start = 0;
    while (start < kept.length() - 1 && kept.charAt(start) == '0') start++;   // keep at least one digit
    return kept.length() == 0 ? "0" : kept.substring(start);
}
```

**O(n) time, O(n) space.** A `StringBuilder` works as a stack of characters and makes the final string cheap to build.

</HintLadder>

<h2 id="rain-in-layers">Side quest 5 · Rain in layers</h2>

**The card says:** you met Trapping Rain Water in <a href={`${BASE}dsa/lessons/two-pointers/#catch-the-rain`}>the Two Pointers book</a>, with twins walking in from both ends. Now the bars arrive **one at a time from the left**, and you never know how many are still coming. There is no right end to start from.

**The reasoning:** fill the water in **horizontal layers** instead of columns. Keep bar indexes whose heights **never increase** from bottom to top. When a taller bar arrives, pop the top: it is a **floor**. The new top is the left wall, and the arriving bar is the right wall. That fills a layer `width = i - left - 1` wide and `min(left wall, right wall) - floor` deep. If the stack is empty after popping a floor, there is no left wall, and the water runs off.

<Figure caption="Heights 2, 0, 1, 0, 3: five units, filled in three layers. When 1 arrives it fills 1 unit above index 1. When 3 arrives it fills 1 unit above index 3, then a 3-wide layer from height 1 to 2 across indices 1 to 3. Per column, that is 2 + 1 + 2 = 5.">

</Figure>

```java id=trapStack
public int trap(int[] height) {
    Deque<Integer> stack = new ArrayDeque<>();      // indexes; heights never increase bottom to top
    int water = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < height.length; i++) {
        while (!stack.isEmpty() && height[i] > height[stack.peek()]) {
            int floor = stack.pop();
            if (stack.isEmpty()) break;             // no left wall: water runs off
            int left = stack.peek();
            int width = i - left - 1;
            int depth = Math.min(height[left], height[i]) - height[floor];
            water += width * depth;                 // one horizontal layer
        }
        stack.push(i);
    }
    return water;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(n) space.** The two-pointer version is O(1) space but needs both ends up front. This one works on a stream. Equal heights are safe: a floor next to an equal wall adds a layer of depth 0.

</HintLadder>

<h2 id="subarray-minimums">Side quest 6 · Count, don’t find</h2>

**The card says:** add up the minimum of **every** contiguous subarray (modulo 10⁹ + 7). There are n(n + 1)/2 subarrays, far too many to list.

**The reasoning:** turn it around. For each element, ask **in how many subarrays is it the minimum?** An element at `m` is the minimum of every subarray that starts after its nearest smaller element on the left and ends before its nearest smaller element on the right. Its contribution is `arr[m] × (m - left) × (right - m)`.

**The duplicates trap.** With `[2, 2]`, the subarrays are `[2]`, `[2]` and `[2, 2]`, and the sum is 6. If both sides stop only at strictly smaller elements, both 2s claim `[2, 2]`, and you get 8. If both sides stop at smaller-or-equal elements, neither claims it, and you get 4. Fix: **strict on one side, non-strict on the other**, so every subarray is credited to exactly one of its minimums.

That's the histogram loop exactly: pop while `arr[top] >= current`. The new top is the nearest **strictly** smaller element on the left. `i` is the nearest smaller-**or-equal** element on the right. Each subarray is credited to its *rightmost* minimum.

```java id=sumSubarrayMins
public int sumSubarrayMins(int[] arr) {
    final long MOD = 1_000_000_007L;
    int n = arr.length;
    long total = 0;
    Deque<Integer> stack = new ArrayDeque<>();      // indexes; values strictly increase bottom to top
    for (int i = 0; i <= n; i++) {
        while (!stack.isEmpty() && (i == n || arr[stack.peek()] >= arr[i])) {
            int m = stack.pop();
            int left = stack.isEmpty() ? -1 : stack.peek();   // nearest strictly smaller on the left
            // i is the nearest smaller-or-equal on the right (or n)
            total = (total + (long) arr[m] * (m - left) * (i - m)) % MOD;
        }
        stack.push(i);
    }
    return (int) total;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(n) space.** Here the Sentinel is `i == n` itself, which flushes everything without inventing a height. The product of three factors can exceed `int`, so it's computed in `long`, with the modulus taken at every step.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** sum of subarray **maximums**: which comparisons flip? Then the **sum of ranges** (maximum minus minimum over every subarray): how do the two sums combine?

<h2 id="choose-the-tool">When the gallery needs a back door</h2>

<Scene title="The three-night window" mood="storm">

The astronomers change their request. *"For each night, the warmest night within the next three. Nights further away are no use to us."*

You reach for the gallery. Then you stop. In the gallery a visitor waits as long as it takes. Now a night that has fallen three nights behind must *leave*, and it leaves from the **back** of the corridor, where there is no door.

The Keeper smiles for the first time all season. *"Your rule didn't fail. It just never promised a back door."*

</Scene>

A stack only removes from one end. When items must **expire from the old end** as well, as in a sliding window of size k, you need a **deque**. A monotonic deque pops beaten values from the back, exactly as you've been doing, and also drops expired indexes from the front. That's Sliding Window Maximum, and it belongs to the <a href={`${BASE}dsa/lessons/sliding-window/`}>Sliding Window book</a>.

Three more ways to tell a stack isn't the right tool:

- **"The warmest day anywhere after this one"** asks for a maximum, not the *nearest* warmer day. One backward pass with a running maximum does it in O(n) with O(1) extra space.
- **"The k-th largest so far"** or **"the best k"** wants a ranking, not a nearest neighbour. Use a heap (the <a href={`${BASE}dsa/lessons/heaps/`}>Heaps book</a>).
- **Pairs anywhere in the array** that satisfy a sum or difference don't care about who is nearest. Use sorting with two pointers, or hashing.

<Reveal q="Isn't ArrayDeque already a deque? Why does the tool matter?">

The class can do both jobs. What matters is the **promise** your algorithm keeps. A stack algorithm only ever touches the newest end, and its correctness argument says nothing about expiring old items. Once old items must leave, you need a new invariant, such as "every index in the deque is inside the window and values decrease from front to back". You can't borrow the stack proof for that.

</Reveal>

**Recursion is a stack you didn't write.** Every recursive DFS can be turned into a loop with an explicit `Deque`. Do that when the recursion depth could overflow the call stack (say, a path of 10⁵ nodes).

**A monotonic stack is not a heap.** Both give you a "best" item at the top, but a stack's top is the *most recent survivor*, not the global best. The top of a waiting stack is the *coldest* waiting day, which is exactly what the next arrival needs.

Choose one. Use your editor, keep this page closed, and write down what is waiting, the pop condition, and the stopping rule before you code.

1. **Look left for something smaller.** For each value, return the nearest value to its **left** that is strictly smaller, or −1. Test an increasing input, a decreasing input, and repeated values.
2. **Unpack the message.** Decode strings like `3[a2[c]]` into `accaccacc`. Counts can have several digits, and brackets nest. Test `2[ab]3[c]` and `10[a]`.
3. **The biggest window of light.** Given a grid of 0s and 1s, find the area of the largest rectangle containing only 1s. Test an all-zero grid, a single row, and a single column.

<Reveal q="I've written and tested it. What should my reasoning contain?">

**Look left:** go left to right. Pop while the top is **greater than or equal to** the current value; whatever remains on top is strictly smaller and nearest, or −1 if the stack is empty. Then push the current value. The stack's values strictly increase from bottom to top. Each value is pushed and popped once: O(n).

**Unpack:** keep two stacks, one of counts and one of the strings built so far. On `[`, push the current count and the current string, then start fresh. On `]`, pop them and set the current string to the saved string plus the current one repeated count times. Digits accumulate as `count = count * 10 + d`. This is a stack of *saved state*, the same shape as the reckoning engine.

**Biggest window:** turn each row into a histogram. `heights[j]` counts the consecutive 1s ending at this row in column j, and resets to 0 on a 0. Run Chapter 7 on every row and keep the best: O(rows × cols).

Then explain why a slightly wrong version fails on one of your tests. A counterexample you found yourself is stronger evidence of understanding than a memorised template.

</Reveal>

Passing once is a useful checkpoint. Rebuild one of these on another day, with a changed input and without a heading to tell you which pattern to use.

<h2 id="cheat-sheet">The Keeper's notebook</h2>

| When you see… | Start with… | The promise to keep |
|---|---|---|
| Brackets, nesting, "valid sequence" | Push the expected closer; a closer must match the top | The stack lists every unclosed opener, innermost on top |
| O(1) minimum with undo | A parallel stack of "minimum at or below this level" | Each level's extra information stays true while that level exists |
| Postfix expression | Push numbers; an operator pops right, then left | The stack holds finished sub-expression values in order |
| Next warmer / greater, to the right | Waiting stack of indexes; pop while strictly beaten | Values never increase bottom to top; everyone on it is unanswered |
| Next greater in a circular array | Sweep 2n with `i % n`; push only in lap one | Every index waits once and sees all its successors |
| Cars catching up, no passing | Sort front first; compare solo time with the fleet ahead | Fleet arrival times strictly increase bottom to top |
| Largest rectangle under heights | Pop while top ≥ arrival; width i − newTop − 1; height-0 sentinel | Heights strictly increase; a popped rectangle is complete |
| Next greater for a subset of queries | One pass over the reference list into a value → answer map | Each answer is computed once; lookups are O(1) |
| Span looking back, streaming | (price, span) pairs; pop on ≤ and absorb spans | A popped day can never block anyone after today |
| Collisions between neighbours | Stack of survivors; a left-mover fights right-moving tops | Survivors so far never collide with each other |
| Smallest number after k deletions | Non-decreasing stack of digits; pop larger digits while k remains | Kept digits never decrease; trim leftovers from the tail |
| Trapped water, bars arriving live | Stack of non-increasing heights; pop a floor, fill a layer | Each pop fills one horizontal layer, exactly once |
| Sum over every subarray's minimum | Contribution counting with histogram boundaries | Strict on one side, non-strict on the other: each subarray counted once |

A stack solution is **not** automatically O(n). Count pushes and pops: if every item is pushed once and popped at most once, the total work is linear. If items can be pushed more than once, as with a careless second lap, recount.

## Five bugs the Keeper keeps in her pocket

1. **Popping or peeking an empty stack.** `")("`, the histogram's empty-stack width, and RPN on bad input. Check `isEmpty()` first, and remember that `peek()` returns `null`.
2. **Forgetting the leftovers.** Return `stack.isEmpty()` for brackets. Run the Sentinel for the histogram. Make sure the default answer (0, −1) is already in place for anyone still waiting.
3. **Operand order.** The first pop is the right-hand operand. `5 3 -` is 2.
4. **Values where you needed indexes.** Distances and widths need positions. Push indexes and read values through them.
5. **`>` versus `>=` without a reason.** Decide what equal values do. Warmer means strictly warmer. A span counts equal prices. The histogram and the subarray minimums need one strict side and one non-strict side.
