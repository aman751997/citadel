<h2 id="prologue">Prologue · The roll call at dusk</h2>

<Scene chapter="Prologue" title="The roll call at dusk" mood="dusk">

Gullwick Light stands on a black rock at the end of the Saltmarrow coast, and it is built the wrong way up. It is narrow at the top, where the great lamp turns, and wider on every floor below, like a bell. Every room has at most two stairways down — a left stair and a right stair — and each leads to exactly one room on the floor below.

At dusk, before the lamp is lit, the Keeper calls the roll. Every keeper must answer, top floor first, and each floor from west to east. Last season's apprentice did it by running: down the left stairs as far as they went, back up, down the next. He heard every name, but the logbook came out in the order of his running, not the order of the floors.

The Keeper hands you the logbook. *"One floor at a time,"* she says. *"But you can speak to only one keeper at a time, and from up here you can't see the floor below. How will you know where a floor ends?"*

</Scene>

The tower is a **binary tree**, drawn the way trees are always drawn in interviews: root at the top.

- The lamp room is the **root**, on floor 0. A room's **children** are the rooms at the foot of its left and right stairs.
- A room's floor is its **depth**: the number of stairs (edges) between it and the lamp room.
- A room with no stairs down is a **leaf**. The number of rooms on a floor is that floor's **width**.

The last apprentice walked **depth-first**: as deep as possible, then back up. The roll call needs **breadth-first** order: every room on floor 0, then every room on floor 1, then floor 2. The tool is a **queue**, the line on the landing. Call the keeper at the front; she sends for her two stair-keepers, and they join the **back**. Because newcomers always join behind everyone already waiting, **nobody from floor d + 1 can be called before the last keeper on floor d**.

The Keeper asks her three questions here, as everywhere in the realm:

1. **What is settled?** Every keeper already called, and every floor whose row is closed. Nobody is ever called twice.
2. **Why is this move safe?** The line is first-in, first-out. Children join behind their whole floor, so floors leave the line in order, and within a floor, west to east.
3. **When do I stop?** When the line is empty. Every keeper joined the line exactly once (sent for by her parent) and left it exactly once.

> “The line holds the rest of this floor, then the start of the next — never anything else.” That is the promise. Every chapter keeps it, then asks a different question of each floor.

**Reading LeetCode’s array form.** `[3, 9, 20, null, null, 15, 7]` lists the root, then each floor west to east. Every real node takes the next two entries as its left and right child; `null` means “no child here”, and trailing nulls are dropped. 9 takes `null, null` (a leaf); 20 takes `15, 7`. Every tree in this book, and every practice prompt, uses this form.

### When this pattern fires

The signal is that **the answer is organised by depth**, or that you want **the nearest of something**:

- **“Row by row”, “level by level”, “top to bottom”** — return the floors as lists (Chapter 1), alternate their direction (Chapter 2), or keep one node per floor (Chapter 3).
- **One answer per floor** — the average, sum, maximum, or first or last node of each floor (side quest 1).
- **Nearest, shallowest, fewest steps** — in a tree or any unweighted graph, the first time a breadth-first walk reaches something, it got there by the fewest edges (side quests 2 and 6).
- **Neighbours on the same floor** — link them, measure their spread, compare their parents (side quests 3–5).

**What it replaces.** The naive way to get floor d is to walk the whole tree once per floor and keep the nodes at depth d: O(n · h), which is **O(n²)** on a tree that is one long chain. The roll call is **one pass, O(n)**. (A depth-first walk that carries a `depth` argument and appends to `rows[depth]` is also O(n), and it is exactly how this lesson’s tests check every answer. Breadth-first wins when you want to **stop early** at the nearest thing, or when the problem is about **rings of distance** rather than rows.)

<h2 id="level-order">Chapter 1 · The landing line</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 1" title="The landing line" mood="dusk">

A warden stands on the landing outside the lamp room. Her name is Queue, and she keeps a line: whoever arrived first is called first, and anyone new joins the back. Nobody cuts in.

You call the lamp-room keeper. She answers, and sends a runner down both her stairs; the keepers at the foot of each come up and join the back of Queue's line. You call the front of the line, and that keeper sends for her own stair-keepers, who join behind.

It works. Names come out top to bottom, west to east. But Row, the scribe, is frowning over the logbook. He must start a new line when floor 1 ends and floor 2 begins, and nothing in Queue's line says who came from which floor.

Size, the bell-ringer, lifts her rope. *"Tell me when to count,"* she says.

</Scene>

**Problem: Binary Tree Level Order Traversal · queue + size snapshot**

Return the values floor by floor: `[[3], [9, 20], [15, 7]]` for the tree above. The queue already produces the right order; the only problem is **where one floor ends**.

Here is the observation that solves it. **At the moment the previous floor is finished, the line holds exactly the next floor and nobody else.** Everyone from the earlier floor has been called; nobody from the floor after has been sent for yet, because only this floor's keepers can send for them. So count the line **right then**, once:

```text
size = queue.size()        // exactly one floor is waiting
call exactly size keepers  // their children join behind, but are not counted
close the row              // now the line is exactly the next floor
```

The children who join during the floor stand **behind** the counted ones, and the count stops before them. That frozen number is the **size snapshot**. It is the whole trick, and it is why the code has two loops: the outer `while` runs once per floor, the inner `for` runs exactly `size` times.

<Trace title="Count the line once" steps={levelOrder} takeaway="Remember: snapshot size = queue.size() BEFORE the inner loop. Children join behind the snapshot; at the loop's exit the line is empty and there is one row per floor." />

**Why it works:** by induction on floors. Floor 0 is the only thing in the line at the start. If the line holds exactly floor d when the outer loop begins, the inner loop calls exactly those `size` keepers, west to east, and the only keepers who join are their children — which is all of floor d + 1, west to east, because each parent sends for left before right and parents are called west to east. So the next outer iteration starts with exactly floor d + 1.

**What stays true:** at the top of every outer iteration, the line holds **one whole floor, in left-to-right order, and nothing else**. Every floor above it is closed in the answer.

**Narrate the exit** (say this out loud in an interview; it is how you show the loop is finished): when `while` ends, the line is empty, every node was offered once and polled once, and `levels.size()` equals the number of floors (the tree's height, counted in nodes).

**Before you code, say:** O(n) time — every node joins and leaves the line once. O(w) extra space, where w is the widest floor. The invariant is the sentence above.

<Reveal q="Why not just loop while i < queue.size()?">

Because `queue.size()` changes inside the loop. It grows when children join and shrinks when you poll. On `[3, 9, 20, null, null, 15, 7]` a live bound calls 3 (the line becomes `[9, 20]`, size 2), then 9 (line `[20]`, size 1), then stops at `i = 2`. The rows come out `[[3, 9], [20, 15], [7]]`. The snapshot is the only value that still means “how many were on this floor”.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="I’ve seen a version that pushes a null marker after each floor. Is that wrong?">

It works, but it is more fragile. The idea: after each floor, put a marker in the line; when you poll the marker, the floor is over. Two traps: you must **re-add the marker only if the line is not empty**, or the loop spins forever re-adding it; and a `null` marker forces a `LinkedList`, because **`ArrayDeque` refuses null**. A dedicated marker object avoids the second trap:

```java id=levelOrderMarker
public List<List<Integer>> levelOrder(TreeNode root) {
    List<List<Integer>> levels = new ArrayList<>();
    if (root == null) return levels;
    final TreeNode END = new TreeNode();             // a marker object: ArrayDeque refuses null itself
    Queue<TreeNode> queue = new ArrayDeque<>();
    queue.offer(root);
    queue.offer(END);
    List<Integer> level = new ArrayList<>();
    while (!queue.isEmpty()) {
        TreeNode node = queue.poll();
        if (node == END) {                           // this floor is over
            levels.add(level);
            level = new ArrayList<>();
            if (!queue.isEmpty()) queue.offer(END);  // re-arm only if another floor waits
        } else {
            level.add(node.val);
            if (node.left != null) queue.offer(node.left);
            if (node.right != null) queue.offer(node.right);
        }
    }
    return levels;
}
```

The snapshot version needs no marker, no special case and no extra check. Prefer it; recognise this one.

</Reveal>

```java id=levelOrder
public List<List<Integer>> levelOrder(TreeNode root) {
    List<List<Integer>> levels = new ArrayList<>();
    if (root == null) return levels;                 // ArrayDeque refuses null: guard first
    Queue<TreeNode> queue = new ArrayDeque<>();
    queue.offer(root);
    while (!queue.isEmpty()) {
        int size = queue.size();                     // snapshot: exactly one floor is waiting
        List<Integer> level = new ArrayList<>(size);
        for (int i = 0; i < size; i++) {
            TreeNode node = queue.poll();
            level.add(node.val);
            if (node.left != null) queue.offer(node.left);    // the next floor joins the BACK
            if (node.right != null) queue.offer(node.right);
        }
        levels.add(level);                           // the bell is silent: close the row
    }
    return levels;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(w) extra space** for the line (plus the O(n) answer). The line holds the rest of one floor plus the start of the next, so it never exceeds two floors' worth, and on a complete tree it peaks at the bottom floor.

</HintLadder>

**How big is w?** For a chain (every room has one stair), w = 1 and the line never holds more than one keeper. For a **complete** tree, the bottom floor holds about **half of all the nodes**: a full tree with h floors has 2ʰ − 1 nodes, and its last floor alone has 2ʰ⁻¹ of them, which is (n + 1) / 2. So the roll call's space is O(1) on a chain but **O(n) on a bushy tree** — the opposite of a depth-first walk, whose stack is O(h): O(n) on a chain and O(log n) on a bushy tree. Keep that trade in your pocket; Chapter 3 spends it.

<Reveal q="Why ArrayDeque and not LinkedList? Both implement Queue.">

Three reasons. **It refuses nulls**, so `offer(null)` throws a `NullPointerException` at the exact line of the bug instead of letting a null wander through the line and crash (or silently produce a wrong answer) later. **It is faster**: one growable array, no node object per element; the Java documentation itself says it is likely to be faster than `LinkedList` when used as a queue. And **it does both jobs**: `offer`/`poll` as a queue, `push`/`pop` as a stack, `offerFirst`/`pollLast` as a deque.

A common habit with `LinkedList` is to offer both children unconditionally and skip nulls when polling. With the size snapshot, the nulls are counted as part of a floor. On the single-node tree `[1]` that produces a phantom last floor: `[[1], []]`. `ArrayDeque` would have thrown at the first `offer(null)`.

</Reveal>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 2" title="The sweeping lamp" mood="night">

The old lamp at Gullwick never turned all the way round. It swept east, then back west, then east again, and the keepers still keep its habit: the roll for floor 0 is written west to east, floor 1 east to west, floor 2 west to east again.

**Problem: Binary Tree Zigzag Level Order Traversal · level order + write direction**

Return the floors with alternating direction: `[[1], [3, 2], [4, 5, 6, 7]]` for the tree 1..7.

**The line never changes; only the pen does.** Run Chapter 1 exactly. The inner loop still calls floor d west to east. On a west-to-east floor, the i-th keeper called is written in slot `i`; on an east-to-west floor, in slot `size − 1 − i`. The snapshot does double duty: it ends the floor, **and** it tells you where the far end of the row is, before you have seen the whole floor.

**Why it works:** Chapter 1's invariant is untouched — the line still holds exactly one floor, west to east — because nothing about the line changed. The slot formula reverses a sequence of known length `size` in place: the first called goes last, the last called goes first.

**What stays true:** at the top of every outer iteration, the line holds one whole floor in left-to-right order, and `leftToRight` says how that floor will be written.

<Reveal q="Are there other correct ways?">

Yes, and they are worth recognising. **Reverse alternate rows:** build each row west to east, then `Collections.reverse` it on odd floors; still O(n) in total. **Add to the front:** collect the row in a `LinkedList<Integer>` (or `ArrayDeque`) with `addFirst` on odd floors. **Two stacks:** pop floor d from one stack while pushing children into another — left-then-right on even floors, right-then-left on odd ones. That flip is correct only because a **stack** reverses the next floor on its own; with a queue, the same flip scrambles it (catch the bug below).

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.zigzag}>

```java id=zigzagLevelOrder
public List<List<Integer>> zigzagLevelOrder(TreeNode root) {
    List<List<Integer>> levels = new ArrayList<>();
    if (root == null) return levels;
    Queue<TreeNode> queue = new ArrayDeque<>();
    queue.offer(root);
    boolean leftToRight = true;
    while (!queue.isEmpty()) {
        int size = queue.size();
        Integer[] row = new Integer[size];            // the snapshot also sizes the row
        for (int i = 0; i < size; i++) {
            TreeNode node = queue.poll();
            row[leftToRight ? i : size - 1 - i] = node.val;   // only the pen changes direction
            if (node.left != null) queue.offer(node.left);     // the line never does
            if (node.right != null) queue.offer(node.right);
        }
        levels.add(Arrays.asList(row));
        leftToRight = !leftToRight;
    }
    return levels;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(w) extra space.** Each value is written once, straight into its final slot.

</HintLadder>

```java fragment
// tree 1..7: floor 1 comes out [3, 2] (correct), floor 2 comes out [6, 7, 4, 5] (scrambled)
```

Floor 1 is right, which is what makes this bug convincing. But floor 1 was *called* 3, then 2, so 3's children joined the line before 2's. [Solve Binary Tree Zigzag Level Order Traversal](https://leetcode.com/problems/binary-tree-zigzag-level-order-traversal/).

<h2 id="right-side-view">Chapter 3 · The view from the sea</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 3" title="The view from the sea" mood="night">

A schooner lies at anchor east of the rock. From her deck the captain can see exactly one window on each floor: the easternmost lit one. Every other window on that floor is hidden behind it.

**Problem: Binary Tree Right Side View · last node of each level (or right-first DFS)**

The view is **one node per floor: the last one on that floor**, wherever it hangs. “Right side” does not mean “right children”.

<Figure caption="Top: LeetCode’s example [1, 2, 3, null, 5, null, 4]; the ship sees 1, 3, 4. Bottom: [1, 2, 3, 4]; the ship sees 1, 3 and then 4, a left child, because nothing on floor 2 stands east of it.">
  <Tree {...figures.rightView} />
  <Tree {...figures.leftHidden} />
</Figure>

**Way 1, the roll call:** run Chapter 1 and record the node when `i == size − 1`. The snapshot is what makes “last on this floor” knowable at the moment you call her.

**Way 2, the climb:** walk depth-first, **right stair before left**, carrying the depth. The first room you reach on each new floor is the easternmost one, because everything east of it was explored first. You know a floor is new when `depth == view.size()`: the view has one entry per floor seen so far.

<Trace title="Which window?" steps={rightView} takeaway="Remember: the view is the LAST node of each floor (i == size − 1), or the FIRST node reached at each new depth when you climb right-first." />

**Why it works:** the roll call calls each floor west to east, so the last one called has nobody east of it. The right-first climb reaches floor d for the first time along the path that turns east at every chance; any node on floor d east of the one it reaches would have been reached earlier.

**What stays true:** roll call — `view` holds the last node of every closed floor. Climb — `view.size()` is the number of floors already seen, and `view[d]` is the easternmost node on floor d.

<HintLadder hints={hints.rightView}>

```java id=rightSideView
public List<Integer> rightSideView(TreeNode root) {
    List<Integer> view = new ArrayList<>();
    if (root == null) return view;
    Queue<TreeNode> queue = new ArrayDeque<>();
    queue.offer(root);
    while (!queue.isEmpty()) {
        int size = queue.size();
        for (int i = 0; i < size; i++) {
            TreeNode node = queue.poll();
            if (i == size - 1) view.add(node.val);    // last called on this floor: nobody east of her
            if (node.left != null) queue.offer(node.left);
            if (node.right != null) queue.offer(node.right);
        }
    }
    return view;
}
```

```java id=rightSideViewDfs
public List<Integer> rightSideView(TreeNode root) {
    List<Integer> view = new ArrayList<>();
    climb(root, 0, view);
    return view;
}

private void climb(TreeNode node, int depth, List<Integer> view) {
    if (node == null) return;
    if (depth == view.size()) view.add(node.val);     // first node reached on a new floor
    climb(node.right, depth + 1, view);               // east stair first
    climb(node.left, depth + 1, view);
}
```

**Both are O(n) time.** The roll call uses **O(w)** extra space; the climb uses **O(h)** stack.

</HintLadder>

### Which walk? The trade, in one table

| | Roll call (BFS, queue) | Climb (DFS, recursion or stack) |
|---|---|---|
| Visits nodes | floor by floor, west to east | one path at a time, as deep as possible |
| Extra space | O(w): up to about n / 2 on a complete tree | O(h): up to n on a chain, about log₂ n when balanced |
| Natural for | rows, per-floor answers, **nearest / fewest edges**, stopping early | answers that come **up from subtrees**: height, balance, diameter, path sums, LCA |
| Right side view | `i == size − 1` | right first, `depth == view.size()` |

**When the floor order is the point** — rows, “nearest”, “fewest steps”, per-floor aggregates — reach for the roll call; it gets them without any bookkeeping. **When the answer is built from subtrees**, climb. When both work, as here, **pick by shape**: a wide, shallow tree favours the climb's O(h); a long chain favours the roll call's O(w) (and a very deep recursion risks a `StackOverflowError` in Java). Say that sentence in the interview — it is what the follow-up is fishing for.

</Reveal>

<h2 id="average-levels">Side quest 1 · The oil tally</h2>

**The new rule:** each keeper reports the oil left in her lamp. Don't list the floors; report **the average oil per floor**.

**The reasoning:** the same roll call, but the row becomes **two numbers**: a running sum and the snapshot `size`. When the bell falls silent, the floor's average is `sum / size`. This is the template for every **per-floor aggregate** — maximum, minimum, sum, count, first, last: replace the list with whatever the floor needs.

<Reveal q="LeetCode allows values up to 2³¹ − 1. What goes wrong with an int sum and sum / size?">

Two separate bugs. **Overflow:** on `[2147483647, 2147483647, 2147483647]`, floor 1's two values add to 4,294,967,294, which wraps to −2 in an `int`, and the “average” comes out −1.0 instead of 2147483647.0. Use a `long` sum: a floor holds at most 10⁴ values here, so a `long` can't overflow. **Integer division:** with `long sum`, `sum / size` truncates before it becomes a double; on `[3, 9, 20, null, null, 15, 7]` floor 1 gives 14.0 instead of 14.5. Cast first: `(double) sum / size`.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.average}>

```java id=averageOfLevels
public List<Double> averageOfLevels(TreeNode root) {
    List<Double> averages = new ArrayList<>();
    if (root == null) return averages;
    Queue<TreeNode> queue = new ArrayDeque<>();
    queue.offer(root);
    while (!queue.isEmpty()) {
        int size = queue.size();
        long sum = 0;                                // two values of 2^31 - 1 already overflow an int
        for (int i = 0; i < size; i++) {
            TreeNode node = queue.poll();
            sum += node.val;
            if (node.left != null) queue.offer(node.left);
            if (node.right != null) queue.offer(node.right);
        }
        averages.add((double) sum / size);           // divide as a double, not as a long
    }
    return averages;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(w) extra space.** LeetCode's example `[3, 9, 20, null, null, 15, 7]` gives `[3.0, 14.5, 11.0]`.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** Find Largest Value in Each Tree Row — which line changes, and what must the running value start at so a floor of all-negative values still works? (Start at `Integer.MIN_VALUE`, or at the floor's first value.) [Solve Average of Levels in Binary Tree](https://leetcode.com/problems/average-of-levels-in-binary-tree/).

<h2 id="min-depth">Side quest 2 · The nearest door</h2>

**The new rule:** fog is rolling in. Find the **nearest way out**: the fewest rooms on a path from the lamp room down to a room with no stairs down — a leaf, which has a door to the rocks. Count the rooms, including both ends.

**The reasoning:** floors arrive in order of depth, so **the first leaf you call is on the shallowest floor that has one**. Stop right there. Nothing deeper is ever touched.

<Figure caption="[1, 2, 3, null, null, null, 4, null, 5, null, 6]. The roll call calls 1, then 2 — a leaf on floor 1 — and returns 2. 3 is still waiting in the line and is never called; 4, 5 and 6 are never even sent for. A recursion that takes the smaller of both sides has to walk the whole chain first.">
  <Tree {...figures.minDepth} />
</Figure>

**Why it beats the climb on lopsided trees:** a recursive minimum must visit **every** node, because it can't know the right subtree is deeper without exploring it: always O(n). The roll call visits only the floors above the nearest leaf, plus part of that floor. Both are O(n) in the worst case (a perfectly balanced tree has its leaves at the bottom), but on a tree with a leaf near the top and a 100,000-room chain below, the roll call makes a few calls and the climb makes 100,000. The chain is also deep enough that a recursive walk risks a `StackOverflowError` on a default Java stack.

<Reveal q="The one-line recursion 1 + min(minDepth(left), minDepth(right)) — what does it return on [1, 2]?">

**1**, but the answer is **2**. The root has a left child, so it is not a leaf; its missing right child returns 0, and `min` picks that 0. **A null child is not a way out.** The correct recursion only takes the minimum when both children exist:

```java id=minDepthDfs
public int minDepth(TreeNode root) {
    if (root == null) return 0;
    if (root.left == null) return 1 + minDepth(root.right);   // one stair only: follow it
    if (root.right == null) return 1 + minDepth(root.left);
    return 1 + Math.min(minDepth(root.left), minDepth(root.right));
}
```

Correct, but it visits every node every time.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.minDepth}>

```java id=minDepth
public int minDepth(TreeNode root) {
    if (root == null) return 0;
    Queue<TreeNode> queue = new ArrayDeque<>();
    queue.offer(root);
    int depth = 1;                                   // floor 0 holds paths of 1 room
    while (!queue.isEmpty()) {
        int size = queue.size();
        for (int i = 0; i < size; i++) {
            TreeNode node = queue.poll();
            if (node.left == null && node.right == null) return depth;   // first leaf = nearest
            if (node.left != null) queue.offer(node.left);
            if (node.right != null) queue.offer(node.right);
        }
        depth++;
    }
    return depth;                                    // unreachable: every non-empty tree has a leaf
}
```

**O(n) time in the worst case, O(w) extra space** — and it stops at the first leaf.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** this is the first time the roll call answers a **“nearest”** question. Why is the first leaf called guaranteed to be the nearest, and which property of the line does that rely on? (Floors leave the line in order of depth, so nothing shallower can still be waiting.) The same sentence proves breadth-first search finds shortest paths in any unweighted graph. [Solve Minimum Depth of Binary Tree](https://leetcode.com/problems/minimum-depth-of-binary-tree/).

<h2 id="max-width">Side quest 3 · Counting dark windows</h2>

**The new rule:** the harbour master measures each floor from its westernmost lit window to its easternmost, **counting the dark frames in between** — the frames a complete tower would have filled. Report the widest floor.

**The reasoning:** the number of keepers on a floor is not its width. Seat, the mason, numbers frames as if the tower were complete: the lamp room is seat 0, and the stairs from seat s lead to seats **2s + 1** (left) and **2s + 2** (right). A floor's width is **last seat − first seat + 1**, and the roll call hands you the first and last keeper of every floor.

<Figure caption="LeetCode’s example [1, 3, 2, 5, 3, null, 9], with seats renumbered from 0 on each floor. Floor 2 has keepers in seats 0, 1 and 3; seat 2 is a dark frame. Width 3 − 0 + 1 = 4, although only 3 keepers live there.">
  <Tree {...figures.width} />
</Figure>

**Renumber every floor.** Seats double on every floor: by floor 31 they no longer fit in an `int`, by floor 63 not in a `long`, and a chain of 3,000 rooms (LeetCode's limit) would need 3,000-bit numbers. Subtract the floor's first seat from every seat on that floor before computing children. Every number you hold is then a real distance from the floor's west end, which is never more than the answer, and LeetCode promises the answer fits in a 32-bit `int`. Keep seats in a `long` anyway: the **children** of a seat near 2³¹ are near 2³².

<Reveal q="Plenty of Java solutions skip the renumbering and still pass. Why?">

Luck in the arithmetic. Java's `int` and `long` wrap around on overflow, and wrapped numbers still **subtract** correctly as long as the true difference fits: last − first + 1 comes out right even when both seats are garbage. Compare two wrapped seats with `<`, or port the code to a language where overflow is an error or numbers grow without bound, and it fails or crawls. Renumbering makes the code correct by construction instead of by accident.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.width}>

```java id=widthOfBinaryTree
static class Seat {                                  // a keeper and her frame number on this floor
    final TreeNode node;
    final long pos;
    Seat(TreeNode node, long pos) { this.node = node; this.pos = pos; }
}

public int widthOfBinaryTree(TreeNode root) {
    if (root == null) return 0;
    Queue<Seat> queue = new ArrayDeque<>();
    queue.offer(new Seat(root, 0));
    long best = 0;
    while (!queue.isEmpty()) {
        int size = queue.size();
        long first = queue.peek().pos;               // westernmost seat on this floor
        long last = 0;
        for (int i = 0; i < size; i++) {
            Seat s = queue.poll();
            long pos = s.pos - first;                // renumber: the floor starts at seat 0
            last = pos;
            if (s.node.left != null) queue.offer(new Seat(s.node.left, 2 * pos + 1));
            if (s.node.right != null) queue.offer(new Seat(s.node.right, 2 * pos + 2));
        }
        best = Math.max(best, last + 1);             // first is 0 after renumbering
    }
    return (int) best;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(w) extra space.** LeetCode's second example, `[1, 3, 2, 5, null, null, 9, 6, null, 7]`, has width 7 on its bottom floor.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** a heap stores a complete tree in an array with exactly these seat numbers. Why does Check Completeness of a Binary Tree reduce to “after the first missing seat in the roll call, no keeper may appear”? [Solve Maximum Width of Binary Tree](https://leetcode.com/problems/maximum-width-of-binary-tree/).

<h2 id="next-pointers">Side quest 4 · Ropes between windows</h2>

**The new rule:** Next, the rope-maker, strings a rope from every window to the next window east on the same floor; the easternmost window's rope hangs loose (`null`). The tower is **perfect** — every floor full. And the guild has run out of landing space: **no line at all, O(1) extra space.**

**The reasoning:** with a line, this is Chapter 1 plus one rope per call: link each node to the one called after it, unless it is the last on its floor. That costs O(w) for the line. To drop the line, notice what the ropes are: **a finished floor is a linked list**. Stand on floor d, already roped, and walk it east along its own ropes. From each keeper you can reach both of her children, and, through her rope, the children of her eastern neighbour. That is every pair of neighbours on floor d + 1:

- **siblings:** `head.left.next = head.right`;
- **across the gap between two parents:** `head.right.next = head.next.left`.

Then step down to the floor's westernmost room, `leftmost.left`, and repeat.

<Figure caption="The tree 1..7 with its ropes (dashed). Walking floor 1 along 2 → 3, keeper 2 ropes 4 → 5 (siblings) and, through her own rope to 3, ropes 5 → 6 (different parents). 3 ropes 6 → 7. The east end of every floor stays null.">
  <Tree {...figures.next} />
</Figure>

The version with a line works on **any** tree, perfect or not:

```java id=connectQueue
static class Node {
    int val;
    Node left, right, next;
    Node(int val) { this.val = val; }
}

public Node connect(Node root) {
    if (root == null) return null;
    Queue<Node> queue = new ArrayDeque<>();
    queue.offer(root);
    while (!queue.isEmpty()) {
        int size = queue.size();
        for (int i = 0; i < size; i++) {
            Node node = queue.poll();
            if (i < size - 1) node.next = queue.peek();   // the next one called is her eastern neighbour
            if (node.left != null) queue.offer(node.left);
            if (node.right != null) queue.offer(node.right);
        }
    }
    return root;
}
```

<HintLadder hints={hints.connect}>

```java id=connect
static class Node {
    int val;
    Node left, right, next;
    Node(int val) { this.val = val; }
}

public Node connect(Node root) {
    Node leftmost = root;
    while (leftmost != null && leftmost.left != null) {          // a floor exists below this one
        for (Node head = leftmost; head != null; head = head.next) {   // walk this floor by its ropes
            head.left.next = head.right;                          // siblings
            if (head.next != null) head.right.next = head.next.left;   // across the gap between parents
        }
        leftmost = leftmost.left;                                 // step down to the next floor
    }
    return root;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) extra space**: three pointers, no line, no recursion.

</HintLadder>

<Reveal q="Transfer test: the tower is no longer perfect (Populating Next Right Pointers II). What breaks, and what fixes it?">

`head.left` may be missing, the gap between children can span several parents, and the next floor need not start at `leftmost.left`. Fix: while walking floor d by its ropes, **build floor d + 1 as a linked list** with a dummy head and a tail pointer, appending each child that exists. The dummy's `next` is then the next floor's westernmost room. Still O(1) extra space:

```java id=connectAny
static class Node {
    int val;
    Node left, right, next;
    Node(int val) { this.val = val; }
}

public Node connect(Node root) {
    Node leftmost = root;
    while (leftmost != null) {
        Node dummy = new Node(0), tail = dummy;      // the floor below, built as a linked list
        for (Node head = leftmost; head != null; head = head.next) {
            if (head.left != null) { tail.next = head.left; tail = tail.next; }
            if (head.right != null) { tail.next = head.right; tail = tail.next; }
        }
        leftmost = dummy.next;                       // westernmost room of the floor below
    }
    return root;
}
```

The dummy-head trick is straight out of the linked-list toolbox: it removes the “is this the first one?” special case.

</Reveal>

**Catch the bug:** forgetting the rope across the gap. With only `head.left.next = head.right`, keeper 5 in the tree 1..7 is left pointing at null instead of 6. [Solve Populating Next Right Pointers in Each Node](https://leetcode.com/problems/populating-next-right-pointers-in-each-node/).

```java id=isCousins
public boolean isCousins(TreeNode root, int x, int y) {
    if (root == null) return false;
    Queue<TreeNode> queue = new ArrayDeque<>();
    queue.offer(root);
    while (!queue.isEmpty()) {
        int size = queue.size();
        boolean foundX = false, foundY = false;
        for (int i = 0; i < size; i++) {
            TreeNode node = queue.poll();
            if (node.val == x) foundX = true;
            if (node.val == y) foundY = true;
            if (node.left != null && node.right != null
                    && ((node.left.val == x && node.right.val == y) || (node.left.val == y && node.right.val == x)))
                return false;                        // same parent: siblings, not cousins
            if (node.left != null) queue.offer(node.left);
            if (node.right != null) queue.offer(node.right);
        }
        if (foundX && foundY) return true;           // same floor, and the sibling check never fired
        if (foundX || foundY) return false;          // one was here, the other wasn't
    }
    return false;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(w) extra space.** It can stop at the first floor holding either keeper.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** checking only the floor. Compare depths alone and the siblings 2 and 3 in `[1, 2, 3]` pass as cousins. **Transfer test:** Cousins in Binary Tree II replaces every value with the sum of its cousins' values. Which two per-floor numbers do you need? (The floor's total, and, for each parent, the sum of her own children: cousins' sum = floor total − siblings' total.) <h2 id="distance-k">Side quest 6 · The fog bell</h2>

**The new rule:** the fog bell rings in room `target`. The sound climbs **up or down** one stair per beat. Which keepers hear it on exactly beat `k`?

**The reasoning:** stairs in a tree only lead down; the sound also goes **up**, and from a parent down into a different branch. So first give every room a way up: Parent walks the tower once and chalks each room's parent beside its door. Now every room has up to three neighbours — left, right, parent — and the tower is an **undirected graph**. Then run the roll call **from the target**, one ring per beat. Because the sound can come straight back the way it came, keep a **visited set**: a room hears the bell once, on its first beat.

<Figure caption="LeetCode’s example [3, 5, 1, 6, 2, 0, 8, null, null, 7, 4], target 5 (purple), k = 2. Beat 1 reaches 6, 2 and — up the dashed stair — 3. Beat 2 reaches 7, 4 and 1. Answer [7, 4, 1], in any order.">
  <Tree {...figures.distanceK} />
</Figure>

**Narrate the exit:** after `k` rings, the loop stops with the line holding **exactly the keepers at distance k** — no more filtering needed. If the line empties first, nobody is that far away and the answer is empty.

<HintLadder hints={hints.distanceK}>

```java id=distanceK
public List<Integer> distanceK(TreeNode root, TreeNode target, int k) {
    Map<TreeNode, TreeNode> parent = new HashMap<>();
    Queue<TreeNode> queue = new ArrayDeque<>();
    queue.offer(root);
    while (!queue.isEmpty()) {                       // pass 1: chalk every room's parent
        TreeNode node = queue.poll();
        if (node.left != null) { parent.put(node.left, node); queue.offer(node.left); }
        if (node.right != null) { parent.put(node.right, node); queue.offer(node.right); }
    }
    Set<TreeNode> heard = new HashSet<>();
    heard.add(target);
    queue.offer(target);
    for (int beat = 0; beat < k && !queue.isEmpty(); beat++) {   // pass 2: one ring per beat
        int size = queue.size();
        for (int i = 0; i < size; i++) {
            TreeNode node = queue.poll();
            for (TreeNode next : new TreeNode[]{node.left, node.right, parent.get(node)})
                if (next != null && heard.add(next)) queue.offer(next);   // first time only
        }
    }
    List<Integer> ring = new ArrayList<>();
    for (TreeNode node : queue) ring.add(node.val);  // the line is exactly ring k
    return ring;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(n) extra space** for the parent map, the visited set and the line.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** forget the visited set, and the sound bounces back. In the example, beat 2 would then also reach 5 three times — from 6, from 2 and from 3 — and return `[5, 7, 4, 5, 5, 1]`. **This is the bridge to graphs:** a parent map turned a tree into a graph, and the moment a walk can return the way it came, it needs a visited set. **Transfer test:** Amount of Time for Binary Tree to Be Infected asks how many beats until **every** room hears it. What do you return instead of the last ring? (The number of the last beat that reached anyone new: count the rings you process, minus one for the target's own ring.) <h2 id="choose-the-tool">When the roll call is the wrong drill</h2>

<Scene title="The leaning tower" mood="storm">

A storm comes in from the north, and by morning the builders are worried. Is the tower balanced, they ask: at every room, do the left and right stairs go down to depths that differ by at most one floor?

You start the roll call out of habit. Floor 0, one keeper. Floor 1, two. Floor 2, three. You stop. The roll tells you how many keepers stand on each floor, but not which stair they came down, and the question is about every single room.

The Keeper is already at the bottom of the left stair, climbing back up. *"Some questions,"* she says, *"are answered from below. Each room can only tell you how deep it goes after its stairs have told it first."*

</Scene>

The roll call is the right tool when **the answer is organised by floor, or by distance from a start**. It is the wrong tool when:

- **The answer at a node is built from its subtrees.** Height, balance, diameter, “maximum path sum”, lowest common ancestor, subtree sums: each needs answers to come **up** from the children. A post-order climb (depth-first) does it in one O(n) pass; floors give you nothing to combine.
- **The question is about root-to-leaf paths.** Path Sum, “all root-to-leaf paths”: carry the path down one branch at a time with a climb; a floor mixes many unrelated paths.
- **The tree is a binary search tree and order matters.** An in-order climb visits values in sorted order; the roll call does not.
- **The tree is very wide and shallow, and order doesn't matter.** Both walks are O(n) time, but the roll call holds a whole floor (up to about n / 2 nodes); a climb holds one path.

<Reveal q="Maximum Depth of Binary Tree. Roll call or climb?">

Both are correct and O(n). The climb is one line, `1 + max(depth(left), depth(right))`, with O(h) stack. The roll call counts floors with the snapshot loop and O(w) memory. Interviewers accept either; name the space trade, and pick the roll call if the tree may be a 100,000-room chain (no deep recursion).

</Reveal>

<h2 id="exit-ticket">The lamplighter’s exam · Build a solution with the labels removed</h2>

<Scene title="No chapter titles" mood="dawn">

On the morning of the exam, the Keeper hands you a page with no chapter names and no hints — three problems, a pen, and the three questions carved over the landing door.

*"Pick one,"* she says. *"Before you write a line, tell me what the line holds at the top of each loop, why it can't mix two floors, and what is left in it when you stop."*

</Scene>

Choose one. Keep the tutorial code closed. Say the time, the space and the invariant before coding.

1. **The brightest window.** Return the largest value on each floor of a binary tree. Test an empty tree, a single node, a floor of all-negative values, and a chain.
2. **The lowest corner.** Return the value of the westernmost node on the deepest floor. Test a chain going east (the answer is the bottom room, not a left child), and a tree whose deepest floor has one node.
3. **The fleet's columns.** Put the root at row 0, column 0; a left child is one row down and one column west, a right child one row down and one column east. Return the values column by column, west to east; within a column, top to bottom; and when two nodes share both row and column, smaller value first.

<Reveal q="I’ve written and tested it. What should my reasoning contain?">

**The brightest window:** side quest 1 with `max` instead of a sum. Start each floor at `Integer.MIN_VALUE` (or at its first value), never at 0, or a floor of negatives reports 0.

**The lowest corner:** record the node at `i == 0` on every floor; the last recorded value wins. Or call each floor **east to west** (offer right before left) with no snapshot at all: the very last node polled is the answer. Either way, “westernmost” means first on its floor, not “a left child”.

**The fleet's columns:** carry `(node, row, col)` in the line. The roll call delivers rows in order, so within a column nodes arrive top to bottom — but two nodes in the same row and column can arrive in either value order, so each column must still be **sorted by (row, value)**. Group by column in a map, then output columns from the smallest to the largest. (LeetCode's premium Binary Tree Vertical Order Traversal changes only the tie rule: same row and column keeps west-to-east order, which the roll call already gives for free, so no sort is needed there.)

Now explain why a slightly wrong version fails one of your tests — a running max starting at 0, “westernmost” read as “left child”, or a column not sorted by value. That counterexample is stronger evidence of understanding than a memorised template.

</Reveal>

Passing this once is a useful checkpoint. Rebuild one of these on another day, with a changed input and without the headings choosing the pattern for you.

<h2 id="cheat-sheet">The Keeper’s logbook</h2>

| When you see… | Start with… | The promise to keep |
|---|---|---|
| Return the tree row by row | `ArrayDeque`; `size = queue.size()` before the inner loop | The line holds exactly one floor at the top of each iteration |
| Rows alternate direction | Same loop; write into slot `i` or `size − 1 − i` | The line stays left to right; only the pen flips |
| What you see from the right (or left) | `i == size − 1` (or `i == 0`); or climb right-first with `depth == view.size()` | One entry per floor: the last (or first) on it |
| An average, sum, max per row | Replace the row list with an accumulator (`long` sums, `double` division) | The aggregate covers exactly the snapshot's nodes |
| Nearest leaf, fewest steps, shallowest anything | Floor by floor; return at the first match | Floors leave the line in order of depth |
| Width counting gaps | Carry seats; children at 2s + 1, 2s + 2; renumber each floor | Width = last seat − first seat + 1 |
| Link each node to its right neighbour, O(1) space | Walk floor d by its `next` links; link floor d + 1 (dummy head if not perfect) | A finished floor is a linked list |
| Same depth, different parents | Catch siblings at the parent; then “both on this floor?” | A floor with only one of them ends the search |
| Everything K steps from a node | Parent map, then rings from the target with a visited set | After k rings the line is exactly the answer |
| Height, balance, diameter, path sums | A climb (DFS) that returns answers upward | — wrong tool for the roll call |

Count the memory: the roll call is O(w), which is about n / 2 on a complete tree; a climb is O(h), which is n on a chain. The parent map and visited set of side quest 6 are O(n). Only the roped walk of side quest 4 is O(1).

## Five bugs the Keeper keeps in her pocket

1. **The live bound.** `for (int i = 0; i < queue.size(); i++)` re-reads a size that changes inside the loop, and floors bleed into each other. Snapshot `size` first.
2. **Nulls in the line.** `ArrayDeque.offer(null)` throws; a `LinkedList` accepts it and produces phantom floors or a later crash. Guard the root, and offer only non-null children.
3. **Flipping the line instead of the pen.** Reordering how children join scrambles every later floor. Choose the direction only when writing.
4. **A null child is not a leaf.** In minimum depth, a node with one child is not a way out; `min(left, right)` with a 0 from the missing side returns too early.
5. **Walking back the way you came.** Once a tree has parent links, it is a graph: without a visited set, the walk bounces between a node and its parent.
