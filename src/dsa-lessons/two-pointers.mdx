<h2 id="prologue">Prologue · The promise</h2>

<Scene chapter="Prologue" title="Two lanterns" mood="dawn">

Arraytown is a town of one long street. Every door carries a number, and the first door is number 0 — the founders were programmers, and nobody has had the heart to fix it.

On your first morning at the Pointer Guild, the Keeper hands you two brass lanterns. *"Each lantern marks one position,"* she says. *"You will never see the whole street at once. You see what your lanterns light — and you remember what you have promised."*

"Promised?"

*"Before a lantern moves, you answer three questions out loud. If you can't answer them, you don't move."* She taps the wall, where three lines are carved into the stone.

</Scene>

A **pointer** is just a position you are tracking. Give each one a job. Then, before every move, ask the Keeper's three questions:

1. **What is already settled?** A matched pair, a kept prefix, a sorted suffix?
2. **Why is this move safe?** Which answers become impossible, or which value can be placed for good?
3. **What makes me stop?** No pair left, no unread values, or an empty unknown region?

The promise that survives every move is your **invariant**. You don't need formal language for it. “Everything before Write is the correct answer so far” is a perfectly good promise.

<Figure caption="The promise, drawn. Everything outside the lanterns is settled. All remaining work lives between them — and every move makes that stretch smaller.">
  <ArrayStrip values={[2, 3, 5, 8, 11, 15, 21]} pointers={[{ at: 2, label: 'LEFT' }, { at: 5, label: 'RIGHT' }]} tones={{ 0: 'done', 1: 'done', 6: 'done' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 1, label: 'settled', tone: 'green' }, { from: 2, to: 5, label: 'still in play', tone: 'gold' }, { from: 6, to: 6, label: 'settled', tone: 'green' }]} />
</Figure>

That is the whole secret of this pattern. Brute force checks every pair — n² of them. A pointer that moves only when it can **prove** something is finished visits each position once. The rest of this book is eleven different ways to make that proof.

<h2 id="mirror-check">Chapter 1 · The folded word</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 1" title="The folded word" mood="dawn">

Someone has painted a message across the town gate, one character per plank: **A man, a plan, a canal: Panama.** The mayor wants to know whether it reads the same backwards. Punctuation and capital letters don't count, she says. Digits do.

The twins take opposite ends of the gate. Left lifts her lantern on the first *A*; Right lifts his on the last *a*. "Same," they call, and step inward.

Then Left's light falls on a space. Two steps later, a comma. She hesitates. Is that a mismatch — or simply none of her business?

</Scene>

**Problem: Valid Palindrome · Opposite ends, with skipping**

Imagine folding the word in half. Matching letters should land on each other. Compare the two ends, then move inward. For this problem, ignore punctuation and letter case; digits still count.

<Figure caption="Folding racecar. Everything outside the twins has already matched; one unchecked pair remains, and then the middle letter needs no partner.">
  <ArrayStrip values={['r', 'a', 'c', 'e', 'c', 'a', 'r']} pointers={[{ at: 2, label: 'LEFT' }, { at: 4, label: 'RIGHT' }]} tones={{ 0: 'done', 1: 'done', 5: 'done', 6: 'done', 3: 'ghost' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 1, label: 'matched', tone: 'green' }, { from: 5, to: 6, label: 'matched', tone: 'green' }]} />
</Figure>

Try the awkward bit first: what should happen when a lantern lands on punctuation?

<Trace title="Meet in the middle" steps={palindrome} takeaway="Remember: skip characters that do not count, compare the ones that do, then move both ends. A real mismatch ends the search." />

**Why it works:** everything outside the two pointers has already matched or been safely ignored. Once the pointers meet or cross, there are no unchecked pairs left. A single middle character needs no partner.

**Catch the bug:** skip punctuation *before* comparing. Keep checking `left < right` while skipping, too; a string made entirely of punctuation can make the pointers meet.

<HintLadder hints={hints.palindrome}>

```java id=isPalindrome
public boolean isPalindrome(String s) {
    int left = 0, right = s.length() - 1;
    while (left < right) {
        while (left < right && !Character.isLetterOrDigit(s.charAt(left))) left++;
        while (left < right && !Character.isLetterOrDigit(s.charAt(right))) right--;
        if (Character.toLowerCase(s.charAt(left)) !=
            Character.toLowerCase(s.charAt(right))) return false;
        left++;
        right--;
    }
    return true;
}
```

This fits the problem's ASCII input. Each position is visited at most once: **O(n) time, O(1) extra space**. Nothing needs to be sorted or copied.

</HintLadder>

**Try on your own:** `".,"`, `"0P"`, and `"A man, a plan, a canal: Panama"`. Predict the results before tracing. [Solve Valid Palindrome](https://leetcode.com/problems/valid-palindrome/).

<h2 id="find-a-pair">Chapter 2 · Exact change</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 2" title="Exact change">

The market stalls on Ledger Lane are arranged by price — cheapest at the west gate, dearest at the east. A traveller needs two items that cost **exactly 18 coins** together. Not 17. Not 19.

Left starts at the cheapest stall: 2 coins. Right starts at the dearest: 15. Together, 17. One coin short.

"Should I move?" Right asks.

*"Think,"* says the Keeper. *"Fifteen is the most expensive thing in this market. If even the dearest stall can't lift 2 up to 18 — who could?"*

Nobody could. And so Left's stall isn't just wrong for *this* pair. It is finished, for every pair it could ever make.

</Scene>

**Problem: Two Sum II · Opposite ends, with elimination**

Now the two ends don't need to match. They need to **add up to a target**. Sorted order lets you rule out a whole set of pairs with one move.

<Figure caption="One move, a whole column of pairs gone. 2 + 15 = 17 is too small, and 15 was 2's best possible partner — so 2 is out for good. Next, 7 + 15 = 22 is too big; 7 was 15's smallest possible partner, so 15 is out. Then 7 + 11 = 18.">
  <ArrayStrip values={[2, 7, 11, 15]} pointers={[{ at: 1, label: 'LEFT' }, { at: 2, label: 'RIGHT' }]} tones={{ 0: 'out', 3: 'out', 1: 'hot', 2: 'hot' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 0, label: 'too small for anyone', tone: 'dim' }, { from: 3, to: 3, label: 'too big for anyone', tone: 'dim' }]} />
</Figure>

Try **Find a pair** in the [practice yard](#playground). Its four cases include negative numbers, duplicates, and a search with no answer.

**Too small? Move left.** Even its biggest remaining partner wasn't enough. That left number cannot work with anyone left.

**Too big? Move right.** Even its smallest remaining partner was too big. That right number cannot work with anyone left.

**Exactly right? Stop.** You have a pair at two different positions. Equal values are allowed: `[2, 2]` can make 4.

> “I only throw away a number once I know it can't work with anyone left.”

**What stays true:** if a valid pair still exists, it is inside the current boundaries.

<Reveal q="Would the same moves work on [7, 2, 15, 11]? What about negative numbers?">

The unsorted array breaks the reasoning: moving a pointer no longer reliably changes the sum in the direction you need. Sorting can help, but adds work and changes positions. For an unsorted problem asking for original indexes, a hash map is another option.

Negative numbers are fine when the array is sorted. In `[-8, -3, 2, 6]`, target −1, the ends add to −2. Move left: −8 still cannot reach the target with anyone left.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.pair}>

```java id=twoSum
public int[] twoSum(int[] nums, int target) {
    int left = 0, right = nums.length - 1;
    while (left < right) {
        long sum = (long) nums[left] + nums[right];
        if (sum == target) return new int[]{left + 1, right + 1};
        if (sum < target) left++;
        else right--;
    }
    return new int[]{-1, -1};
}
```

Two Sum II returns **1-based positions**, hence the `+ 1`. Casting before addition prevents integer overflow. At most n − 1 inward moves: **O(n) time, O(1) extra space** for already-sorted input.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** `left <= right` would allow `[3]` to “make” 6 by using the same element twice. For a pair, use `left < right`. [Solve Two Sum II](https://leetcode.com/problems/two-sum-ii-input-array-is-sorted/).

<h2 id="build-a-triplet">Chapter 3 · Three in a boat</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 3" title="Three in a boat" mood="night">

The canal ferry only floats level if its three passengers' weights — heavy ones counted as positive, the balloon-sellers counted as negative — add up to exactly **zero**. The ferryman keeps a sorted list of tonight's passengers and has one wish: every *different* balanced trio, written down once.

"Three is a crowd," Left mutters. "We're a pair."

*"Then let someone sit down first,"* says the Keeper. *"Seat one passenger who weighs −2. Now what do the other two need to add up to?"*

Right does the arithmetic, and grins. It's Chapter 2 again — with a new target.

</Scene>

**Problem: 3Sum · Fix one number, search for the other two**

Three numbers sounds like a new problem. Hold one still. If it is −2, the other two need to make +2. You already know how to search for that pair.

Sort the input, pick a fixed position, and run the pair search **only to its right**. Repeat with the next fixed position.

<Figure caption="The seated passenger is fixed. The twins search only to its right, for a pair that cancels it out.">
  <ArrayStrip values={[-2, 0, 0, 2, 2]} pointers={[{ at: 0, label: 'FIXED', tone: 'purple' }, { at: 1, label: 'LEFT' }, { at: 4, label: 'RIGHT' }]} tones={{ 0: 'purple' }} regions={[{ from: 1, to: 4, label: 'pair search: need +2', tone: 'gold' }]} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="Three becomes two" steps={triplets} takeaway="Remember: fix one, search two, skip repeated answers. Keep duplicate input values available when a valid triplet needs them." />

**Why it works:** every sorted triplet has a first position. Trying each possible first position gives the pair search a chance to find its other two. Starting `left` after the fixed position prevents reusing the same element.

**The extra skill is avoiding duplicate answers.** Skip a fixed value if it equals the previous fixed value. After finding a triplet, move both ends and skip repeats of the pair values. This avoids rediscovering the same value triplet.

<Reveal q="Why not remove duplicate numbers before starting?">

Because `[0, 0, 0]` is a valid triplet. So is `[-1, -1, 2]`. You need enough positions to form the answer. Avoid duplicate **results**, not duplicate **input values**.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.triplets}>

```java id=threeSum
public java.util.List<java.util.List<Integer>> threeSum(int[] nums) {
    java.util.Arrays.sort(nums);
    var result = new java.util.ArrayList<java.util.List<Integer>>();
    for (int fixed = 0; fixed < nums.length - 2; fixed++) {
        if (fixed > 0 && nums[fixed] == nums[fixed - 1]) continue;
        if (nums[fixed] > 0) break; // Every remaining value is positive.
        int left = fixed + 1, right = nums.length - 1;
        while (left < right) {
            long sum = (long) nums[fixed] + nums[left] + nums[right];
            if (sum < 0) left++;
            else if (sum > 0) right--;
            else {
                result.add(java.util.List.of(nums[fixed], nums[left], nums[right]));
                left++;
                right--;
                while (left < right && nums[left] == nums[left - 1]) left++;
                while (left < right && nums[right] == nums[right + 1]) right--;
            }
        }
    }
    return result;
}
```

**O(n²) time**: up to n fixed positions, each with an O(n) scan. The pointer scan uses O(1) extra space; sorting workspace and the returned triplets are additional. This version sorts the input in place.

The same reduction extends to **4Sum**: fix two positions, then search the pair, giving O(n³) time. Each extra fixed loop changes the cost. Use a wide type for sums.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** a successful pair must move both ends; otherwise the loop can keep adding the same answer forever. [Solve 3Sum](https://leetcode.com/problems/3sum/).

<h2 id="biggest-container">Chapter 4 · The reservoir walls</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 4" title="The reservoir walls">

North of town stands a row of thin stone walls, all different heights. The waterworks may choose **any two** of them and fill the space between with water. It rises until it spills over the *shorter* wall; the taller one can't help past that point.

Left and Right start at the two outermost walls — the widest reservoir there is, though maybe not the deepest. They measure it, write the number down, and then look at each other.

One of them has to step inward. Get this wrong, and you walk straight past the best reservoir in the north.

</Scene>

**Problem: Container With Most Water · Move the limiting side**

Two vertical lines hold water. The shorter one sets the water height; the distance between them sets the width.

**Area = shorter height × width.** Your job is to find the biggest area across all pairs of lines.

<Figure caption="The classic input. The best reservoir uses the walls at positions 1 and 8: height min(8, 7) = 7, width 7, area 49. The outermost pair only manages 1 × 8 = 8, because the wall of height 1 caps everything.">
  <Bars heights={[1, 8, 6, 2, 5, 4, 8, 3, 7]} pair={[1, 8]} lines pointers={[{ at: 1, label: 'L' }, { at: 8, label: 'R' }]} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="Which wall is holding you back?" steps={container} takeaway="Remember: record the area, then move the shorter side. Keeping it while shrinking the width cannot improve the answer." />

**Why the shorter side moves:** keep that short line and move the other side inward, and the width gets smaller while usable height cannot increase. None of those pairs can beat the pair you just measured. You can safely leave the short line behind.

**What stays true:** the best area so far covers every pair you have ruled out; any better answer must still be between the pointers. If the heights tie, either side can move.

<HintLadder hints={hints.container}>

```java id=maxArea
public int maxArea(int[] height) {
    int left = 0, right = height.length - 1, best = 0;
    while (left < right) {
        int area = Math.min(height[left], height[right]) * (right - left);
        best = Math.max(best, area);
        if (height[left] <= height[right]) left++;
        else right--;
    }
    return best;
}
```

Measure **before** moving. **O(n) time, O(1) extra space**. The problem's limits fit `int`; use `long` for area if your inputs can make the product overflow.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** don't sort the heights. Their original positions determine the widths. Don't add all areas together either — you need the single largest one. [Solve Container With Most Water](https://leetcode.com/problems/container-with-most-water/).

<h2 id="catch-the-rain">Chapter 5 · After the storm</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 5" title="After the storm" mood="storm">

The storm rolls out over the hills at dawn. Arraytown's street is a row of stone blocks — some tall, some sunk to nothing — and rain is sitting in every hollow between them.

This is not the reservoir. Every block is solid and takes up space, and *every* puddle counts. The mayor wants one number: how many units of rain are standing on the street right now?

The twins start at the ends again, each carrying a note of the tallest wall they've passed. Right's note says 3. Left's says 1. *"Then one of you,"* the Keeper says, *"already knows enough to finish the next block. Which one?"*

</Scene>

**Problem: Trapping Rain Water · Settle the side with the lower known wall**

This time every bar occupies space. Rain sits **above individual bars**, and you add those amounts together.

At a position, the water level is limited by the lower of the tallest walls on its left and right. Subtract the bar's own height to find the water above it.

<Figure caption="The puddles after the storm: 1 + 1 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 6 units. Each blue number is min(tallest wall left, tallest wall right) − the block's own height.">
  <Bars heights={[0, 1, 0, 2, 1, 0, 1, 3, 2, 1, 2, 1]} water={[0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 2, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0]} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="How much fits above this bar?" steps={rain} takeaway="Remember: the lower known wall is enough to settle its side. Add water above each bar; the bar itself takes up space." />

Keep `leftMax` and `rightMax`, the tallest walls seen from each end. **Process the side with the smaller max.** The opposite side already supplies a wall at least that tall, so you have enough information to settle this next position.

If the next bar is taller than the smaller max, it becomes the new wall and holds zero water. Otherwise, add the difference. Once processed, a position's contribution is final.

<HintLadder hints={hints.rain}>

```java id=trap
public int trap(int[] height) {
    if (height.length < 3) return 0;
    int left = 0, right = height.length - 1;
    int leftMax = height[left], rightMax = height[right];
    int water = 0;
    while (left < right) {
        if (leftMax <= rightMax) {
            left++;
            leftMax = Math.max(leftMax, height[left]);
            water += leftMax - height[left];
        } else {
            right--;
            rightMax = Math.max(rightMax, height[right]);
            water += rightMax - height[right];
        }
    }
    return water;
}
```

On the trace's input `[4, 1, 0, 2, 5]`, the inside bars contribute 3, 4, and 2: total **9**. Update the wall height before subtracting, so a new taller bar contributes zero rather than negative water. **O(n) time, O(1) extra space**. The given problem limits fit `int`; larger inputs may need a `long` total.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** using only the immediate neighbouring bars misses taller walls farther away. Also keep the two water problems straight: **reservoir = best single pair; storm = sum above every bar**. [Solve Trapping Rain Water](https://leetcode.com/problems/trapping-rain-water/).

<h2 id="keep-the-good-stuff">Chapter 6 · Sorting the mail</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 6" title="Sorting the mail" mood="dawn">

The post office has a problem: one long shelf of envelopes, and mixed among them are blanks — envelopes marked **0**, addressed to nobody. The postmaster wants every real letter at the front, *in its original order*, and every blank pushed to the back. There is no second shelf.

Two clerks volunteer. Read will look at every envelope, one by one. Write takes a duller job: standing at the next free slot of the keep pile.

*"I only move,"* Write says, *"when you hand me something worth keeping."*

</Scene>

**Problem: Move Zeroes · Read and Write (also called reader and scribe)**

`read` looks at every value. `write` marks the next slot for a nonzero. A zero makes only `read` advance. A nonzero goes into `write`, then both advance. Swapping leaves zeroes behind without disturbing the order of nonzero values.

<Figure caption="Mid-shift at the post office: the kept letters are finished and in order, the blanks Read has already passed wait behind them, and the rest of the shelf is unread.">
  <ArrayStrip values={[1, 3, 0, 0, 12, 0, 5]} pointers={[{ at: 2, label: 'WRITE' }, { at: 4, label: 'READ', tone: 'blue' }]} tones={{ 0: 'done', 1: 'done', 2: 'out', 3: 'out' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 1, label: 'kept, in order', tone: 'green' }, { from: 2, to: 3, label: 'scanned zeroes', tone: 'dim' }, { from: 4, to: 6, label: 'unread', tone: 'blue' }]} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="One explores. One keeps." steps={compact} takeaway="Remember: write counts kept values, not swaps. Everything before write is the finished nonzero prefix." />

Freeze the loop just before reading a value:

```text
[ finished nonzero values | scanned zeroes | unread values ]
  0                write                  read
```

That's the promise for **this swapping version of Move Zeroes**. Other reader/writer problems can leave arbitrary ignored values in the middle; don't assume that zone is always zero.

<HintLadder hints={hints.compact}>

```java id=moveZeroes
public void moveZeroes(int[] nums) {
    int write = 0;
    for (int read = 0; read < nums.length; read++) {
        if (nums[read] != 0) {
            if (read != write) {
                int temp = nums[write];
                nums[write] = nums[read];
                nums[read] = temp;
            }
            write++; // A value was kept, even if no swap was needed.
        }
    }
}
```

Yes, `write` advances even when the value was already in the correct place. **O(n) time, O(1) extra space**. The nonzero order stays the same.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** moving every zero by shifting the whole suffix can turn this into O(n²) work. You only need one read pass. [Solve Move Zeroes](https://leetcode.com/problems/move-zeroes/).

<h2 id="control-duplicates">Chapter 7 · The bouncer</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 7" title="The bouncer" mood="night">

The Guild hall is hosting its winter dinner, and the queue outside is sorted by family name — so all the Smiths arrive together. House rule: **at most two guests from any one family.**

Write takes the door. Read walks down the queue, sending guests forward one at a time.

When a third Smith steps up, Write doesn't need to remember the whole evening. She glances at whoever came in **two seats ago**. Another Smith? Then two are already inside. *"Sorry, friend."*

</Scene>

**Problems: Remove Duplicates I and II · Same jobs, a different keep rule**

Now the array is sorted, and you're building a prefix with a limit on repeated values. `read` still visits everything. `write` still counts what you've kept.

For **one copy**, keep a value when it differs from the last kept value. For **two copies**, compare it with the kept value **two slots back**. If those values match, two copies are already in the answer.

<Figure caption="The third 1 arrives. Write looks two kept seats back — another 1 — so two are already inside. Read moves on; Write stays put.">
  <ArrayStrip values={[1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3]} pointers={[{ at: 0, label: 'WRITE − 2', tone: 'purple' }, { at: 2, label: 'READ · WRITE', tone: 'gold' }]} tones={{ 0: 'purple', 1: 'done' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 1, label: 'kept: two 1s already inside', tone: 'green' }]} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="The duplicate bouncer" steps={duplicates} takeaway="Remember: compare against the kept prefix. For a K-copy limit, keep the first K values, then compare with nums[write − K]." />

**Why it works:** sorted order groups equal values together. If the current value equals the one K places back in the kept prefix, that prefix already ends in K copies of it. Skipping the new copy keeps the limit intact.

**What stays true:** everything before `write` is the correct answer so far, in order, with the allowed number of copies. Values after that prefix don't have to look tidy.

<HintLadder hints={hints.duplicates}>

```java id=removeDuplicates
public int removeDuplicates(int[] nums) {
    int write = 0;
    for (int read = 0; read < nums.length; read++) {
        if (write == 0 || nums[read] != nums[write - 1]) {
            nums[write++] = nums[read];
        }
    }
    return write;
}

public int removeDuplicatesII(int[] nums) {
    int write = 0;
    for (int read = 0; read < nums.length; read++) {
        if (write < 2 || nums[read] != nums[write - 2]) {
            nums[write++] = nums[read];
        }
    }
    return write;
}
```

Java's short-circuit `||` protects the negative indexes: it does not evaluate the second condition when the first is true. Both versions take **O(n) time, O(1) extra space**.

For a positive limit K, generalize to `write < K || nums[read] != nums[write - K]`. If K is zero, the retained length is zero. This rule depends on sorted input.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** the return value is the **new logical length**, not the original array length. For `[1, 1, 1, 2]`, keeping two copies returns 3; only `[1, 1, 2]` matters. The remaining slot is ignored.

Practice both: [Remove Duplicates I](https://leetcode.com/problems/remove-duplicates-from-sorted-array/) · [Remove Duplicates II](https://leetcode.com/problems/remove-duplicates-from-sorted-array-ii/).

<h2 id="sort-three-colors">Chapter 8 · The paint works</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 8" title="The paint works">

At the paint works every can is red (**0**), white (**1**), or blue (**2**), and a careless delivery has jumbled them along one long shelf. The foreman wants reds, then whites, then blues. One pass. No second shelf.

Three sorters take their places. Low guards the end of the red section. High guards the start of the blue section, working in from the far end. Mid walks through the cans nobody has looked at yet.

Their only job is to make that unknown stretch — between Mid and High — shrink to nothing. And Mid has one superstition the others laugh at, until the day it saves them: *never trust a can that came from High's side.*

</Scene>

**Problem: Sort Colors · Partition into three zones**

Sometimes two jobs need three positions. Here values are 0, 1, or 2. You want all 0s, then all 1s, then all 2s.

- `low`: the next slot for a 0.
- `mid`: the value you're inspecting.
- `high`: the next slot for a 2, from the right.

<Figure caption="Four zones, one promise: only the unknown middle stretch still needs looking at.">
  <ArrayStrip values={[0, 0, 1, 1, 2, 0, 1, 2, 2]} pointers={[{ at: 2, label: 'LOW', tone: 'red' }, { at: 4, label: 'MID', tone: 'gold' }, { at: 6, label: 'HIGH', tone: 'blue' }]} tones={{ 0: 'done', 1: 'done', 2: 'done', 3: 'done', 7: 'blue', 8: 'blue' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 1, label: '0s', tone: 'red' }, { from: 2, to: 3, label: '1s', tone: 'dim' }, { from: 4, to: 6, label: 'unknown', tone: 'gold' }, { from: 7, to: 8, label: '2s', tone: 'blue' }]} />
</Figure>

At each step, `[0, low)` is 0s, `[low, mid)` is 1s, `[mid, high]` is unknown, and everything after `high` is 2s. Your job is to shrink the unknown zone.

<Trace title="Don't skip the surprise" steps={colors} takeaway="Remember: a swap from high brings unknown cargo. Keep mid still and inspect it. A swap from low brings a known 1, or swaps with itself." />

**The three moves:** 0 swaps with `low`, then `low` and `mid` advance. A 1 needs no swap; advance `mid`. A 2 swaps with `high`, then only `high` moves left.

<Reveal q="Why is a swap with low safe to move past, but a swap with high isn't?">

When `low < mid`, the zone from `low` to `mid - 1` is already known to contain 1s. A swap with `low` therefore sends a known 1 to `mid`. When `low == mid`, it is a self-swap that settles the 0.

The value at `high` is still in the unknown zone. It could be anything. Advancing `mid` after receiving it would skip its inspection — that's Mid's superstition, and it is exactly right.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.colors}>

```java id=sortColors
public void sortColors(int[] nums) {
    int low = 0, mid = 0, high = nums.length - 1;
    while (mid <= high) {
        if (nums[mid] == 0) {
            int temp = nums[low];
            nums[low] = nums[mid];
            nums[mid] = temp;
            low++;
            mid++;
        } else if (nums[mid] == 1) {
            mid++;
        } else { // The input contains only 0, 1, and 2.
            int temp = nums[high];
            nums[high] = nums[mid];
            nums[mid] = temp;
            high--;
        }
    }
}
```

Use `mid <= high`: when they are equal, one unknown value still needs inspection. Every iteration shrinks the unknown zone, even when `mid` stays put. **O(n) time, O(1) extra space**.

This is also called the **Dutch National Flag** partition. Partitioning objects this way does not generally preserve their original order within a group.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** trace `[1, 2, 0]`. An extra `mid++` after the high-swap leaves `[1, 0, 2]`. One tiny input exposes the entire mistake. [Solve Sort Colors](https://leetcode.com/problems/sort-colors/).

**Problem: Merge Sorted Array · Two readers, one writer**

You have two sorted arrays. `nums1` has spare slots at its end. Put both arrays' values into it without losing anything you haven't read yet.

Writing the smallest value at the front sounds natural — until it overwrites an unread value. So use the free space: **choose the largest unread value and write from the back**.

<Figure caption="The dashed pitches are free. Write fills them from the back with the larger of the two captains' wagons, so nothing unread is ever parked on.">
  <ArrayStrip name="nums1" values={[1, 4, 7, '·', '·', '·']} pointers={[{ at: 2, label: 'i' }, { at: 5, label: 'WRITE', tone: 'green' }]} tones={{ 3: 'ghost', 4: 'ghost', 5: 'ghost' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 2, label: 'unread', tone: 'blue' }, { from: 3, to: 5, label: 'free space', tone: 'green' }]} />
  <ArrayStrip name="nums2" values={[2, 5, 6]} pointers={[{ at: 2, label: 'j', tone: 'purple' }]} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 2, label: 'unread', tone: 'purple' }]} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="Protect the unread values" steps={merge} takeaway="Remember: write into the free space at the end. When nums2 is exhausted, the remaining nums1 prefix is already where it belongs." />

**What stays true:** everything after `write` is the finished sorted suffix. The prefixes ending at `i` and `j` are unread. The write position cannot overwrite an unread `nums1` value while `nums2` still has elements waiting.

This is the same family as a normal forward merge into a separate output array. The destination's layout decides which direction is safe.

<HintLadder hints={hints.merge}>

```java id=merge
public void merge(int[] nums1, int m, int[] nums2, int n) {
    int i = m - 1, j = n - 1, write = m + n - 1;
    while (j >= 0) {
        if (i >= 0 && nums1[i] > nums2[j]) {
            nums1[write--] = nums1[i--];
        } else {
            nums1[write--] = nums2[j--];
        }
    }
}
```

If `i` runs out first, copy the remaining `nums2` values. If `j` runs out first, stop: the remaining `nums1` values are already correctly placed. **O(m + n) time, O(1) extra space** beyond the given destination buffer.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** spare slots may contain zeroes, but zero can also be a real value. Use `m` and `n` to identify real data, not the contents of a slot. Try `m = 0`, then `n = 0`. [Solve Merge Sorted Array](https://leetcode.com/problems/merge-sorted-array/).

**Problem: Append Characters to Make Subsequence · One pointer per string**

A subsequence keeps order but allows gaps. `abc` is a subsequence of `axbyc`: you skip x and y. The characters don't have to touch.

Let the source pointer explore. Let the target pointer say **“this is the next character I need.”** Advance that target pointer only when the source supplies a match.

<Figure caption="Reading “axby” while hunting for “abc”. a and b were found in order (x and y skipped); the letter ran out before any c. Append one character.">
  <ArrayStrip name="letter" values={['a', 'x', 'b', 'y']} tones={{ 0: 'done', 2: 'done', 1: 'out', 3: 'out' }} />
  <ArrayStrip name="codeword" values={['a', 'b', 'c']} pointers={[{ at: 2, label: 'NEEDED', tone: 'purple' }]} tones={{ 0: 'done', 1: 'done', 2: 'hot' }} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 1, label: 'matched in order', tone: 'green' }, { from: 2, to: 2, label: 'append', tone: 'gold' }]} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="Wait for the character you need" steps={subsequence} takeaway="Remember: source always explores; target only advances on a match. Append the target suffix that never matched." />

**Why taking the earliest match is safe:** choosing an earlier matching character leaves at least as much source text available for later characters as choosing a later match would. You lose no opportunity by accepting it.

**What stays true:** the target prefix before `needed` has been matched in order using source characters already visited. Once the source ends, the remaining target suffix must be appended.

<HintLadder hints={hints.subsequence}>

```java id=appendCharacters
public int appendCharacters(String source, String target) {
    int needed = 0;
    for (int read = 0; read < source.length() && needed < target.length(); read++) {
        if (source.charAt(read) == target.charAt(needed)) needed++;
    }
    return target.length() - needed;
}
```

Return 0 if everything matched. The guard also handles an empty target without reading past it. **O(source.length()) time in the worst case, O(1) extra space**, for the problem's lowercase-letter input.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** sorting either string destroys the order. Advancing both pointers on a mismatch pretends you matched a character you never saw. [Solve Append Characters to Make Subsequence](https://leetcode.com/problems/append-characters-to-string-to-make-subsequence/).

**Problem: Squares of a Sorted Array · Opposite ends + backwards output**

Sorted input: `[-7, -3, 2, 5]`. Squaring gives `[49, 9, 4, 25]`, which is no longer sorted.

But there's still useful structure: **the largest absolute value is at one of the ends**. Compare their squares, place the larger one at the back of the output, and repeat.

<Figure caption="Two moves you already know, combined: the twins choose from the ends (Part One); Write fills a fresh page from the back (Part Three).">
  <ArrayStrip name="ledger" values={[-7, -3, 2, 5]} pointers={[{ at: 1, label: 'LEFT' }, { at: 3, label: 'RIGHT' }]} tones={{ 0: 'out' }} />

</Figure>

<Trace title="Borrow two moves you already know" steps={squares} takeaway="Remember: choose the larger square from the ends; write it into the last free output slot. You combined opposite-end selection with backwards writing." />

**What stays true:** the output after `write` is final and sorted. Every value still waiting to be squared lies between `left` and `right`.

<Reveal q="Why does this loop need left <= right, unlike the pair-search loop?">

The final remaining value still needs to be squared and written. You aren't trying to form a pair; you are consuming every input position exactly once.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.squares}>

```java id=sortedSquares
public int[] sortedSquares(int[] nums) {
    int left = 0, right = nums.length - 1;
    int[] result = new int[nums.length];
    for (int write = nums.length - 1; write >= 0; write--) {
        int leftSquare = nums[left] * nums[left];
        int rightSquare = nums[right] * nums[right];
        if (leftSquare > rightSquare) {
            result[write] = leftSquare;
            left++;
        } else {
            result[write] = rightSquare;
            right--;
        }
    }
    return result;
}
```

The output-driven loop also processes the last remaining value. **O(n) time, O(n) space for the returned array**, with O(1) other working space. Squares fit `int` under this problem's bounds; general integer inputs may need a `long[]` result and casts before multiplication.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** filling from the front with the larger square produces the wrong order. Compare the ends for the **largest**, and write at the **back**. [Solve Squares of a Sorted Array](https://leetcode.com/problems/squares-of-a-sorted-array/).

<h2 id="one-deletion">Side quest 1 · You may delete one character</h2>

**The card says:** ordinary palindrome checking rejects the first mismatch. Now you have one deletion to spend. Everything else must stay in order.

Try `abca`. The outer a's match. Now b faces c. You have two candidates: remove b and check `c`, or remove c and check `b`.

**The reasoning:** if the two ends differ, deleting an interior character leaves those ends mismatched. A successful single deletion must remove one of the mismatched endpoints. Check both remaining ranges as ordinary palindromes, with **no more deletions**.

<Reveal q="Why is always deleting the left endpoint wrong? Try cbbcc.">

The outer c's match, leaving `bbc`. Deleting the left b leaves `bc`, which fails. Deleting the right c leaves `bb`, which works. You need the OR of both range checks; you don't get to guess one branch.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={['Find the first mismatch after matching inward.', 'At that mismatch, try a range that skips left and a range that skips right.', 'Each remaining range must be a plain palindrome. Two linear checks still give O(n) total time.']}>

```java id=validPalindromeOneDeletion
public boolean validPalindromeOneDeletion(String s) {
    int left = 0, right = s.length() - 1;
    while (left < right) {
        if (s.charAt(left) != s.charAt(right)) {
            return isPalindromeRange(s, left + 1, right)
                || isPalindromeRange(s, left, right - 1);
        }
        left++;
        right--;
    }
    return true;
}
private boolean isPalindromeRange(String s, int left, int right) {
    while (left < right) {
        if (s.charAt(left++) != s.charAt(right--)) return false;
    }
    return true;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) extra space.** This version treats every character as meaningful. It allows *at most* one deletion, so an existing palindrome is already valid.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** why would allowing two deletions require a different branching argument? Explain it before trying to change the code.

<h2 id="count-pairs">Side quest 2 · Count the pairs, including duplicates</h2>

**The card says:** finding one pair isn't enough. First clarify what counts as a different answer.

For `[1, 1, 2, 2]`, target 3:

| The question asks for… | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| One valid pair | `[1, 2]` | Any valid pair is enough |
| Unique value pairs | 1 | Only the value combination 1 + 2 |
| Pairs of positions, with i &lt; j | 4 | Either of two 1s can pair with either of two 2s |

For **position pairs**, scan by sum as before. At a match, count the equal-value run on each end. If the end values differ, their runs contribute **left copies × right copies**.

If the endpoints are equal, sorted order means every value in between is equal too. With m remaining positions, choose any two: **m × (m − 1) / 2**. Count them and finish.

<Reveal q="Four 2s, target 4. Why isn't the answer 4 × 4?">

That would allow a position to pair with itself and count each pair in both orders. The six valid index pairs are `(0,1)`, `(0,2)`, `(0,3)`, `(1,2)`, `(1,3)`, and `(2,3)`.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={['Move by the sum until you find a match.', 'On a match, count whole runs of equal values instead of moving each end once.', 'Different end values: multiply run lengths. Equal end values: choose any two remaining positions and stop.']}>

```java id=countPairs
public long countPairs(int[] nums, int target) {
    int left = 0, right = nums.length - 1;
    long count = 0;
    while (left < right) {
        long sum = (long) nums[left] + nums[right];
        if (sum < target) left++;
        else if (sum > target) right--;
        else if (nums[left] == nums[right]) {
            long size = right - left + 1;
            count += size * (size - 1) / 2;
            break;
        } else {
            int lowValue = nums[left], highValue = nums[right];
            long lowCopies = 0, highCopies = 0;
            while (left <= right && nums[left] == lowValue) {
                lowCopies++;
                left++;
            }
            while (right >= left && nums[right] == highValue) {
                highCopies++;
                right--;
            }
            count += lowCopies * highCopies;
        }
    }
    return count;
}
```

For already-sorted input: **O(n) time, O(1) space**. The answer can be quadratic in n even though the work is linear; `long` protects the count.

For **unique value pairs**, increment once per match and skip both value runs. Don't multiply the run sizes.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** 3Sum skips duplicate *results*. Position-pair counting must include the combinations that those duplicate positions create. The question decides the rule.

<h2 id="count-less">Side quest 3 · Count every pair below a target</h2>

**The card says:** sorted `[1, 2, 4, 7]`, target 6. You want position pairs whose sum is **strictly less** than 6.

Start at 1 and 7. Too big: move right. Now 1 + 4 = 5. That proves **every partner from 2 through 4 works with 1**. Add `right - left`, which is 2, then move left.

You counted two answers with one decision. Next, 2 + 4 = 6 is equality, so it doesn't count. Final answer: 2 pairs.

<Figure caption="1 + 4 is under 6, and 4 is 1's biggest remaining partner — so every partner between them works too. One decision, right − left = 2 pairs counted.">
  <ArrayStrip values={[1, 2, 4, 7]} pointers={[{ at: 0, label: 'LEFT' }, { at: 2, label: 'RIGHT' }]} tones={{ 3: 'out', 1: 'done', 2: 'done' }} regions={[{ from: 1, to: 2, label: 'all pair with 1', tone: 'green' }]} />
</Figure>

**The promise:** all qualifying pairs involving retired left positions have been counted exactly once. The remaining range still contains every uncounted candidate.

<HintLadder hints={['A match with the largest remaining partner can tell you about all smaller partners.', 'When the end sum is below the target, add right − left for this left position.', 'Then advance left. Otherwise move right. Stop when left and right meet.']}>

```java id=countPairsBelow
public long countPairsBelow(int[] nums, long target) {
    int left = 0, right = nums.length - 1;
    long count = 0;
    while (left < right) {
        if ((long) nums[left] + nums[right] < target) {
            count += right - left;
            left++;
        } else {
            right--;
        }
    }
    return count;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) space** for sorted input. Counting pairs at or below a target changes `<` to `<=`. Say which comparison the problem requires before coding.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** how could two calls to this function count sums in an inclusive interval `[low, high]`? Keep the upper-bound arithmetic in `long`.

<h2 id="two-way-partition">Side quest 4 · Split into two groups</h2>

**The card says:** you don't need three colours every time. Sometimes the task is simply **values below a pivot first, all other values afterward**. Order inside either group isn't required.

For `[5, 1, 4, 2]` with pivot 3, the smaller values are 1 and 2. A valid final array is `[1, 2, 4, 5]`, but sorting everything would do more work than needed.

Use a reader and a boundary writer. Before `write`: values that pass the rule. Between `write` and `read`: inspected values that don't. At and after `read`: unknown.

<HintLadder hints={['The task needs groups, not fully sorted values.', 'read inspects each value; write marks the next slot for a value below the pivot.', 'Swap each matching value into write and advance write. Always advance read. Return write as the group boundary.']}>

```java id=partitionLess
public int partitionLess(int[] nums, int pivot) {
    int write = 0;
    for (int read = 0; read < nums.length; read++) {
        if (nums[read] < pivot) {
            int temp = nums[write];
            nums[write] = nums[read];
            nums[read] = temp;
            write++;
        }
    }
    return write;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) extra space.** All values remain present. This does not guarantee the original order of the non-matching group. For example, partitioning `[5, 4, 1]` below 3 produces `[1, 4, 5]`, reversing the two large values.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** replace “below pivot” with “even.” What changes in the code? Then require both groups to keep their original order: why isn't the same swap rule sufficient?

<h2 id="target-difference">Side quest 5 · Look for a difference</h2>

**The card says:** a target **sum** and a target **difference** need different movement rules.

For sorted `[1, 3, 8, 12]`, find distinct positions whose larger value minus smaller value is 5. Start two readers near the beginning. If the difference is too small, move the larger-value reader right. If it is too big, move the smaller-value reader right.

Trace it: `3 − 1 = 2` → advance the larger reader. `8 − 1 = 7` → advance the smaller reader. `8 − 3 = 5` → found.

**Why moving is safe:** if the difference is too small, keeping that larger value while increasing the smaller one would only shrink the difference. If it's too big, increasing the larger value would only grow it. Each reader moves forward at most n times.

<HintLadder hints={['Both readers move forward, but their jobs are smaller candidate and larger candidate.', 'Keep the positions distinct. If they meet, move the larger-value reader ahead.', 'Compare larger minus smaller to the target. Increase the larger reader if too small, the smaller reader if too big.']}>

```java id=hasPairDifference
public boolean hasPairDifference(int[] nums, int target) {
    if (target < 0) return false; // This asks for larger minus smaller.
    int left = 0, right = 1;
    while (right < nums.length) {
        if (left == right) {
            right++;
            continue;
        }
        long difference = (long) nums[right] - nums[left];
        if (difference == target) return true;
        if (difference < target) right++;
        else left++;
    }
    return false;
}
```

**O(n) time, O(1) space** for sorted input. A target difference of zero needs two equal values at different positions, not one value paired with itself.

</HintLadder>

**Catch the bug:** copying the sum-search movement rules can skip valid difference pairs. Derive the direction from the expression you are actually comparing.

<h2 id="intersection">Side quest 6 · Keep only what both inputs contain</h2>

**The card says:** two sorted sequences, but now you want their **intersection**, not their merged contents.

For `[1, 1, 4, 7]` and `[1, 3, 4, 4]`, the unique intersection is `[1, 4]`.

If values match, emit once and advance both. Otherwise, advance the smaller reader: that value cannot match the other sequence's current value or anything later, because those values are at least as large.

<HintLadder hints={['A smaller current value has no partner in the other remaining sorted suffix.', 'Give each input one reader. Only matching values go into the result.', 'Advance the smaller reader; on equality, emit if new and advance both. Stop when either input ends.']}>

```java id=intersectionUnique
public java.util.List<Integer> intersectionUnique(int[] a, int[] b) {
    var result = new java.util.ArrayList<Integer>();
    int i = 0, j = 0;
    while (i < a.length && j < b.length) {
        if (a[i] < b[j]) i++;
        else if (a[i] > b[j]) j++;
        else {
            if (result.isEmpty() || result.get(result.size() - 1) != a[i]) {
                result.add(a[i]);
            }
            i++;
            j++;
        }
    }
    return result;
}
```

**O(m + n) time**, with O(k) space for k returned values and O(1) other space. For a multiset intersection, emit on every match: matching one position from each input naturally returns the smaller copy count.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** merging continues until all values are placed. Intersection stops when either input ends. Explain that difference before changing the loop condition.

<h2 id="choose-the-tool">When to put the lanterns down</h2>

<Scene title="The unsorted market" mood="storm">

One market day the stalls arrive in no order at all: **3, 2, 4**. A traveller needs two items totalling 6. The twins, eager to show off, take the ends. 3 + 4 = 7 — too big. Right steps inward, abandoning the 4.

And with it, the only answer: 2 + 4.

The Keeper doesn't say anything for a while. Then: *"Your rule didn't fail. Its proof did. It needed the stalls to be sorted, and nobody checked."*

</Scene>

Try opposite-end sum moves on **unsorted** `[3, 2, 4]`, target 6. The ends make 7, so the memorised rule says to move right. That discards 4 — and loses the valid pair 2 + 4.

The rule failed because its **proof needed sorted order**. A pattern name does not supply that missing condition.

Before choosing two pointers, ask whether your move can settle a value or eliminate a whole group of possibilities. If not, consider another tool. For unsorted Two Sum with original indexes and expected O(n) time, a hash map is a good fit. Sorting paired values and original indexes is another approach when O(n log n) time is acceptable.

<Reveal q="Does every two-pointer problem need sorted input?">

No. Palindrome checking uses symmetry. Move Zeroes uses a keep rule. Sort Colors uses known value groups. Container and rainwater use boundary arguments and must retain original positions. Identify the actual reason for each move instead of treating “sorted” as a universal requirement.

</Reveal>

<h2 id="wider-family">The neighbouring guilds · How the other roadmap patterns connect</h2>

Arraytown isn't the only town with lanterns. Your roadmap gives some pointer techniques their own chapters elsewhere. Keep those boundaries, but recognise the shared idea: **two positions with a relationship worth preserving**.

| Relative movement | What the relationship tells you | Where it belongs |
|---|---|---|
| One pointer moves one hop, another two | In a cycle, their relative position eventually repeats and they meet | <a href={`${BASE}dsa/lessons/linked-lists/`}>Fast/slow pointers · Linked Lists</a> |
| Both move with a fixed gap | The trailing position is a known distance behind the leading one | <a href={`${BASE}dsa/lessons/linked-lists/`}>Nth node from the end · Linked Lists</a> |
| Two boundaries enclose a contiguous range | State tracks what is inside; expand or shrink while preserving the required condition | <a href={`${BASE}dsa/lessons/sliding-window/`}>Sliding Window</a> |
| Both move outward from a center | Every inner pair already matches; compare the next outer pair | <a href={`${BASE}dsa/lessons/dp-1d/`}>Palindrome expansion · 1-D DP</a> |

**Fast/slow is not reader/writer.** Both positions follow the structure; neither is building an output prefix. Starting them together is not evidence of a cycle — compare after advancing.

**Fixed gap is not target difference.** A fixed-gap technique preserves a number of positions or hops. The difference-search above compares values and changes the position gap as needed.

**An expanding center has two starting shapes.** Try `aba` (one middle character) and `abba` (a gap between two middle characters). Check both kinds of center. Trying every center takes O(n²) time in the worst case, with O(1) extra space for the expansion itself.

**A window needs its own movement proof.** For example, shrinking because a sum is too large relies on appropriate value constraints. With negative numbers, removing a left value can increase a sum, and extending can decrease it. Don't transplant a positive-only rule without checking.

*"Pick one,"* she says. *"Before you write any code, tell me what each lantern is for, why each move is safe, and when you'll stop."*

Choose one. Use your editor, keep the tutorial code closed, and write down your pointer jobs, safe-move argument, and stopping condition before coding.

1. **Count within a band.** Sorted input, count position pairs with a sum in inclusive `[low, high]`. Include duplicates. Test an empty input, all-equal values, and bounds near integer limits.
2. **Change the filter.** Keep at most three copies in a sorted array. Return the correct prefix length. Test fewer than three values, a long repeated run, and an all-distinct input.
3. **Change the output.** Return the multiset intersection of two sorted arrays, retaining the smaller frequency of each value. Test one empty input and uneven duplicate runs.

<Reveal q="I've written and tested it. What should my reasoning contain?">

**Band count:** `countPairsBelow(nums, (long) high + 1) - countPairsBelow(nums, low)`, assuming `low <= high`. Each call has its own fresh pointers. Counts and the upper-bound addition use `long`.

**Three copies:** keep if `write < 3 || nums[read] != nums[write - 3]`. The compared value belongs to the kept prefix. Return `write`; ignore the tail.

**Multiset intersection:** advance the smaller reader; on equality, emit one and advance both. Unlike unique intersection, do not suppress repeated matches. Stop when either sequence ends.

Now explain why a slightly wrong version fails on one of your tests. That counterexample is stronger evidence of understanding than a memorised template.

</Reveal>

<h2 id="cheat-sheet">The Keeper's notebook</h2>

| When you see… | Start with… | The promise to keep |
|---|---|---|
| Symmetry, ignoring some characters | Two ends; skip, compare, move both | Everything outside has matched or been ignored safely |
| Sorted input + target pair | Two ends; move according to sum | No possible answer has been thrown away |
| Unique triplets | Fix one + pair search + skip repeated results | Each unique value triplet is returned once |
| Largest container between lines | Measure area; move shorter side | Ruled-out pairs cannot beat the best already measured |
| Water above bars | Left/right maxima; process lower known wall | Processed positions have final water amounts |
| Move zeroes / filter in place | Reader + writer | Kept prefix is final and in order |
| At most K duplicates | Reader + writer; compare K kept slots back | Kept prefix respects the copy limit |
| Three categories in place | low / mid / high | Only the middle unknown zone needs inspection |
| Merge two sorted sequences | One reader each + output writer | Written output is final; unread data is safe |
| Subsequence with gaps | Source reader + next needed target character | Target prefix matched in order |
| Sorted squares | Compare end squares; fill output backwards | Written suffix contains the largest squares in order |
| One deletion allowed | At the first mismatch, check both endpoint-removal branches | Each branch has no deletion left |
| Count duplicate position pairs | Count runs at matched ends | Every distinct position pair is counted exactly once |
| Count sums below a bound | Add a whole partner range when its largest member works | Retired left positions have all their pairs counted |
| Partition into two groups | Read + boundary writer | Known prefix satisfies the predicate; all values are preserved |
| Sorted target difference | Two readers moving forward | Movement follows the difference, with distinct positions |
| Sorted intersection | Advance the smaller reader; emit on equality | Output values have appeared in both inputs |

Two pointers does **not** automatically mean O(n) time or O(1) space. Count every loop and every output structure. 3Sum has repeated scans; sorted squares builds an output array.

## Five bugs the Keeper keeps in her pocket

1. **Moving without a reason.** Say what has become impossible or settled before moving.
2. **Overwriting something unread.** Decide whether a copy, swap, or backwards write protects it. In-place does not always mean swapping.
3. **Counting swaps instead of kept values.** `write` advances whenever a value is kept, including self-position cases.
4. **Skipping unknown cargo.** After Sort Colors swaps with `high`, inspect `mid` again.
5. **Borrowing the wrong loop boundary.** Pair search stops when pointers meet; partitioning and writing may still have one value to process. Trace that last iteration.
