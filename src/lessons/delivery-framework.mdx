<h2 id="prologue">Prologue · Eighteen minutes</h2>

<Scene chapter="Prologue" title="Eighteen minutes" mood="dusk">

Mara runs mock interviews on Friday afternoons, in the small room with the bad whiteboard. Today it's you. She puts her phone face-down on the table — a 45-minute timer — and says: *"Design Kestrel's link shortener."*

You are good at this part. You ask about custom aliases, expiry, analytics, abuse, regions, deep links into the app. Every question is a fair question. When you finally pick up the marker, the timer says 21 minutes gone.

At minute 38 you are still drawing arrows into a database. At 41 Mara turns the phone over. *"Time."*

She doesn't mention a wrong answer. There weren't any. She draws a long line on the board and splits it: a fat block at the start, a thin sliver at the end. *"You knew everything,"* she says. *"You ran out of clock. The sliver is where the offer was."*

Then she hands you the marker. *"So where should the forty-five minutes go?"*

</Scene>

A design round is **a structured conversation you are expected to drive.** The single biggest difference between a hire and a no-hire at the same level of knowledge is whether the candidate controls the clock or the interviewer does. A framework is how you control it: the steps never change from one problem to the next, so your attention goes to the content, not to "what do I do now?"

This lesson is personal. Your own mock logs list the gaps it attacks:

- **Pacing** — 18 minutes on requirements, leaving deep dives a few minutes. You already do requirements *well*. That's the danger: you do them for too long.
- **Silent skips** — leaving things out without saying so, and going quiet instead of reasoning out loud. The interviewer scores only what you say.
- **Decomposition from scratch** — freezing when a problem doesn't match one you've rehearsed.
- And three that get a rule each below: **layer conflation** (answering tenant isolation when resource authorization was asked), **M:N modelled as 1:N** (Role ↔ Permission), and **"we'll cache it in Redis"** with no key, TTL or invalidation.

**The spine. Six steps, in order, every time:**

<Figure caption="The six steps. Each produces one output on the whiteboard and ends with a sentence that moves you to the next.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    R["1 · Requirements\nfeatures + NFR numbers"] --> E["2 · Core entities\nnouns + cardinality"]
    E --> A["3 · API\nresources, verbs, shapes"]
    A --> H["4 · High-level design\nboxes that meet each FR"]
    H --> D["5 · Deep dives\n2–3 hard parts, committed"]
    D --> W["6 · Wrap-up\nbottlenecks, failure, 10×"]`} />
</Figure>

| # | Step | Output on the board | Exit sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Requirements** | 3 core features, out-of-scope list, 3–5 non-functionals with numbers | "So we're building X and Y at Z scale, favouring availability — does that match?" |
| 2 | **Core entities** | The nouns and the relationships between them, with cardinality | "Those are the nouns; here's how a client talks to them." |
| 3 | **API** | Core endpoints: method, path, request, response | "That's the contract. Let me build something that serves it." |
| 4 | **High-level design** | Boxes and arrows that satisfy every functional requirement, each flow traced | "This works. Now let's make it meet the non-functionals." |
| 5 | **Deep dives** | 2–3 hard problems, each ending in a committed decision | "With the time left, let me flag what I'd do next." |
| 6 | **Wrap-up** | Bottleneck at 10×, failure modes, what you'd monitor | (Hand the floor back for questions.) |

**Where did estimation go?** Your old card had it as its own five-minute step. Keep the habit; change the timing. A few numbers — daily users, read/write ratio, data size — belong *inside* requirements, because they are how you quantify the non-functionals. Deeper arithmetic happens **when a number is about to decide something**, usually in a deep dive. The rule from your old card stands unchanged: **only compute numbers that change a decision.** "1M QPS" matters because it forces a cache tier and horizontal scaling. "37 GB of logs" matters because it fits on one Postgres box. A number that doesn't end in a design conclusion was wasted breath. (Some interviewers do expect the numbers up front. Ask, in one sentence — see Chapter 2.)

The rows add up: 3 + 6 + 2 + 4 + 10 + 13 + 3 + 4 = 45, and 5 + 7 + 3 + 5 + 12 + 20 + 3 + 5 = 60. If the interviewer skips introductions, the spare minutes go to deep dives, not to requirements.

**Four checkpoints, not eight.** You can't watch eight boundaries while thinking. Write four times in the corner of the board before you start — for a 45-minute round **9 · 15 · 25 · 38** (for 60: **12 · 20 · 32 · 52**) — the moments you must be leaving requirements, starting to draw, starting deep dives, and wrapping up. Glance at them each time you finish a thought.

**Your card, reconciled.** The printed card you've been using splits a clean 45 minutes as requirements 0–5, estimation 5–10, API and data model 10–15, high-level 15–30, deep dives 30–42, wrap-up 42–45. That is the same spine on a design-only clock, with no intros or questions. Use it when an interviewer really does give you the full 45; otherwise use the table above. Either way, the proportions are the point: **a third or more of the design time goes to deep dives, and requirements get a sixth or less.** (45 minutes: 13 of 38 design minutes for dives, 6 for requirements. 60 minutes: 20 of 50, and 7.)

**Worked numbers: what 18 minutes of requirements costs.** Design runs 0:03 to 0:41 (38 minutes). Requirements planned at 6 run to 18 — you reach 0:21. Entities, API and high-level take their planned 2 + 4 + 10 = 16, so it is 0:37. That leaves **4 minutes** for deep dives and wrap-up, which were budgeted 13 + 3 = 16. The overspend didn't come out of requirements' neighbours. It came out of the step where senior signal lives.

| Where the 12 extra minutes came from | Planned | Actual |
|---|---|---|
| Deep dives + wrap-up | 16 min | 4 min |
| Signal the interviewer can write down about depth | Two committed dives | Half of one, or none |

**How to keep requirements to six minutes.** Write as you talk — a functional list, an out-of-scope list, a non-functional list with numbers. Ask at most five clarifying questions, each one that would change the design (a question whose answer wouldn't change a box is a question for later). When an answer is "up to you", **assume, say the assumption, write it down, move on.** At the 0:09 checkpoint, say the bridge sentence whether or not you feel finished.

<Reveal q="The interviewer is chatty and the intros run eight minutes. What changes?">

The budget shrinks from the front, so you cut from the back of the *early* steps, not from the dives. Say it: *"We've got about 33 minutes — I'll keep requirements to five and move fast to the design."* Compress entities into the API step (name the nouns as you write the endpoints), draw the high-level design in eight minutes, and protect at least ten minutes for deep dives. Naming the squeeze out loud is itself a signal: you are managing the clock, not being managed by it.

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** "Requirements are the important part — getting them right saves time later." Getting them *right* does. Getting them *exhaustive* doesn't. Most of what you'd find in minutes 7 to 18 is out of scope anyway, and an interviewer who wants a feature you parked will tell you.

<h2 id="drive">Chapter 2 · Who holds the marker</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 2" title="Who holds the marker">

Mara plays the interviewer again, but differently. After your requirements she says nothing. Ten seconds. Twenty. You wait for her to tell you what's next.

She lets the silence run to thirty seconds, then breaks character. *"That was the test,"* she says. *"At senior level, nobody tells you what's next. Who was holding the marker just now?"*

You look down. You were.

</Scene>

**Driving** means the interviewer never has to ask "what's next?" You announce each step, do it out loud, check in, and bridge to the next. **Being driven** means waiting for questions and answering them. Both candidates might say the same correct things; only one of them demonstrates that they can run a design discussion at work.

<Figure caption="The loop you run at every step. The checkpoint is a question, not a pause — keep moving unless they redirect you.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    A["Announce\n“Next: the API”"] --> D["Do it out loud\nwrite as you talk"]
    D --> K["Checkpoint\n“Does that match?”"]
    K --> B["Bridge sentence\nto the next step"]
    B --> A`} />
</Figure>

**Narrate the transitions.** Interviewers can't read your mind mid-silence. Use stock sentences verbatim until they're reflex:

- "Before I design, let me get a feel for the numbers." (→ scale numbers)
- "The numbers say [conclusion], so here's the shape I'd start with." (→ high-level design)
- "The hard part here is clearly [X] — let me go deep on it." (→ deep dive)
- "With the time left, let me flag what I'd do next." (→ wrap-up)

**Narrate the skips too.** The fix for silent skips is one sentence: *"I'll assume a standard auth layer gives us a user id — happy to go into it."* *"I'm skipping bandwidth; text payloads at this rate are a non-issue."* A spoken skip is a decision. A silent one reads as a gap.

**Driving is not ignoring.** The interviewer can steer at any time, and **every steer is a hint about what they want to score.** Follow it immediately, say what you are parking (*"I'll pause the remaining endpoints"*), and come back only if it matters. Two more driving moves:

- **Ask how they like to run it, once, at the start.** *"I'll do requirements, a quick sense of scale, the API, a simple design, then deep dives — would you like full capacity estimates up front, or numbers as they become relevant?"* Ten seconds, and you'll never be surprised by an interviewer who expected a different order.
- **Think out loud, not silently.** If you need to think, say what you're weighing: *"I'm deciding between a counter and random codes — the question is whether the counter becomes a single point of failure."* Twenty seconds of narrated thinking is signal. Twenty seconds of silence is a blank in their notes.

**What graders write down.** Rubrics differ between companies and levels are named differently (L4/L5/L6, E4/E5/E6), so treat this as a composite of how interviewers commonly describe the bar, not any one company's form. Most write-ups cover the same axes: **problem navigation** (did you scope and prioritise?), **solution design** (does it meet the requirements?), **technical depth** (do you know how the pieces behave, with numbers?), **tradeoffs and judgment** (alternatives named, a choice committed for a reason), and **communication** (driving, clarity, responding to hints).

| | Mid-level | Senior | Staff |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Who drives** | You drive the early steps; the interviewer may steer you into the dives | You drive the whole round; the interviewer mostly probes | You drive and you shape the problem — reframing a requirement, saying what not to build |
| **Breadth vs depth** | A working end-to-end design; depth in one area with help | A working design quickly, then depth in two or three areas, with numbers | Depth where the business risk is, plus evolution: migration, multi-region, cost, failure domains |
| **Deep dives** | Can go deep when pointed at a problem | Finds the hard problems unprompted | Ranks the hard problems by impact and says which ones not to solve yet |
| **Tradeoffs** | Knows the options | Picks one, tied to a requirement or number | Names second-order effects and the condition that would flip the decision |
| **Failure and operations** | Mentions replicas and retries | Identifies specific failure modes and mitigations | Designs for blast radius, graceful degradation, and how the system is run and changed |

The same design can pass at one level and fail at the next. The difference is rarely knowledge; it is **who found the hard parts, and whether each one ended in a decision.**

<Trace title="Hold the marker" steps={driving} takeaway="Announce, do it out loud, check in, bridge. Follow every steer at once, and say what you are parking." />

<Reveal q="The interviewer drives hard from the first minute — asking pointed questions, steering constantly. Should you fight to take the marker back?">

No. Some interviewers run the round as a conversation they steer, and that is their choice to make. Answer what they ask, crisply, and **keep the framework running underneath**: when they pause, bridge back to it (*"So with that settled, the remaining piece of the design is…"*). You still show structure through how each answer is shaped — problem, options, decision — even when you don't choose the order.

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** "I'll let the interviewer guide me, since they know what they want." At mid-level that can pass. At senior level, an interviewer who has to ask "what's next?" three times will usually write that you needed guidance.

<h2 id="requirements">Chapter 3 · Three features and a number</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 3" title="Three features and a number" mood="dawn">

Iris walks past the mock room and leans in. *"You're designing the link shortener? Oh, and also — can people pick their own short codes? And see click counts? And make links expire? And —"*

Mara holds up a hand and turns to you. *"Every one of those is a real feature. You have six minutes. Which three are the product, and how do you say no to the rest without sounding like you're dodging?"*

</Scene>

**Functional requirements** are what users can do: "Users can…" statements. **Non-functional requirements** are the qualities the system must have while doing it: how fast, how available, how consistent, how durable, at what scale. Both are spoken and written down.

**Picking the three core features.** A product has dozens of features; a 45-minute design has room for about three.

1. **List the user verbs the product exists for.** Shorten a link. Follow a link. See clicks. Customise a code. Expire a link.
2. **Keep the ones without which it isn't this product.** Without "shorten" and "redirect", it isn't a link shortener. Without click counts, it still is.
3. **Prefer the ones that create the hard engineering problem** — they generate your deep dives. For Instagram that's the home feed; for Ticketmaster, booking a seat that many people want; for Uber, matching a rider to a nearby driver.
4. **Name the actors** when there's more than one (rider and driver; buyer and seller). Each actor often has its own core flow.
5. **Say the rest out loud as out of scope, and invite a veto.**

> "Users can shorten a URL. Users can follow a short URL and get redirected. Out of scope: custom aliases, analytics dashboard, expiry — tell me if you want any of those."

The *out of scope* sentence is not optional. It's how you buy time for depth later, and interviewers score it as maturity.

**Non-functional requirements — only the ones that shape the design, each with a number or an explicit choice:**

| Quality | Vague (scores nothing) | Quantified (drives a decision) |
|---|---|---|
| **Scale** | "Millions of users" | "100M redirects a day, 1M new links a day — reads outnumber writes 100 to 1" → caching, read replicas |
| **Latency** | "Fast" | "Redirect p99 under 100 ms" → the read path can't make several sequential database calls; cache in front |
| **Availability vs consistency** | "Highly available and consistent" | "Redirects favour availability; a brand-new link may take a second to resolve everywhere" → replicas and caches are allowed to lag |
| **Durability** | "Reliable" | "An acknowledged link is never lost" → synchronous replication or a durable write before we return 201 |
| **Availability target** | "Always up" | "99.9% for redirects" → about 43 minutes of downtime a month |
| **Security / compliance** | (usually omit) | Only if it changes boxes: "links must not be guessable" → random codes, not sequential |

Pick **three to five**. Say the consistency choice **per feature**, not for the whole system: a bank might take availability for viewing balance history and consistency for moving money.

**The nines, worked.** A 30-day month is 30 × 24 × 60 = 43,200 minutes; a year is 525,600 minutes.

| Target | Downtime allowed per 30-day month | Per year |
|---|---|---|
| 99% | 432 minutes (7.2 hours) | about 3.65 days |
| 99.9% | 43.2 minutes | about 8.8 hours |
| 99.95% | 21.6 minutes | about 4.4 hours |
| 99.99% | 4.3 minutes | about 53 minutes |

The jump from three nines to four is the jump from "an engineer can fix it by hand" to "failover must be automatic". That sentence is why the number is worth saying.

**Scale numbers in about two minutes**, using the ÷10⁵ rule from <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/estimation/`}>Estimation</a> (a day is 86,400 seconds; round to 100,000):

- 100M redirects/day ÷ 10⁵ ≈ **1,000 reads/s** average; assume peak is 3× → **about 3,000/s**.
- 1M new links/day ÷ 10⁵ ≈ **10 writes/s** average, **about 30/s** peak.
- Storage: 1M × 500 B = 500 MB/day → about 180 GB/year → **about 1 TB over five years**.

Then the conclusion, because a number without one was wasted breath: *"One relational database holds five years of links; the read rate is what we'll design around."*

**The bridge sentence** (memorise the shape): *"So to summarise: we're building X and Y at Z scale, prioritising availability over consistency — does that match what you had in mind?"* Then wait for the nod. **That nod is your contract**; every later decision can point back to it.

<Scene title="Interlude · The interview room" mood="night">

The Interviewer, twenty minutes into a different round, asks: *"How would you handle resource-level authorization?"* The candidate launches into tenant isolation — row-level filters by tenant id, separate schemas. It's a good answer. It's an answer to a different question.

</Scene>

**Clarify the layer before you answer — at any point in the round, not just at the start.** "Authorization" alone can mean four different layers: **identity** (who are you — authentication), **tenant** (which customer's data can you see), **role** (what kind of user are you), and **resource** (may *this* user act on *this* document). One clarifying sentence — *"Do you mean which tenant's data, or whether this user may edit this specific document?"* — costs five seconds. Answering the wrong layer costs the whole dive. The same applies to "consistency" (replica lag? transactions? cache staleness?) and "scale" (users? data? requests?).

<Trace title="Scope it" steps={requirements} takeaway="Three features the product can’t live without, an out-of-scope list, and three to five non-functionals that each carry a number or a choice." />

<Reveal q="The interviewer says: “You decide the requirements — I’m interested in how you think.” What do you do?">

Decide quickly and visibly. Propose the three features and the out-of-scope list in one breath, attach numbers to the non-functionals as assumptions (*"I'll assume 100 million redirects a day"*), and ask for a nod. "You decide" is not an invitation to explore every option; it's a test of whether you can scope without help. Spending longer here because nobody stopped you is exactly the 18-minute trap.

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** listing non-functionals as adjectives — "scalable, reliable, maintainable, secure". Every system wants all of them, so naming them changes nothing. If a requirement doesn't carry a number or a choice, it won't shape a single box, and the interviewer knows it.

<h2 id="entities">Chapter 4 · The nouns on the table</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 4" title="The nouns on the table">

Theo is reviewing an access-control design from another team. He points at one arrow. *"A permission belongs to one role?"* he asks. *"So when two roles both need 'export reports', you make two permissions with the same name?"*

Silence. You recognise the bug. You've made it yourself, in a mock, with exactly these two nouns.

</Scene>

**Core entities** are the nouns your system stores and serves. You list them in about two minutes — **names and relationships, not full schemas**. Fields arrive later, written next to the database box when you draw it, because the design decides which fields matter.

For the link shortener: **Link** (short code, long URL, created at, creator) and **User** (if creators are tracked). For Instagram: **User**, **Post**, **Follow**, **Media**. For a booking system: **Event**, **Seat**, **Booking**, **User**.

**The cardinality habit.** For every pair of entities that touch, ask: **"Can each side have many of the other?"**

- Yes, once → one-to-many. A foreign key on the "many" side (a Post has an author_id).
- Yes, twice → **many-to-many. A join table, and you say its name out loud.**
- And when the relationship carries data of its own — when it was created, a status, a role — **the relationship is an entity.** A Follow has a created_at. A Booking has a price and a status.

<Figure caption="Role ↔ Permission is many-to-many: a role grants many permissions, and a permission is granted by many roles. Same for User ↔ Role. Two join tables, named out loud.">
  <Mermaid code={`erDiagram
    USER ||--o{ USER_ROLE : "has"
    ROLE ||--o{ USER_ROLE : "granted via"
    ROLE ||--o{ ROLE_PERMISSION : "grants"
    PERMISSION ||--o{ ROLE_PERMISSION : "granted by"`} />
</Figure>

This is the trap from your own mock history: **Role ↔ Permission modelled as one-to-many when it's many-to-many.** The fix is the habit, not the memory — ask the question for every pair, every time.

<Trace title="Name the nouns" steps={entities} takeaway="Two minutes: nouns, relationships, cardinality. Yes-twice means a join table, named out loud." />

<Reveal q="Why bother with entities at all, when the API step will name the resources anyway?">

Because the entities are where relationships live, and the API can hide them. `POST /users/{id}/follow` looks like a simple endpoint until you notice that Follow is a many-to-many table that will hold billions of rows, needs indexes in both directions, and drives the whole feed. Two minutes of nouns gives you and the interviewer a shared vocabulary, and it is the cheapest place to catch the M:N mistake before it becomes a box.

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** writing full DDL with column types for ten minutes. It feels rigorous and burns the budget, and most of it changes once you see the access patterns.

<h2 id="api">Chapter 5 · The contract</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 5" title="The contract" mood="dusk">

Dev forwards a bug report. A customer on a train tapped "Place order" once. The request reached Kestrel's marketplace, the order was created, and the response died in a tunnel. The app retried. The customer was charged twice.

*"The code was correct,"* Dev says. *"The contract wasn't."*

</Scene>

The API step writes down **how clients talk to your system**: the core endpoints only — one or two per functional requirement — with method, path, request and response. Four minutes.

**REST, resource-first.** Paths name **resources** (plural nouns); the HTTP method is the verb.

| Method | Meaning | Safe? | Idempotent? |
|---|---|---|---|
| `GET` | Read | Yes | Yes |
| `POST` | Create (or a non-idempotent action) | No | **No** |
| `PUT` | Replace at a known URL | No | Yes |
| `PATCH` | Partial update | No | Not by definition |
| `DELETE` | Remove | No | Yes |

(Safe and idempotent are defined by the HTTP specification, RFC 9110. Idempotent means repeating the request has the same effect on the server as sending it once.)

The link shortener's whole API:

```text
POST /urls          { longUrl }          → 201 { shortCode, shortUrl }
GET  /{shortCode}                        → 302 Location: longUrl   (404 if unknown)
```

Three habits that score:

- **Identity comes from the auth token, never the body.** `POST /urls` with `userId` in the body lets any client claim to be anyone. Say: *"The user comes from the session token."*
- **Status codes that carry meaning.** `201 Created` for creation, `404` for unknown, `409 Conflict`, `429 Too Many Requests` when rate-limited. For the redirect: **302** sends every click back to you (so you could count clicks later); **301** is permanent and browsers may cache it, so repeat clicks from the same browser may never reach you. Choose and say why.
- **Not everything is REST.** Server-to-client pushes (chat, live location) want WebSockets or server-sent events; internal service-to-service calls often use gRPC; clients that need flexible shapes may use GraphQL. One sentence each, when the problem calls for it — <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/networking/`}>Networking</a> goes deeper.

**Pagination — any endpoint that returns a list.**

| | Offset (`?limit=20&offset=400`) | Cursor (`?limit=20&cursor=…`) |
|---|---|---|
| How | Skip N rows, return the next page | Return items after the last one seen; the response carries `nextCursor` |
| Inserts while scrolling | Shift every page: duplicates or skipped items | Stable — the cursor names a position in the data |
| Deep pages | The database reads and discards every skipped row: `offset=10000` reads 10,020 rows to return 20 | An index seek to the cursor, then 20 rows |
| Jump to page 40 | Yes | No |
| Use for | Admin tables, small or slowly changing lists with page numbers | Feeds, timelines, anything large or live |

A cursor is usually an opaque, encoded form of the last item's sort key, with a unique tie-breaker so equal timestamps don't lose rows:

```text
GET /users/me/feed?limit=20&cursor=eyJ0IjoiMjAyNi0xMC0wN1QwOTowMDowMFoiLCJpZCI6OTg3fQ
-- server decodes (created_at, id) of the last item seen, then:
SELECT ... WHERE (created_at, id) < (:t, :id) ORDER BY created_at DESC, id DESC LIMIT 20
```

**Idempotency keys — any create that must not happen twice.** A retried `POST` creates a second resource. For a paste that's harmless clutter; for a payment or an order it's a double charge. The standard fix:

1. The client generates a unique key (a UUID) **per logical operation** and sends it in an `Idempotency-Key` header. Retries reuse the same key.
2. The server records the key atomically (a unique constraint) before doing the work, then stores the result against it.
3. A retry with a known key gets the **stored result replayed**, not a second execution.
4. A retry that arrives **while the first is still running** is told to retry later (an IETF draft suggests `409 Conflict`); a reused key with a **different body** is a client error (the same draft suggests `422`).
5. Keys expire after a window — Stripe documents keeping them for at least 24 hours; check current docs for any provider's exact policy.

<Figure caption="The train-tunnel bug, fixed. The second POST carries the same key, so the server replays the first result instead of creating a second order.">
  <Mermaid code={`sequenceDiagram
    participant App
    participant API as Orders API
    participant DB
    App->>API: POST /orders · Idempotency-Key k1
    API->>DB: INSERT key k1 (unique), create order 77
    DB-->>API: ok
    API--xApp: 201 order 77 (lost in tunnel)
    App->>API: retry POST /orders · Idempotency-Key k1
    API->>DB: key k1 exists → stored result
    API-->>App: 201 order 77 (replayed, no new order)`} />
</Figure>

A client-chosen id in the URL — `PUT /orders/{clientOrderId}` — is the same idea expressed differently: PUT to a known URL is idempotent by definition. <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-contention/`}>Contention</a> covers idempotency end to end, including exactly-once effects across services.

<Trace title="Write the contract" steps={api} takeaway="Nouns in paths, verbs in methods, identity from the token, cursors for live lists, idempotency keys for creates that must not repeat." />

<Reveal q="Do you need pagination and idempotency keys on every API in every interview?">

No — and saying where you *don't* need them is part of the signal. A link shortener has no list endpoint, so no pagination; say so. A duplicate short link from a retried create is harmless, so an idempotency key is optional; say so. The point is to show you checked: *"No list endpoints yet, so no pagination; creates are harmless to repeat, so I'll skip idempotency keys — they'd matter if we charged money."*

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** verbs in paths (`/createUrl`, `/getFeed`), `userId` in request bodies, and `GET` requests with side effects. Each takes a second to avoid and each tells the interviewer you haven't built many public APIs.

<h2 id="high-level">Chapter 6 · Boxes that earn their place</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 6" title="Boxes that earn their place" mood="dawn">

In your second mock, you start the design by drawing a load balancer, a cache, a queue, a CDN and four database shards — the shape of every system diagram you've ever studied. Mara points at the queue. *"What's in it?"*

You don't know yet. Nothing in the requirements produces a message.

*"Then it doesn't exist yet,"* she says, and erases it. *"Build what the requirements need. One at a time."*

</Scene>

**The method: satisfy one functional requirement at a time.** Draw the smallest set of boxes that makes the first requirement work, then **trace a request through every box you drew, out loud**. Then the next requirement, reusing boxes where you can. When every functional requirement has been traced, the high-level design is done — and it is *correct* before it is *fast*. Speed, scale and resilience are what the deep dives are for.

**Trace out loud** — the sentence for the create path: *"The POST hits the load balancer, which routes to a URL service instance. The service generates a short code, inserts the code and long URL into Postgres, and returns 201 with the short URL."* And the redirect: *"GET with the code hits the service, which looks up the code — cache first, database on a miss — and returns a 302 with the long URL."*

**Every box earns its existence in one sentence.** A box you can't justify is a box the interviewer will delete in front of you.

<Figure caption="The link shortener's high-level design. Numbers trace the create path, letters the redirect. Nothing here exists without a requirement behind it.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    C["Client"] -->|"1 · POST /urls"| LB["Load balancer"]
    C -->|"A · GET /abc123"| LB
    LB --> S["URL service\nstateless × N"]
    S -->|"2 · INSERT code, long_url"| DB[("Postgres\nurls: code PK")]
    S -->|"B · GET url:abc123"| R[("Redis")]
    S -.->|"C · miss → SELECT by code"| DB`} />
</Figure>

**Worked numbers that decide the boxes.** From Chapter 3: about 3,000 redirects/s at peak, about 30 creates/s, about 1 TB over five years. So: **one relational database** (1 TB fits one machine for years; sharding is not a day-one problem), **stateless service instances behind a load balancer** (add instances to add throughput), and a **cache** for the read path. Is the cache load-bearing at 3,000 reads/s? A primary-key lookup on an indexed table is cheap, and a well-provisioned database plus a read replica can very likely serve that rate — benchmark it rather than assume. So the cache is there for **latency headroom and viral links**, not because the database would fall over. Saying that distinction is the point of doing the arithmetic.

**Decomposition from scratch — when the problem isn't one you've rehearsed.** You don't need a remembered architecture. You need a procedure:

1. **For each functional requirement, name its write path and its read path.** What gets stored, and what gets fetched?
2. **Ask what must happen synchronously** (before the user gets a response) **and what can happen later.** Anything "later" — sending notifications, transcoding video, updating counters — goes behind a queue. Now the queue has a reason.
3. **Put each entity in the store that fits its access pattern.** Relational for related records and transactions; key-value for lookups by one key at very high rates; object storage for large blobs; a search index for text queries.
4. **Start with one service.** Split a service only for a reason you can say: **different scaling** (one path is CPU-heavy, the other isn't), **failure isolation** (a slow dependency shouldn't take down the core path), **data ownership**, or **different release cadence/teams**.

| Signal in the requirements | Box it earns |
|---|---|
| Large files (photos, video, documents) | Object storage + CDN; upload directly via pre-signed URL |
| Work the user doesn't wait for | Queue + workers |
| Server must push to the client | WebSocket / SSE gateway |
| Text search, ranking | Search index fed from the primary store |
| Reads far exceed writes, same items repeat | Cache (with its four promises) |
| "Nearby" queries | Geospatial index |

Some problems are mostly a data pipeline (a web crawler, an ad-click aggregator). For those, replace the API sketch with a **data-flow sketch** — each stage from input to output — and design the stages.

<Trace title="Draw only what’s needed" steps={highLevel} takeaway="One functional requirement at a time, traced out loud. Split services and add boxes only for a reason you can say." />

<Reveal q="Decompose Google Calendar from scratch — three features, the entities, two endpoints, and the two deep dives you'd pick.">

**Features:** create an event (possibly recurring), invite people and let them respond, view my calendar for a date range. Out of scope: rooms, reminders, sharing whole calendars.

**Entities:** User, Calendar, Event, and **Attendance** — users and events are many-to-many (an event has many attendees; a user attends many events), and the relationship carries data (response status), so it's an entity. A recurring event stores **one row with a recurrence rule** (the iCalendar RRULE format, RFC 5545), not one row per occurrence — "every Monday forever" has no last row.

**API:** `POST /events` (body includes the recurrence rule and attendee emails); `GET /calendars/{id}/events?start=…&end=…` returns occurrences in the range; `PUT /events/{id}/attendance/me` to respond.

**Deep dives:** (1) **Recurrence expansion** — expand rules into occurrences at read time for the requested range, and store exceptions ("this Monday moved to Tuesday") as override rows. (2) **Time zones** — store a recurring event's wall-clock time plus its IANA time zone (e.g. Europe/London) rather than a fixed UTC instant, so the 9 a.m. meeting stays at 9 a.m. local time across daylight-saving changes. Conflict detection (free/busy) is a strong third if time allows.

Nothing here required having seen Google Calendar designed. It required the procedure.

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** drawing the "final" architecture first — every box you've seen in every diagram. Unjustified boxes cost time now and credibility later, and you skip the step that proves the design actually meets the requirements.

<h2 id="deep-dives">Chapter 7 · Where offers are won</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 7" title="Where offers are won" mood="night">

Third mock. You reach the end of the high-level design at minute 25, exactly on the checkpoint, and feel the relief of a finished diagram.

*"Good,"* Mara says. *"Now — what's hard about this?"*

You look at the boxes. They all work. That's the problem: a design where everything works is a design that hasn't been questioned yet.

</Scene>

The high-level design proves the system **works**. Deep dives prove it **meets the non-functional requirements** — and they're where interviewers find out how deep your knowledge goes. In a 45-minute round you have about 13 minutes: **two dives, maybe three.**

**How to choose the dives.** Candidates come from four places; pick the two or three with the most risk:

1. **Each quantified non-functional the high-level design hasn't proven.** "p99 under 100 ms at 3,000/s" is a dive until you've shown how. This is why vague NFRs hurt: they generate no dives.
2. **The problem's signature hard part.** Feeds → fan-out. Ticketing → contention for the same seat. Ride-hailing → geospatial matching. Chat → real-time delivery. The pattern lessons in this course are each one of these.
3. **The most loaded or most fragile component** in your diagram — whatever has the highest QPS, the largest data, or no redundancy.
4. **The interviewer's hints.** If they've poked at something, that's dive one, whatever you'd planned.

**The shape of every dive** — five moves, about six minutes:

<Figure caption="Every deep dive has the same shape. The last move is the one candidates skip, and it's the one that scores.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    P["1 · Name the problem\none sentence"] --> O["2 · Two or three options"]
    O --> T["3 · Tradeoff of each\none line"]
    T --> N["4 · The numbers\nthat decide"]
    N --> C["5 · Commit\nand update the diagram"]`} />
</Figure>

**Worked dive: unique short codes.**

1. **Problem:** "Two concurrent creates must never get the same code, and codes shouldn't be guessable in order."
2. **Options:** (a) a central counter, base62-encoded; (b) random base62 codes with retry on conflict; (c) a hash of the long URL, truncated.
3. **Tradeoffs:** (a) no collisions, but sequential codes are guessable and a single counter is a single point of failure unless it hands out ranges; (b) unguessable and needs no coordination, but needs a uniqueness check; (c) the same URL always gets the same code (nice), but truncation still collides and two users shortening one URL share one link.
4. **Numbers:** 7 base62 characters give 62⁷ ≈ 3.5 trillion codes. Five years at 1M/day is about 1.8 billion links — 0.05% of the space. So a fresh random code collides about **1 time in 2,000**. Note that what matters is **how full the code space is**, not the write rate.
5. **Commit:** *"Random 7-character base62 with a unique index on the code column and retry on conflict. At 0.05% fill, a retry happens about once per 2,000 creates, and there's no counter to fail."*

**The hand-wave rule.** A cache is not a design decision until it has a key shape, a TTL with a reason, and an invalidation story — and, if it's load-bearing, a failure plan. "We'll cache in Redis" scores zero. *"Cache `url:{code}` → long URL, cache-aside, TTL 24 hours since mappings never change, delete the key if a link is deleted; a viral link gets a one-second in-process cache on each server so one Redis shard doesn't melt"* scores. <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/caching/`}>Caching</a> is the full treatment.

| Dive length | What it buys | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| 2 minutes each, six topics | Looks broad | No decision lands; reads as a tour |
| 6 minutes each, two topics | Two committed decisions with numbers | Fine — this is the target |
| 13 minutes on one topic | Real depth | Acceptable if the interviewer is driving it; otherwise you've skipped other risks |

<Trace title="Choose and finish the dives" steps={deepDives} takeaway="Dives answer the requirements the design hasn’t proven yet. Two finished dives beat six started ones; every dive ends in a commit." />

<Reveal q="You planned to dive into code generation, but the interviewer asks: “What happens if Redis goes down?” Do you finish your plan first?">

No. Their question is now dive one. Answer it with the same shape: the problem (*"if Redis is down, every redirect hits Postgres"*), the numbers (*"about 3,000 reads/s at peak — a replica pair can likely absorb that, so the cache isn't load-bearing at today's scale; a viral spike might not be"*), and the commitment (*"replicated Redis with automatic failover, and a circuit breaker so a dead cache fails fast rather than adding a timeout to every request"*). Then: *"With the time left, I'd like to cover code generation."*

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** a dive that ends with "so there are a few ways to do this." The interviewer writes down the decision you made. If you made none, there is nothing to write.

<h2 id="wrap-up">Chapter 8 · The last three minutes</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 8" title="The last three minutes" mood="dusk">

Dev reads design docs from the bottom up. *"The last section tells me if the author has ever been paged,"* he says. *"Either it says what breaks first and how we'd know — or it says 'future work: scale'."*

</Scene>

Wrap-up is short and disproportionately memorable, because it's the last thing the interviewer hears before writing notes. In about three minutes:

1. **Recap against the contract.** "The design meets the three features; redirects stay under 100 ms through the cache; codes are unique by index."
2. **The bottleneck at 10×.** "At 30,000 redirects a second, the database read path is first — more replicas and the cache absorb it; at 10× storage we'd shard by short code."
3. **Failure modes.** What breaks, and what users see: "If a Postgres primary fails, creates pause for the failover window; redirects keep working from replicas and cache."
4. **What you'd monitor.** Specific: p99 redirect latency, cache hit ratio, 404 rate (broken or scraped links), create error rate, replica lag.
5. **What you'd do with more time.** One or two items from the out-of-scope list: "analytics via a click-event stream; abuse detection on creates."

The one-breath version from your old card still works: *"The bottleneck at 10× is X, I'd shard by Y, and I'd want dashboards on Z before launch."*

**Then hand back the floor.** If the round reserves time for your questions, use it — one or two real questions about how the team works or what's hard in their systems. Don't spend it re-defending the design.

**Catch the trap:** "Future work: make it scalable." That's the sentence Dev reads as "has never been paged". Name the first thing that breaks, at what number, and how you'd know.

<h2 id="tradeoffs">Chapter 9 · Saying “because”</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 9" title="Saying “because”">

In a Kestrel design review, a new engineer presents two databases side by side with pros and cons, perfectly balanced, and then stops. The room waits. Mara finally asks: *"So which one?"* The engineer says, *"It depends."*

*"It always depends,"* Mara says. *"Tell me on what — and then pick."*

</Scene>

A tradeoff is narrated well when it has **four parts**:

1. **The choice:** "Cursor pagination, not offset."
2. **The reason, tied to a requirement or a number:** "because the feed changes while people scroll, and they scroll deep."
3. **The cost you accept:** "We give up jumping to page N."
4. **The condition that would flip it:** "For an admin table with page numbers, I'd use offset."

The fourth part is what separates a senior answer from a rehearsed one. It shows you understand the decision as a function of the requirements, not as a favourite.

**"It depends" is half a sentence.** Finish it: *"It depends on whether reads must see the latest write. Here they don't — a new post can take a few seconds to reach followers — so I'll take the asynchronous replica."*

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "NoSQL scales better." | "Key-value, because every read is by one key at 3,000/s and we never join; we lose ad-hoc queries, which no requirement asks for." |
| "Both have pros and cons." | "Random codes over a counter: no coordination and no guessability; the cost is a uniqueness check, about one retry per 2,000 creates." |
| "We'll use Kafka." | "A durable log between upload and transcoding, because transcoding takes minutes and must survive worker crashes; a simpler queue would also work at this rate." |

Notice the last row admits a simpler option would work. **Saying when the fancy tool isn't needed is a strength, not a weakness.**

<Reveal q="The interviewer pushes back on a choice you made: “Why not just use the other option?” Should you switch?">

Not by reflex — and not by digging in either. Restate the requirement that drove the choice and ask whether it has changed: *"I picked asynchronous replicas because reads can be a few seconds stale. If you need every read to see the latest write, I'd switch to reading from the primary for that path, and pay for it in primary load."* If they've given you new information, switching is the right answer and you should say why. If they're testing your conviction, the requirement-based reason is what they wanted to hear.

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** "It depends" with no ending, or a pros-and-cons table with no pick. Both sound balanced; both leave the interviewer with nothing to write under "judgment".

<h2 id="dont-know">Chapter 10 · Three honest words</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 10" title="Three honest words" mood="night">

The Interviewer leans back. *"What's Kafka's default maximum message size?"*

The candidate doesn't know. You can see the choice play out in their face: guess a number, or say the three words that feel like failure.

</Scene>

Not knowing a fact is normal; **bluffing it is the failure.** An interviewer who catches one confident wrong number starts doubting every confident number you've given. The script:

1. **Say it plainly, once.** "I don't remember that."
2. **Say what you do know, or reason from first principles.** "I know brokers cap message size, and large messages hurt throughput and memory."
3. **Make the design independent of the missing fact.** "So I'd keep events small and put large payloads in object storage, sending a reference."
4. **Say how you'd find out.** "I'd check the broker's docs before committing the limit."

This works because most interview questions about specific product limits are really asking **whether your design depends on things you haven't checked.** Showing that it doesn't is the answer.

| Not knowing this is fine | Not knowing this hurts |
|---|---|
| A product's default config value | What an index is and why a query needs one |
| The exact API of a particular cloud service | What replication lag is, and when it bites |
| How one company built one feature internally | Why a single counter is a single point of failure |
| An exact latency figure for a specific vendor | Orders of magnitude: memory vs network vs disk |

**Catch the trap:** inventing a vendor limit or a "how X company does it" fact to sound experienced. Say "I believe" with a hedge only when you genuinely believe it, and never let the design rest on it.

<h2 id="whiteboard">Chapter 11 · Clean ink</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 11" title="Clean ink" mood="dawn">

Mara photographs the whiteboard after your fourth mock and puts the photo next to one from your first. The first looks like a crime scene: arrows crossing arrows, a database drawn three times, requirements erased to make room. The fourth has a corner nobody touched all round.

*"The interviewer writes notes from this,"* she says. *"Afterwards, it's all they have."*

</Scene>

**Diagram hygiene** — on a physical whiteboard or a virtual one (Excalidraw-style tools are common in remote rounds):

- **Pin a corner.** Functional requirements, the non-functional numbers, and the API stay visible all round. You'll point at them in deep dives ("this is for the 100 ms requirement"). Never erase them for space.
- **Flow left to right.** Clients on the left, data stores on the right, services between. Readers expect requests to move in one direction.
- **One diagram, evolved.** Don't redraw for each deep dive; change the existing one, and mark what the dive added (a different colour, or a star).
- **Label arrows with what moves.** "POST /urls", "INSERT", "publish upload event" — not bare lines.
- **Number the flows.** 1-2-3 for the write path, A-B-C for the read path, so you can say "at step B…" without pointing.
- **Consistent shapes.** Cylinders for data stores, a distinct shape for queues, plain boxes for services. Write the key field or partition key next to each store.
- **Numbers next to boxes.** "3K reads/s", "1 TB / 5 yrs" — the arithmetic stays visible and keeps justifying the boxes.
- **Virtual boards:** set up a few reusable shapes in the first minute, and zoom so the interviewer sees what you're pointing at.

<h2 id="recovery">Chapter 12 · The wrong turn</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 12" title="The wrong turn" mood="storm">

Your first real Kestrel design review: Stories, photos that vanish after 24 hours. You present confidently. Iris adds, mid-slide, *"Oh, and also — viewers should see who else viewed a story."* You absorb it. You're doing well.

Then Theo asks, gently: *"Your ID counter for story ids — what happens when its node dies?"*

The room goes quiet. You realise the answer is: every upload in the company stops. You feel the pull to defend it. Or to start over. Mara catches your eye and mouths one word: *forward.*

</Scene>

Every candidate takes a wrong turn eventually. **How you recover often scores better than never having made the mistake**, because recovering is exactly what senior engineers do in real reviews.

**Notice it.** Interviewers rarely say "that's wrong". They ask a question. Learn the signals:

| The interviewer says | It usually means |
|---|---|
| "How would this handle [X]?" | X is a requirement you haven't met, or the dive they want |
| "Are you sure about that?" | Re-check it out loud — something is likely off |
| "What happens if [component] fails?" | You have a single point of failure or no failure plan |
| "Let's assume [X] is solved." | Stop spending time on X; move on |
| "Interesting — let's move on." | You've said enough there, or you're stuck; either way, move |
| "What if we had 10× the users?" | They want a scaling dive |

**Recover in four moves, in about fifteen seconds:**

1. **Name it:** "That's a single point of failure."
2. **Own it, once:** "Good catch." (Not three apologies.)
3. **Fix forward with the smallest change:** "I'll switch to range allocation: each server leases a block of 1,000 ids from a replicated store, so losing a server wastes at most one unused block."
4. **Update the diagram, and re-check what it touches:** "That doesn't change the API or the read path."

<Figure caption="Fix forward: one box changes. The service keeps its interface; the single point of failure becomes a replicated store that hands out blocks.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    subgraph BEFORE["Before"]
      direction LR
      S1["Upload service"] -->|"next id, every upload"| CTR["ID counter\none node ✗"]
    end
    subgraph AFTER["After"]
      direction LR
      S2["Upload service\nholds a block of 1,000"] -->|"new block when empty"| KV[("Replicated store\nnext free block")]
    end`} />
</Figure>

**Recovering from a bigger wrong turn** — say you chose a store that can't support a query you now need. Don't restart. Say what changes and what doesn't: *"This query needs a secondary index the key-value store doesn't give us. I'll keep the store for the main path and add a search index fed from it for this query."* A local fix to a global design is almost always available, and it's almost always what a real team would do.

**When you're simply stuck** — no idea how to proceed — say what you're trying to achieve and think out loud: *"I need the read path under 100 ms, and right now it does two sequential lookups. Options are to denormalise, to cache, or to precompute…"* Interviewers often help a candidate who is visibly reasoning. They can't help a silent one.

<Trace title="Hold your ground honestly" steps={pressure} takeaway="Choice, reason, cost, flip condition. Admit gaps in one sentence and design around them. Own a mistake in one sentence and fix forward. Say every skip out loud." />

**Catch the trap:** defending a flaw the interviewer has found. They found it; they know it's there. Defending it turns a mistake into a judgment problem.

<h2 id="walkthrough">Chapter 13 · Pastebin, out loud</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 13" title="Pastebin, out loud" mood="night">

Final Friday. Mara sets the phone face-down. *"Design a Pastebin,"* she says. *"I'm not going to help you. Show me the clock."*

You write four numbers in the corner of the board — 9 · 15 · 25 · 38 — and pick up the marker.

</Scene>

What follows is a 45-minute round, compressed to its spoken lines. Timestamps are the clock; read the lines aloud once.

**0:03 · Roadmap and requirements**

> **You:** "Before I draw anything, here's how I'd like to use our time: about six minutes on requirements and scale, a couple on the core entities and the API, a simple design that works, then two or three deep dives on the hardest parts, and a short wrap-up. Stop me any time you want to go somewhere else. Would you like capacity estimates up front, or as they become relevant?"
>
> **Interviewer:** "As relevant is fine."
>
> **You:** "Functional: users can create a paste of text and get a short link; anyone with the link can read it; a paste can expire after a chosen time, or never. Out of scope: user accounts, editing, syntax highlighting, search, analytics, custom links — tell me if any of those matter. I'll assume pastes are anonymous and capped at 1 MB."

**0:05 · Non-functionals with numbers**

> **You:** "Scale: I'll assume a million new pastes a day and ten reads per paste — ten million reads a day. With the divide-by-ten-to-the-fifth rule that's about 10 writes a second and 100 reads a second, roughly 30 and 300 at a 3× peak. Small. Average paste about 10 KB. Reads should be fast — p99 under 200 ms. Reads favour availability. An acknowledged paste must never be lost. And links shouldn't be guessable, because people paste things they think are private."
>
> "Storage is the interesting number: a million pastes at 10 KB is 10 GB a day, about 3.65 TB a year, roughly 18 TB over five years. The metadata is tiny — about 200 bytes a row is 200 MB a day, around 73 GB a year. Bandwidth at 300 reads a second times 10 KB is 3 MB a second — a non-issue, so I'll skip it."

**0:08 · The bridge**

> **You:** "So: create, read and expire text pastes; about 300 reads a second at peak; 18 TB of content over five years but well under a terabyte of metadata; durable writes and unguessable links. The numbers say storage shape matters more than throughput. Does that match what you had in mind?"
>
> **Interviewer:** "Yes."

**0:09 · Core entities**

> **You:** "Two nouns. A **Paste** — its id, an expiry time, a size, a created time, and a pointer to its content. And the **content** itself, the text. No users, so no relationships to model yet — if we added accounts, User to Paste would be one-to-many."

**0:11 · API**

> **You:** "Two endpoints."

```text
POST /pastes   { content, expiresIn? }   → 201 { id, url }        (413 if over 1 MB)
GET  /pastes/{id}                        → 200 { content, createdAt, expiresAt }
                                           404 if unknown or expired
```

> **You:** "No list endpoint, so no pagination. A retried create just makes a second paste — harmless — so I'll skip idempotency keys; they'd matter if this cost money."

**0:15 · High-level design, one requirement at a time**

> **You:** "Create first. The client posts to a load balancer, which routes to a stateless paste service. Because we'll store 18 TB of mostly cold text, I'll split content from metadata: the service writes the content to object storage under the paste id, then inserts a metadata row — id, object key, size, created and expiry times — into a relational database, and returns 201. Content goes first, so a metadata row never points at missing content."
>
> "Read: the service looks up the metadata row by id, checks expiry, fetches the content from object storage, and returns it."
>
> "Expiry: rows carry an expiry time, and the read path treats an expired paste as not found, so correctness doesn't depend on cleanup. A background sweeper deletes expired rows and their objects in batches."

<Figure caption="Pastebin's high-level design. Content and metadata are split because 18 TB of cold text would bloat the database and its backups; the metadata stays well under a terabyte.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    C["Client"] -->|"1 · POST /pastes"| LB["Load balancer"]
    C -->|"A · GET /pastes/id"| LB
    LB --> S["Paste service\nstateless × N"]
    S -->|"2 · PUT content"| OS[("Object storage\npaste bodies · ~18 TB / 5 yrs")]
    S -->|"3 · INSERT metadata"| DB[("Relational DB\npastes: id PK, expires_at")]
    S -->|"B · lookup id"| DB
    S -->|"C · GET content"| OS
    SW["Expiry sweeper\nbatch job"] -.->|"delete expired"| DB
    SW -.-> OS`} />
</Figure>

> **You:** "Why not keep the text in the database? At 10 KB a paste that's defensible — but it would put 18 TB into the database over five years, with backups and replicas to match, for data that's mostly read a few times and then never. Object storage is cheaper per GB and keeps the database small. That's the tradeoff, and I'd flip it if pastes were tiny or we needed to query inside them."

**0:25 · Deep dive 1 — paste ids**

> **You:** "The hard parts are ids, expiry at scale, and hot pastes. Ids first, because the unguessable requirement constrains them. Options: a counter — sequential, so guessable, and a single point of failure; or random base62. I'll take random, 8 characters: 62 to the 8th is about 218 trillion. After five years we'd have about 1.8 billion pastes, so a random guess finds a real paste about once in 120,000 tries — and I'd rate-limit reads per IP so scraping by guessing is impractical. A new id collides with an existing one about 1 time in 120,000, so a unique index plus retry handles it."

**0:31 · Deep dive 2 — expiry**

> **You:** "Correctness comes from the read-path check, so the sweeper only reclaims space. It runs every few minutes, deletes expired rows in batches of a few thousand using an index on expires_at, and deletes the matching objects. If it falls behind, users see nothing wrong — only storage cost grows. I'd alert on the sweeper's backlog. Object stores offer lifecycle rules that delete objects by age, but a single age rule only fits if every paste had a bounded expiry — ours can live forever — so the sweeper owns deletion. It also cleans up any orphaned object left by a create whose metadata insert failed."

**0:35 · Deep dive 3 — a paste goes viral**

> **You:** "Pastes are immutable, which makes caching easy. Cache-aside on `paste:{id}` holding content and metadata; TTL of an hour, or the time until the paste expires if that's sooner, because an expired paste must not be served from cache; no invalidation needed otherwise, since content never changes. Is the cache load-bearing? At 300 reads a second, no — the database and object store handle that without it — so a cache outage only costs latency. For a paste that goes truly viral, I'd put a CDN in front of the read endpoint with the same expiry-aware max-age."

**0:38 · Wrap-up**

> **You:** "To recap: create, read and expire are all served; reads are a key lookup plus an object fetch, comfortably under 200 ms, with a cache for hot pastes. At 10×, the first pressure is storage cost — 180 TB of content over five years — so I'd add compression and tiered storage for old pastes; the metadata, around 730 GB a year, would eventually want partitioning by id. If the database primary fails, creates pause for the failover window while reads continue from replicas. I'd monitor p99 read latency, create error rate, sweeper backlog and 404 rate. With more time, I'd add accounts and abuse detection on creates."

**0:41.** Mara turns the phone over. Four minutes to spare for questions.

**Check the numbers yourself:** 1M pastes ÷ 10⁵ = 10 writes/s; 10M reads ÷ 10⁵ = 100 reads/s; ×3 peak = 30 and 300. Content 1M × 10 KB = 10 GB/day × 365 = 3.65 TB/year × 5 ≈ 18 TB. Metadata 1M × 200 B = 200 MB/day × 365 = 73 GB/year. 62⁸ = 218,340,105,584,896 ≈ 2.18 × 10¹⁴; 5 years × 365 × 1M ≈ 1.825 × 10⁹; 2.18 × 10¹⁴ ÷ 1.825 × 10⁹ ≈ 120,000. At 10×, content is 180 TB over five years and metadata 730 GB a year.

<Reveal q="Which step of the walkthrough would you most likely have skipped silently — and what would it have cost?">

For most people it's the two "we don't need it" sentences in the API step — no pagination, no idempotency keys — and the "bandwidth is a non-issue" sentence. They take five seconds each. Leaving them unsaid makes the interviewer wonder whether you considered them; saying them earns credit for judgment. The other common silent skip is the read-path expiry check: without saying it, the sweeper looks like the only thing keeping expired pastes hidden, and the interviewer will ask what happens when it falls behind.

</Reveal>

<h2 id="interview-script">Say it in the room</h2>

<Scene title="The interview room" mood="night">

The Interviewer finishes the introductions, reads the prompt — *"Design a system that lets users share short-lived text snippets"* — and looks up. A year ago you'd have started asking questions until someone stopped you. Today you say how the next forty minutes will go.

</Scene>

**The opening 90 seconds** (adapt the nouns; the shape is fixed):

> "Before I draw anything, here's how I'd like to use our time. About six minutes on requirements — the core features, what's out of scope, and the non-functionals with rough numbers. Then the core entities and the API, a few minutes. Then a simple design that meets every feature, traced end to end. Then the part I'd most like to spend time on: two or three deep dives into whatever is hardest here, each ending in a decision. I'll finish with bottlenecks and what I'd monitor. Interrupt me any time you'd like to go somewhere else — and would you like capacity estimates up front, or as they become relevant?
>
> So, requirements. The core features are: users can create a snippet and get a link; anyone with the link can read it; snippets expire. Out of scope unless you want them: accounts, editing, search, analytics. For non-functionals I'll assume a million new snippets a day, reads ten times that, p99 reads under 200 milliseconds, availability over consistency for reads, no acknowledged snippet ever lost, and links that can't be guessed. Does that match what you had in mind?"

**Five follow-ups you will get:**

<Reveal q="1 · “Skip the requirements — I’ll just tell you what to build.”">

Great — that's minutes saved. Read their list back in one sentence, then spend thirty seconds attaching numbers to the two or three non-functionals that will matter (*"How many users, roughly? And is it read-heavy?"*). Then go straight to entities. Don't re-open their scope; do make sure you have numbers, because your deep dives will need them.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="2 · “Why didn’t you do a full capacity estimate first?”">

*"I estimate when a number is about to decide something — I did the scale numbers with the requirements because they set the design's shape. Happy to do the full sweep now."* Then do it: QPS with peak, storage over five years, bandwidth (usually "a non-issue, skipping"), memory for the hot set — each ending in a conclusion sentence. Some interviewers simply prefer numbers first; adapt without arguing.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="3 · “We have ten minutes left and you haven’t talked about X.”">

Don't apologise and don't rush everything. Give X a compressed dive — the problem in one sentence, two options, the deciding number, the commitment — in about four minutes. Then use two or three minutes on wrap-up. Say the plan: *"Let me do X properly in four minutes, then wrap up."* Showing you can re-plan under time pressure is itself signal.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="4 · “What would break first at ten times the traffic?”">

Answer with a component, a number and a fix: *"At 10×, reads are 3,000 a second — still fine with the cache. The first real pressure is storage: 180 TB of content over five years, so compression and tiered storage for old pastes, and partitioning the metadata by id once it passes a few terabytes."* Then how you'd know: the metric you'd watch and the threshold that triggers the change.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="5 · “Earlier you said X — doesn’t that conflict with Y?”">

If it does, recover forward: name the conflict, own it once, make the smallest change that resolves it, and update the diagram (*"You're right — the cache TTL could serve an expired paste. I'll cap the TTL at the time until expiry."*). If it doesn't, explain calmly why, pointing at the requirement in your pinned corner. Either way, don't defend by reflex: re-check out loud first.

</Reveal>

## Five mistakes to leave behind

1. **Eighteen minutes on requirements.** Six, with numbers, then the bridge — whether or not you feel finished.
2. **Silent skips.** Every skip gets a sentence: "I'll assume…", "non-issue, skipping", "no list endpoint, so no pagination".
3. **Drawing the final architecture first.** One functional requirement at a time, traced, every box justified.
4. **Dives that end in a menu.** Every dive ends in a commitment, with the number that decided it.
5. **Defending the wrong turn.** Name it, own it, fix forward.
