<h2 id="prologue">Prologue · A number is not an answer</h2>

Picture a design review where someone has already drawn a forty-node Cassandra ring. Nobody can say where forty came from. One line of arithmetic would have settled it: the app stores about 5 GB of new data a day, under 2 TB a year. That fits on one Postgres primary, so the ring is wasted.

Interviewers do not care whether you land on 11,600 QPS or 12,000. They care whether your numbers **end in design decisions**. Every estimate must finish with a sentence like:

> "About 12K read QPS, so a single Postgres will struggle, so a cache tier in front."

**No conclusion, no credit.** The arithmetic exists to make one of a handful of decisions: one box or many, cache or no cache, CDN or origin, shard now or later, synchronous or queued.

**How to read the numbers in this lesson.** Two labels appear throughout:

- **Rule of thumb** means a widely used round number (86,400 seconds in a day, 2¹⁰ ≈ 10³, memory about 100 ns). Safe to quote, but still approximate.
- **Assumption** means a number chosen for this example (50M daily users, 3x peak, 1 KB per post). In an interview you pick it, say it aloud, and accept a correction.

Two slips cause most bad estimates, and both are 1000x errors, not 10% ones:

- **The unit slip.** GB and TB confused, or one rung of the unit ladder skipped. Every conclusion after it is wrong. Chapter 1 is the cure.
- **Stale numbers.** Reusing the previous problem's (or previous axis's) numbers because they are still on the whiteboard. Chapter 15 is the cure.

Two more are about behaviour: **silent skips** (Chapter 16) and **pacing**: when time is short, compute only what decides something.

**The four axes, in this order:**

| Axis | Question it answers | Design decision it feeds |
|------|--------------------|--------------------------|
| **QPS** | How many requests per second, at peak, reads versus writes? | App server count, cache or not, read replicas, load balancer |
| **Storage** | How big does the data get over the years we plan for? | One database or shards, retention, object storage |
| **Bandwidth** | Bytes per second in and out? | CDN or not, direct-to-storage uploads (often: *decide what to ignore*) |
| **Memory** | Does the hot working set fit in RAM? | One cache node or a cluster, and *why* a cluster |

Three more questions turn those into machines: **how many servers** (per-server throughput and Little's law), **how available** (nines, in series and in parallel), and **how many shards** (storage or throughput, whichever forces the split first).

<h2 id="powers">Chapter 1 · Climb one rung at a time</h2>

Estimation is mostly multiplying big round numbers. Stop thinking in digits and think in **powers of ten**: write each number as *mantissa × 10ⁿ*, multiply the mantissas, add the exponents, then name the result.

**Why it matters.** A real incident: a table used a signed 32-bit primary key, "basically infinite" at 2.1 billion. At a few million inserts a day it ran out in about a year and stopped accepting writes. The person who knew 2³¹ ≈ 2.1 × 10⁹ would have caught it in a design review.

**The ladder.** Every rung is ×1000:

<Figure caption="Each rung is 10³. Say every rung out loud as you climb; never jump two in your head.">
  <ArrayStrip values={['B', 'KB', 'MB', 'GB', 'TB', 'PB']} join="→" indices={false} pointers={[{ at: 0, label: '10⁰' }, { at: 2, label: '10⁶', tone: 'blue' }, { at: 3, label: '10⁹' }, { at: 5, label: '10¹⁵', tone: 'blue' }]} tones={{ 3: 'hot' }} />
</Figure>

| Power of two | Exact | ≈ Power of ten | Name | Binary/decimal gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2¹⁰ | 1,024 | 10³ | thousand · KB | 2.4% |
| 2²⁰ | 1,048,576 | 10⁶ | million · MB | 4.9% |
| 2³⁰ | 1,073,741,824 | 10⁹ | billion · GB | 7.4% |
| 2⁴⁰ | ≈ 1.10 × 10¹² | 10¹² | trillion · TB | 10% |
| 2⁵⁰ | ≈ 1.13 × 10¹⁵ | 10¹⁵ | quadrillion · PB | 12.6% |

**2¹⁰ ≈ 10³ is the bridge between the two worlds.** Strictly, a kibibyte (KiB) is 1,024 bytes and a kilobyte (KB) is 1,000. The gap grows with each rung but is only about 13% at a petabyte, far smaller than the error in any user-count guess. Use powers of ten and move on.

Powers of two that cause real outages:

- **2³¹ − 1 ≈ 2.1 × 10⁹**: the largest signed 32-bit integer (Java `int`, a Postgres `integer` column).
- **2³² ≈ 4.3 × 10⁹**: the unsigned 32-bit range, and the number of IPv4 addresses.
- **2⁶³ ≈ 9.2 × 10¹⁸**: the largest signed 64-bit integer. Use 64-bit ids and you will not run out.
- **62⁶ ≈ 5.7 × 10¹⁰** and **62⁷ ≈ 3.5 × 10¹²**: how many short codes six or seven base-62 characters can spell (Chapter 12).

**Two pairs people confuse:**

| Pair | What differs | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Bits vs bytes | Links are quoted in bits per second (b), storage in bytes (B). 1 byte = 8 bits. | 1 Gbps moves 125 MB/s. 10 Gbps moves 1.25 GB/s. Writing "Mb" for "MB" is an 8x slip. |
| KB vs KiB | 1,000 vs 1,024 bytes. | 1 TB disk label is 10¹² B; the OS may show 931 GiB. Ignore the gap while estimating. |

**Byte-size anchors** (rules of thumb, ranges because real rows vary):

| Thing | Size |
|-------|------|
| `int` / `long` / timestamp | 4 B / 8 B / 8 B |
| UUID | 16 B |
| Short text row (URL mapping, a like, a counter) | ~100–500 B |
| Post with metadata | ~1 KB |
| Chat message stored with ids and index overhead | ~200 B |
| Photo, compressed | ~200 KB (feed-sized) to a few MB (original) |
| One minute of 1080p video | ~40–60 MB (about 5–8 Mbps) |

**Worked example.** A URL shortener: 1M new URLs a day at about 500 B each (assumption).

```text
1M × 500 B  = 10⁶ × 5 × 10²  = 5 × 10⁸ B
            = 500 × 10⁶ B     = 500 MB per day       ← name the rung: 10⁶ is mega
```

**Interview takeaway:** "I climb the unit ladder one rung at a time and I convert bits to bytes before comparing a link with a disk."

<h2 id="time">Chapter 2 · A day is a hundred thousand seconds</h2>

A product forecast says 50 million daily users, opening the feed about ten times a day. The first question is: how many requests per second is that, at the worst second of the day, and how many are writes?

A day has 86,400 seconds. **Round it to 10⁵.** Dividing by 10⁵ instead of 86,400 makes your QPS about 14% low. Your DAU guess is far less precise than that, and the peak factor you apply next swamps it.

```text
requests per day ÷ 100,000 = average requests per second
```

| Per day | ÷ 10⁵ (fast) | ÷ 86,400 (exact) | What it usually means |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1M | 10/s | ~12/s | Tiny: one server |
| 100M | 1,000/s | ~1,160/s | A real service: load balancer and several app servers |
| 1B | 10,000/s | ~11,600/s | Big: caching and replicas are no longer optional |
| 10B | 100,000/s | ~116,000/s | Cache tier mandatory, regional deployment conversation |

Two forms of the same rule: **"divide by 10⁵"** for speed, or **"1M a day ≈ 12 a second"** to be within a few percent. Pick one and use it consistently. This lesson uses ÷ 10⁵ and notes the exact figure where it matters. A 30-day month is about **2.6 × 10⁶ seconds** and a year about **3.15 × 10⁷**.

**From users to QPS:**

1. **Requests per day** = DAU × actions per user per day. Do each action separately (feed opens, posts, likes): their rates and costs differ.
2. **Average QPS** = requests per day ÷ 10⁵.
3. **Peak QPS** = average × a peak factor. **Rule of thumb: 2–3x for consumer apps**; much more for events (ticket drops, live sports). *Say it aloud:* "assuming peak is about 3x average."
4. **Read:write ratio.** Name it. "100:1 read-heavy" justifies caching and read replicas; "write-heavy" points at partitioning, log-structured stores and queues.

**Average vs peak, the difference that matters:**

| | Average | Peak |
|---|---|---|
| Use it for | Storage growth, monthly cost | Servers, cache nodes, connection pools |
| Wrong use | Sizing a fleet (it falls over every evening) | Multiplying storage by 3 (bytes accumulate at the average rate) |

**Worked numbers (assumptions):** 50M DAU; each opens the feed 10 times a day; 10% of users post one photo a day; peak is 3x average.

```text
Feed reads   50M × 10        = 500M per day  → ÷ 10⁵ = 5,000/s avg (exact 5,787)
             × 3 peak                         = 15,000/s peak
Uploads      50M × 10%        = 5M per day    → ÷ 10⁵ = 50/s avg → 150/s peak
Ratio        500M ÷ 5M        = 100 : 1 read-heavy
```

**Conclusion:** "15K feed requests a second at peak is a fleet of stateless app servers behind a load balancer. At 100:1 reads, a cache and read replicas carry the feed; 150 uploads a second is nothing for the write path."

<h2 id="latency">Chapter 3 · How far away is the answer?</h2>

Say a feed request takes 94 ms, and the trace shows a hundred tiny identical bars: one call to the author service per post, one after another. Each call is fast. That is the problem. To reason about this you need the **orders of magnitude** of common operations and the ratios between them.

Exact figures vary with hardware, so these are **rules of thumb**, descended from Jeff Dean's well-known list (see Beyond this page):

| Operation | Rough time | Order (ns) |
|---|---|---|
| CPU L1 cache reference | ~1 ns | 10⁰ |
| Main-memory reference | ~100 ns | 10² |
| Send 1 KB over a 1 Gbps link | ~10 µs | 10⁴ |
| Random 4 KB read from an SSD | ~100 µs (tens of µs on modern NVMe) | 10⁵ |
| Round trip inside one data centre | ~0.5 ms (often less) | ~10⁵·⁷ |
| Read 1 MB sequentially from an SSD | ~1 ms (less on NVMe) | 10⁶ |
| Hard-disk seek | ~10 ms | 10⁷ |
| Round trip across a continent or an ocean | ~100–150 ms | 10⁸ |

<Figure caption="The same table on a log scale: each bar is the power of ten, in nanoseconds. Memory to data-centre round trip is about 5,000x; a cross-continent round trip is about 300 same-DC round trips.">
  <Bars heights={[0, 2, 5, 5.7, 7, 8.2]} pointers={[{ at: 0, label: 'L1' }, { at: 1, label: 'RAM' }, { at: 2, label: 'SSD', tone: 'blue' }, { at: 3, label: 'DC', tone: 'blue' }, { at: 4, label: 'HDD', tone: 'purple' }, { at: 5, label: 'WAN', tone: 'red' }]} tones={{ 5: 'hot' }} />
</Figure>

What to take from it:

- **Memory is about 5,000x faster than a same-DC network hop** (100 ns versus 0.5 ms). That is why an in-process cache beats even Redis for very hot keys.
- **Sequential round trips add up.** 100 serial calls at 1 ms each is 100 ms. One batched call is about one round trip. N+1 queries are a latency bug before they are a load bug.
- **Distance is physics.** Light in fibre travels about 200,000 km/s, so a round trip costs at least **1 ms per 100 km**. New York to London is about 5,600 km: a floor of about 56 ms, typically 70–80 ms in practice. Only moving data closer (CDNs, regional replicas) helps.
- **Sequential beats random**, on disks and SSDs alike. That is why logs, LSM trees and Kafka are built around appends.

**Latency vs throughput, the pair people blur.** Latency is how long one request takes. Throughput is how many finish per second. A faster disk can raise both; adding servers raises throughput only. A cross-ocean call stays 150 ms however many servers you add.

**Throughput anchors** (rules of thumb: say "assume", offer to benchmark):

| Thing | Rough throughput |
|---|---|
| Hard disk, sequential | ~100–200 MB/s |
| SATA SSD / NVMe SSD, sequential | ~500 MB/s / several GB/s |
| 1 / 10 / 25 Gbps network card | 125 MB/s / 1.25 GB/s / ~3.1 GB/s |
| Redis node, simple GETs | ~100,000 ops/s (benchmark it) |
| Relational primary, simple indexed queries | thousands to low tens of thousands per second; a few thousand writes per second is a safe planning number |
| Stateless app server, 8 cores, ~8 ms of CPU per request | ~1,000 req/s at full CPU (8 cores ÷ 0.008 s) |
| WebSocket gateway | ~100,000 mostly idle connections as a conservative cap |

<Trace title="Place it on the line" steps={latency} takeaway="Know the orders of magnitude, multiply round trips when they are sequential, and remember that distance is physics." />

**Common slip:** fake precision. "A Redis GET takes 0.21 ms" invites "on what hardware?" Say "well under a millisecond in the same data centre": the order of magnitude is what the design needs.

**Interview takeaway:** "Memory is nanoseconds, a same-DC hop is half a millisecond, a cross-ocean hop is a hundred and fifty. So I batch sequential calls and move data closer to users."

<h2 id="storage">Chapter 4 · Bytes that never leave</h2>

Traffic goes home at night. Bytes don't. QPS rises and falls every day, but storage only climbs, and it keeps every mistake in your estimate for years. So ask two things first: how many years are we planning for, and how many copies?

**The method: records per day × bytes per record, then climb the unit ladder one rung at a time, saying each rung.** Then apply the multipliers, each said aloud:

```text
raw storage = records/day × bytes/record × 365 × years × replication × overhead
```

- **Years.** Pick a horizon (one year for "do we shard now?", five for "what is the end state?").
- **Replication.** A primary with two replicas, or a quorum store with three copies, is ×3 raw disk. A managed object store handles redundancy itself and bills logical bytes.
- **Overhead.** Indexes, row headers and fragmentation. Either fold it into bytes per record (as these examples do) or apply a multiplier such as ×1.5. Say which.

**Worked example, URL shortener** (assumption: 1M new URLs a day, ~500 B per row including index):

```text
1M × 500 B  = 500 MB/day
× 365       ≈ 180 GB/year   (exact 182.5)
× 5 years   ≈ 0.9 TB ≈ 1 TB logical   (× 3 replicas ≈ 2.7 TB raw)
```

**Conclusion:** "1 TB over five years: a single Postgres instance holds this comfortably, so sharding is not a day-one problem." That sentence is the deliverable, not the 1 TB.

**Worked example, photo feed** (assumptions: 5M posts a day, 3 MB stored per photo = 2 MB original + about 1 MB of resized copies, 1 KB metadata per post). Two very different kinds of bytes:

```text
Photos     5M/day × 3 MB = 15 TB/day → × 365 = 5,475 TB ≈ 5.5 PB/year logical
           self-run, 3 replicas: ≈ 16.4 PB raw     erasure-coded at 1.4×: ≈ 7.7 PB raw
Metadata   5M/day × 1 KB = 5 GB/day → × 365 ≈ 1.8 TB/year logical → × 3 replicas ≈ 5.5 TB raw
           5 years ≈ 9.1 TB logical
```

Two conclusions: **photos are object storage, never database rows** (petabytes, written once, read via CDN). **Metadata fits one Postgres primary for year one** and needs a partitioning plan by about year two.

  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    A["5M posts per day\n× 1 KB"] --> B["5 GB per day"] --> C["× 365\n≈ 1.8 TB per year"] --> D["× 3 replicas\n≈ 5.5 TB raw per year"] --> E["Decision\none primary for year one,\npartition plan by year two"]`} />

**Replication vs erasure coding**, two ways to survive disk loss:

| | Replication (3 copies) | Erasure coding (e.g. Reed–Solomon 10+4) |
|---|---|---|
| Raw space for 1 TB | 3 TB | 1.4 TB |
| Survives | Loss of 2 copies | Loss of any 4 of 14 blocks |
| Cost | Plain disk | CPU, and reading many blocks to rebuild |
| Typical home | Databases, hot data | Large object stores, cold data |

<h2 id="bandwidth">Chapter 5 · Decide what to ignore</h2>

**Bandwidth = QPS × payload size.** Do it separately for **ingress** (data coming in, such as uploads) and **egress** (data going out, such as downloads), and convert to bits when comparing with network links or egress pricing.

For most text-payload systems the answer is "kilobytes per request at thousands of QPS gives tens or hundreds of MB/s, spread over a fleet: a non-issue, moving on." Saying **"bandwidth is a non-issue here, I'll skip it"** is a correct and strong answer. It shows judgment.

It matters when payloads are **media**: photos, video, file sync. Then the estimate decides **CDN versus origin**, and **direct-to-storage uploads** (presigned URLs) versus streaming bytes through your app servers.

**Worked numbers, photo feed** (assumptions: 20 posts at 1 KB in each feed response; about 10 images of 200 KB shown per open; 2 MB per uploaded photo; 95% CDN hit ratio):

```text
                 → across 25 app servers: ~100 Mbps each. Non-issue.
Feed images      10 images × 200 KB = 2 MB per open
                 500M opens/day × 2 MB = 1 PB/day → ÷ 10⁵ = 10 GB/s avg (exact 11.6) → × 3 = 30 GB/s peak

Uploads (in)     5M/day × 2 MB = 10 TB/day → 100 MB/s avg → 300 MB/s peak = 2.4 Gbps
```

<h2 id="memory">Chapter 6 · The hot slice</h2>

Someone proposes caching "all the posts". A better first question is how big all the posts are, and a better second one is how many of them anyone looked at today. The second number is often more than a hundred times smaller than the first. Size the cache for that one.

The question is never "how big is the data?" but **"how big is the hot data?"** The 80/20 heuristic says about 20% of items serve about 80% of reads, and real access is often steeper (popularity tends to follow a long-tailed, Zipf-like curve). It is a **rule of thumb**: state it, and replace it with data when you have some.

**The sizing chain:** dataset → working set → × overhead → ÷ usable memory per node = nodes for **capacity**. Then peak operations ÷ (operations per node × target utilisation) = nodes for **throughput**. Take the larger, then add replicas for **availability**.

**Three reasons to add cache nodes, and why they differ:**

| Reason | Question | Adding replicas helps? |
|---|---|---|
| Capacity | Does the working set fit in one node's RAM? | No, replicas hold the same data |
| Throughput | Can one node serve the peak operations per second? | Only for reads, and only if clients may read from replicas |
| Availability | If a node dies, does the cache (and the database behind it) survive? | Yes, that is their job |

**Worked numbers (assumptions):** about 25 GB usable per node; about 100,000 simple ops/s per node as a rule of thumb (benchmark it); plan for 60% utilisation.

```text
Rate limiter    50K API keys × ~100 B (key + counter + timestamp) = 5 MB
                → fits one Redis node a thousand times over

Profiles        50M profiles × 2 KB = 100 GB dataset; ~10% read daily = 10 GB working set
                → fits one node

Feed posts      posts from the last 48 hours: 5M/day × 2 days × 1 KB = 10 GB
                × 1.5 for per-key and structure overhead = 15 GB → capacity: 1 node
                peak lookups: 15,000 feeds/s × 20 posts = 300,000/s
                ÷ (100,000 × 0.6) = 5 nodes → throughput decides: 5 primaries + replicas
                (3 nodes would be exactly 100% busy at peak, with no headroom)
```

The rate-limiter conclusion: "5 MB, so a cluster would be for availability, not capacity." Saying which of the three reasons applies is a senior-level sentence.

<h2 id="servers">Chapter 7 · Boxes, threads and Little’s law</h2>

A classic incident: CPU at 30%, memory fine, database idle, and every request timing out. A payment provider had slowed from a tenth of a second to two seconds, and every worker thread in the fleet was waiting on it. The servers were not overloaded. They were full.

**How many servers?**

```text
servers = peak QPS ÷ (per-server QPS × target utilisation)   then add redundancy
```

- **Per-server QPS** comes from a benchmark, or from first principles: cores ÷ CPU time per request. 8 cores at 8 ms of CPU each = 1,000 req/s at 100% CPU.
- **Target utilisation** below 100%, because queues explode near saturation. In the simplest queueing model (M/M/1), average time in the system is service time ÷ (1 − utilisation): **2x at 50%, 5x at 80%, 10x at 90%**. About 60% is a common planning figure (rule of thumb) that leaves room for bursts and failures.
- **Redundancy**: survive one server, or one zone, without breaching your latency target.

**Worked numbers, feed app tier:**

```text
15,000/s peak ÷ (1,000/s × 0.6) = 25 servers
spread over 3 zones → 9 per zone = 27 servers
lose a zone → 18 servers left: 15,000 ÷ 18 ≈ 833/s each ≈ 83% of capacity: survivable
```

**Little's law** is the most useful equation in capacity planning:

```text
L = λ × W       concurrency = throughput × time each item spends in the system
```

It holds for any stable system, whatever the arrival pattern, using long-run averages. It answers "how many at once?":

- **Requests in flight.** Each server handles 15,000 ÷ 25 = 600 req/s at peak. At 100 ms average latency, L = 600 × 0.1 = **60 requests in flight**. A thread-per-request server needs at least 60 threads, plus headroom.
- **Database connections.** If each request holds a connection for 4 ms in total, one server keeps 600 × 0.004 = **2.4 connections busy** on average; the fleet, 15,000 × 0.004 = **60**. A pool of 5–10 per server is plenty: 25 × 10 = 250 connections. Pools of 100 per server would be 2,500 connections against one Postgres, where every connection is a separate process with its own memory.
- **When a dependency slows down.** Latency from 100 ms to 2 s at the same 600 req/s gives L = 600 × 2 = **1,200 in flight** per server. With 200 worker threads the pool saturates and everything queues. A 300 ms timeout caps it: L ≤ 600 × 0.3 = **180**. Timeouts and bulkheads (a small separate pool per dependency) are Little's law turned into code.

  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR

    S -.->|"W rises to 2 s"| F["L = 1,200\npool of 200 is full"]`} />
</Figure>

<Reveal q="Little's law uses averages. Is that a problem when your SLO is a p99?">

It gives average concurrency, the right number for a pool's steady state. Bursts and tail latency push the instantaneous count above the average, so add headroom (1.5–2x is a reasonable start) and enforce the bound with timeouts. For latency targets reason about percentiles: Little's law says how many are inside, not how long the slowest one waits.

</Reveal>

**Common slip:** "we'll autoscale." Autoscaling reacts in minutes; peaks arrive in seconds. Say the number of servers you start with at peak, how many zones they span, and what happens when one zone disappears.

**Interview takeaway:** "Servers are peak over per-box throughput at about 60% utilisation, spread so a zone can fail. Concurrency is rate times latency, so I put timeouts on dependencies."

<h2 id="availability">Chapter 8 · Counting nines</h2>

A launch slide says "five nines". The fair reply is: which five? Write the feed's request path on a whiteboard: load balancer, app, cache, database. Under each box write its availability, multiply, and watch the nines disappear.

**Availability → downtime.** Learn the table; derive any row with "unavailability × time period".

| Availability | Downtime per year | Per 30-day month | Per day |
|---|---|---|---|
| 99% ("two nines") | 3.65 days | 7.2 hours | 14.4 min |
| 99.9% ("three nines") | 8.76 hours | 43.2 min | 86.4 s |
| 99.95% | 4.38 hours | 21.6 min | 43.2 s |
| 99.99% ("four nines") | 52.6 min | 4.32 min | 8.6 s |
| 99.999% ("five nines") | 5.26 min | 25.9 s | 0.86 s |

Per request: 99.9% success on 1B requests a day still means **1M failed requests a day**. Nines describe a budget, not perfection. An **error budget** uses the same idea for decisions: a 99.9% monthly target allows 43.2 minutes of downtime, so one 30-minute bad deploy spends about 70% of it.

**Composition: the pair people mix up.**

| | In series | In parallel |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | The request needs every component | Any one of N redundant copies is enough |
| Formula | Multiply availabilities: A₁ × A₂ × … | Multiply unavailabilities: 1 − (1 − A₁)(1 − A₂)… |
| Example | Three at 99.9% → 0.999³ ≈ **99.7%**; ten → **99.0%** | Two at 99% → 1 − 0.01² = **99.99%** |
| Effect | Always worse than the weakest part | Better than any single part, *if failures are independent* |

**Worked numbers, the feed path** (each figure an assumption):

```text
load balancer 99.99% × app tier 99.99% × cache 99.9% × database 99.95%
= 0.9999 × 0.9999 × 0.999 × 0.9995 ≈ 0.9983 → 99.83% ≈ 15 hours/year down

if the database could carry full read load without the cache, a cache outage
degrades latency instead of failing requests: the cache leaves the chain
0.9999 × 0.9999 × 0.9995 ≈ 0.9993 → 99.93% ≈ 6 hours/year down
```

That is the design lever: **shorten the serial chain** (make dependencies optional, degrade gracefully) and **add redundancy** to the weakest link.

<h2 id="sharding">Chapter 9 · When one box is not enough</h2>

"We'll shard it" is not a plan until you can say where the number came from. The shard count is whichever of three divisions comes out biggest.

```text
shards = max( total data at the planning horizon ÷ data per shard,
              peak writes ÷ writes per shard,
              peak reads ÷ reads per shard   ← only if replicas and cache can't absorb reads )
```

**Data per shard** is a planning choice, not a hard limit. Teams often pick about 1–4 TB per node so backups, restores and replica rebuilds take hours, not days: copying 2 TB at 200 MB/s takes 10,000 s, about 2.8 hours. This lesson assumes **2 TB per shard**.

**Worked numbers:**

```text
Feed metadata      ≈ 1.8 TB/year; 5-year total ≈ 9.1 TB ÷ 2 TB ≈ 4.6 → 5 shards eventually
                   writes: 150/s peak, trivial. Storage decides, and slowly:
                   year one fits one primary; by end of year two (≈ 3.65 TB) it needs 2.

Like counters      ≈ 90 GB/year (5M new posts/day × 50 B), storage fits one node forever
                   writes: 20,000/s peak ÷ ~5,000/s per primary = 4 shards
                   throughput decides. Or batch increments in memory and flush each second.
```

**Replicas vs shards, the difference people blur:**

| | Read replicas | Shards |
|---|---|---|
| What each node holds | A full copy of the data | A slice of the data |
| Adds read capacity | Yes | Yes |
| Adds write capacity | **No**, every replica applies every write | Yes |
| Adds storage capacity | No | Yes |
| Costs | Replication lag | Cross-shard queries, a partition key to choose, rebalancing |

**Plan the split before you need it.** Hash into many **logical partitions** (say 256) and map them onto a few physical nodes. Growing from 2 nodes to 5 means moving partitions, not rehashing every key. See <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-partitioning/`}>Partitioning</a> for keys, hot partitions and rebalancing.

**Common slip:** "we'll shard it" with no number and no key. Say the count, the axis that forced it, and the partition key, and check that the key spreads load (one celebrity user must not own a whole shard).

**Interview takeaway:** "Shards are the largest of storage, writes and reads divided by what one node handles. Replicas add reads, never writes."

<h2 id="moments">Chapter 10 · A photo feed, end to end</h2>

**The 5-minute script**, the same for every problem:

1. "Let me get the numbers before designing."
2. **QPS**: daily volume ÷ 10⁵, × peak factor, read:write ratio → *conclusion*
3. **Storage**: records/day × bytes, ladder to the planning horizon, replicas → *conclusion*
4. **Bandwidth**: payload × QPS → usually *"non-issue, skipping"*, sometimes *"CDN"*
5. **Memory**: hot set size → *one node or a cluster, and which reason*
6. **Bridge**: "The numbers say [X], so here is the shape I would start with."

**The full sweep.** Assumptions: 50M DAU · 10 feed opens per user per day · 10% post one photo a day · 3 MB stored per photo · 1 KB metadata per post · 20 posts per feed response · 10 images × 200 KB per open · peak 3x · about 100,000 ops/s per cache node at 60% utilisation.

```text
QPS        reads   500M/day → 5,000/s avg → 15,000/s peak
           writes  5M/day   → 50/s avg    → 150/s peak           100:1 → cache + read replicas

           meta    5 GB/day  → ≈ 1.8 TB/year (≈ 5.5 TB raw ×3)       → one primary, year one
```

<h2 id="chat">Chapter 11 · A billion messages a day</h2>

A new requirement arrives: messaging. Someone asks how many connection servers, someone else how many shards. Both answers come from the same few numbers.

**Assumptions:** 20M DAU · 50 messages sent per user per day · about 2 recipients per message on average (groups) · 200 B stored per message (text, ids, timestamps, index) · each user connected about 60 minutes a day in total · peak 3x · media sent as links to object storage, not counted here.

```text
Writes       20M × 50 = 1B messages/day → ÷ 10⁵ = 10,000/s avg (exact 11,574) → 30,000/s peak
Deliveries   1B × 2 recipients = 2B/day → 20,000/s avg → 60,000/s peak pushes

Storage      1B × 200 B = 200 GB/day → × 365 = 73 TB/year logical → × 3 = 219 TB/year raw
```

<h2 id="shortener">Chapter 12 · One prompt, two scales</h2>

The prompt: *Design a URL shortener.* Halfway through your estimate comes: "Now multiply the traffic by a hundred." Many candidates redraw the whole design. You change one column of numbers and three decisions.

**Assumptions:** reads (redirects) outnumber new links 100:1 · 500 B per row including index · 5-year horizon · peak 3x · hot set = links created in the last 30 days.

| | Scale A: launch | Scale B: "100x the traffic" |
|---|---|---|
| New links | 1M/day → 10/s avg, 30/s peak | 100M/day → 1,000/s avg, 3,000/s peak |
| Redirects | 100M/day → 1,000/s avg, 3,000/s peak | 10B/day → 100,000/s avg, 300,000/s peak |
| Storage | 500 MB/day → ~180 GB/yr → **~1 TB in 5 years** | 50 GB/day → ~18 TB/yr → **~91 TB in 5 years** |
| Links in 5 years | 1.825B | 182.5B |
| Code length | 6 chars: 62⁶ ≈ 57B, ~31x headroom | 7 chars: 62⁷ ≈ 3.5T, ~19x headroom (62⁶ is too few) |
| Hot set (30 days) | 30M × 500 B = 15 GB → one cache node | ~1.5 TB → a cache cluster |
| DB reads at 90% cache hits | 300/s peak | 30,000/s peak |
| Bandwidth | 3,000 × 500 B = 1.5 MB/s → skip | 300K × 500 B = 150 MB/s → still skip |
| **Decision** | One Postgres primary + replica, one cache node | Shard by short-code hash (~46 shards at 2 TB), a cache cluster, 7-char codes |

The design did not change shape; **the numbers moved three decisions**: sharding, code length, and one cache node becoming a cluster. That is what "the estimate drives the design" means.

<h2 id="metrics">Chapter 13 · A million samples a second</h2>

An observability pipeline: every host, every service, every ten seconds. A vendor quote with a lot of zeros arrives. Before building anything, compute the samples per second, then what you keep and for how long.

**Assumptions:** 10,000 hosts · 1,000 time series per host · one sample every 10 s · ~100 B per sample on the wire · 16 B per sample uncompressed in storage (8 B timestamp + 8 B value), ~2 B compressed · keep raw data 15 days, then hourly rollups for 1 year · rollup row = min, max, sum, count (4 × 8 B) + 8 B timestamp = 40 B · per-day conversion × 10⁵ (it rounds up about 16%, the safe direction for storage).

```text
Series        10,000 hosts × 1,000 = 10M active series
Ingest        10M ÷ 10 s = 1M samples/s;   wire: 1M × 100 B = 100 MB/s ≈ 0.8 Gbps
Per day       1M × 10⁵ s ≈ 100B samples/day (exact 86.4B)
Raw bytes     uncompressed 100B × 16 B = 1.6 TB/day;   compressed 100B × 2 B = 200 GB/day
Raw, 15 days  15 × 200 GB = 3 TB
Rollups       1 hour = 360 raw samples ≈ 720 B compressed → one 40 B row: ~18x smaller
              10M series × 24 rows/day × 40 B = 9.6 GB/day → 1 year ≈ 3.5 TB
Total         3 TB + 3.5 TB = 6.5 TB logical → × 3 ≈ 19.5 TB raw
Without rollups, 1 year of raw: 200 GB × 365 = 73 TB (219 TB ×3), about 20x more than 3.5 TB
Ingesters     assume ~200K samples/s per node: 1M ÷ (200K × 0.6) ≈ 8.3 → 9 nodes
```

**Conclusions:** the **retention policy is the storage design**: rollups are a 20x lever on the year-long tier. A partitioned log (e.g. Kafka) in front of ingesters, keyed by series hash, keeps ingest horizontal. The risk to watch is **cardinality** (the number of distinct series), not volume. Compressed time-series formats really do reach a byte or two per sample: Facebook's Gorilla paper reports about 1.37 bytes. See <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-timeseries/`}>Time-series</a> for rollups and downsampling.

<h2 id="video">Chapter 14 · The upload firehose</h2>

A short-video app: a million uploads a day. The first prototype streamed uploads through the API servers, and in load tests the fleet spent all its time shovelling bytes and none answering requests. The questions: how many gigabits come in at peak, and why do they touch the API servers at all?

**Assumptions:** 1M uploads a day · 50 MB average (about a minute of 1080p at ~6.7 Mbps) · phone uplink ~10 Mbps · renditions (1080p/720p/480p/360p) add about as much again · transcoding costs about 4 core-minutes per video-minute (benchmark your encoder) · 20M views a day at ~30 MB watched each · peak 3x.

```text
Ingress     1M × 50 MB = 50 TB/day → ÷ 10⁵ = 500 MB/s avg (exact 579) → × 3 = 1.5 GB/s peak
            × 8 = 12 Gbps at peak                       → presigned, resumable uploads to object storage
Per upload  50 MB × 8 = 400 Mb ÷ 10 Mbps = 40 s         → resumable / multipart, mobile links drop
Little      peak arrivals 1M ÷ 10⁵ × 3 = 30/s; × 40 s = 1,200 uploads in flight
Cross-check 1,200 × 10 Mbps = 12 Gbps                    ✓ same answer by a second route
Storage     original 50 MB + renditions ~50 MB = 100 MB/video → 100 TB/day → 36.5 PB/year
Transcode   1M videos × 1 min × 4 core-min = 4M core-min/day ÷ 1,440 min ≈ 2,800 cores busy on average
            peak × 3 ≈ 8,300 cores; at 32 cores/machine: ~87 machines for average vs ~261 for peak
            → a queue lets you provision near the average; videos wait a little at peak
Egress      20M views × 30 MB = 600 TB/day → 6 GB/s avg → 18 GB/s (144 Gbps) peak → CDN
```

**Conclusions:** the bytes never touch the API tier (presigned, resumable uploads). Transcoding is **asynchronous** behind a queue, sized near the average with headroom, with the latency cost stated. Storage growth makes **lifecycle tiering** (cold storage for old renditions) a first-class topic. <h2 id="sanity">Chapter 15 · Is that number even possible?</h2>

A mock-interview recording: the candidate computes storage for a chat app, arrives at 73 petabytes a year, and proposes a tape archive. The reviewer's reply: divide it by the number of users, then by the days in a year, and ask whether anyone types that much.

**The sanity-check habit, thirty seconds every time:**

1. **Normalise per user.** Divide the total by DAU (and by days). 73 PB a year ÷ 20M users ÷ 365 ≈ 10 MB of text per user per day: impossible for text. 1 PB a day of images ÷ 50M users = 20 MB per user per day: ten feed opens of ten 200 KB images is plausible.
2. **Compare with a physical limit.** One 10 Gbps card moves 1.25 GB/s. A hard disk writes ~100–200 MB/s. If one box must beat its hardware, the design needs more boxes or a different shape.
3. **Check by a second route.** Chapter 14 got 12 Gbps from bytes per day *and* from uploads in flight × uplink speed. When two independent routes agree, trust the number.
4. **Check the units through the formula.** req/s × B/req = B/s. If the units do not cancel to what you wanted, the formula is wrong.
5. **Round in the safe direction.** For capacity and storage, round up, and say so.

**The stale-numbers killer.** Reusing the previous problem's or axis's numbers because they are still on the whiteboard. In the photo feed it would be the feed's 15K QPS quietly reused for the upload path, which runs at 150/s: a 100x overestimate that buys a fleet nobody needs. **Antidote: each axis starts by re-stating its inputs**: "5M uploads a day at 2 MB each…".

<h2 id="skip">Chapter 16 · When to put the pen down</h2>

Never skip estimation silently. Sometimes skip it.

Estimation earns its five minutes only when the numbers could change the design. **Skip or shrink it, out loud and with the reason, when:**

- **The prompt gives you the number.** "1M requests per second" is a given. Spend one minute on what it *implies* (an in-memory check, the memory footprint).
- **Every plausible value leads to the same design.** An internal tool for 200 employees runs on one small instance whatever the exact numbers.
- **The interviewer steers away.** If they say "assume it's big" or "skip the numbers", agree and state the one assumption you carry.
- **Time is short.** If requirements ran long, give the one or two numbers that drive the design in under a minute and **estimate lazily**: size storage when you pick the database, the cache when you draw it.

| | Skip | Shrink |
|---|---|---|
| When | Any value gives the same design | One or two numbers decide the design |
| What you say | "At 200 users it is one instance and one database." | "Reads dominate about 100:1, so cache; I'll size storage with the database." |

**The sentence:** "I'll skip detailed estimation: at this scale it's one instance and one database. The only number I'm carrying is about 100:1 reads, which says cache. Happy to size anything specific later." A silent skip reads as "forgot"; a stated skip reads as judgment.

<h2 id="interview-script">Say it in the room</h2>

The interviewer has written *photo-sharing feed* on the whiteboard. You do not reach for a database name. You say, "Let me get the numbers that will shape this," and talk for about ninety seconds.

<AnswerModes id="estimation-feed" q="Estimate the load for a photo-sharing feed with 50 million daily users.">
  <Fragment slot="short">

Reads are about 5,000/s on average and 15,000/s at a 3x peak. Writes are about 150/s at peak, so it is 100:1 read-heavy: cache plus read replicas. Photos run about 5.5 PB a year, so object storage and a CDN. Metadata is under 2 TB a year, so one Postgres primary for year one.

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="medium">

"Let me get the numbers that will shape the design. Assume 50 million daily users opening the feed ten times a day: 500 million reads, about 5,000 a second on average, call it 15,000 at a 3x peak. One in ten posts a photo a day: 5 million uploads, about 50 a second. Reads dominate a hundred to one, which says **cache and read replicas**.

Storage: photos at about 3 MB with renditions are 15 TB a day, about 5.5 PB a year. That is **object storage**, never the database. Metadata at a kilobyte a post is 5 GB a day, under 2 TB a year: **one Postgres primary** for year one, partitioned by user so we can split around year two.

Bandwidth: each feed open pulls about 2 MB of images, so a petabyte a day, 30 GB/s at peak: **a CDN**, with the origin seeing about 5%. Uploads are 2.4 Gbps at peak, so **presigned uploads straight to storage**. Servers: 15,000 at peak over about 600 per box at 60% utilisation is **25, spread over three zones** (27). The hot set of recent posts is about 15 GB, one node's memory, but 300,000 lookups a second need about **five cache primaries with replicas**, for throughput rather than capacity. That is the shape I'll start from."

  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="deep">

Add the assumptions you would confirm with the interviewer: DAU, actions per user, post rate, payload sizes, peak factor, retention, and whether the 20% hot-set rule holds. Then say what is robust to a 10x error (object storage, CDN, cache) and what moves (server count, sharding date). Finish with the failure view: serial availability about 99.8%, a zone loss leaves servers at about 83%, and timeouts bound connection use by Little's law.

  </Fragment>
</AnswerModes>

**Five follow-ups you will get:**

<Reveal q="1 · Why divide by 10⁵ instead of 86,400?">

A day is 86,400 seconds; 10⁵ is about 16% more, so my QPS comes out about 14% low. My DAU and actions-per-user are guesses with far more error than that, and the 3x peak factor dwarfs it. If precision mattered I'd use "1M a day ≈ 12 a second", but no design decision here flips on 14%.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="2 · Where does the 3x peak come from?">

It's an assumption: consumer traffic follows waking hours, so the busiest hour runs a few times the daily average. If there's real data, I'd use it. Event-driven products (ticket drops, live sports) can spike 10x or more in seconds; there I'd design for admission control and load shedding rather than provisioning for the spike, because autoscaling reacts in minutes.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="3 · How many database connections does the app tier need?">

Little's law: connections in use = queries per second × time each holds a connection. At 15,000 requests a second and about 4 ms of database time each, that's about 60 busy across the fleet, about 2–3 per server. A pool of 5–10 per server gives burst headroom, about 250 in total. Not 100 per server: 2,500 connections would hurt Postgres, where each connection is its own process.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="4 · What availability can you promise?">

The product of the serial path. Load balancer, app tier, cache and database at 99.99, 99.99, 99.9 and 99.95% multiply to about 99.83%, roughly 15 hours a year. To do better I'd shorten the chain (if the database can carry reads without the cache, the cache becomes optional and the path is about 99.93%) and put redundancy, in separate zones, on the weakest link.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="5 · What if your numbers are 10x off?">

The arithmetic scales linearly, so I re-check the conclusions. At 10x less it's a handful of servers, but images still want a CDN. At 10x more, metadata grows 18 TB a year and I shard from day one; app servers go to about 250. Object storage for photos, a CDN for images and a cache in front of the database hold across the whole range: those are the robust decisions.

</Reveal>

<h2 id="cheat-sheet">Cheat sheet</h2>

| When you see… | Say / compute… |
|---|---|
| Any number | Unit, one rung at a time: B → KB → MB → GB → TB → PB, ×1000 each |
| Network link or egress | Bits: × 8. 1 Gbps = 125 MB/s, 10 Gbps = 1.25 GB/s |
| Requests per day | ÷ 10⁵ = average QPS (exact: 1M/day ≈ 12/s) |
| Capacity | × peak factor (2–3x stated; more for events) |
| Read-heavy | Name the ratio → cache + read replicas |
| Storage | records/day × bytes × 365 × years × replicas (× overhead) |
| Object storage | Logical bytes; erasure coding inside (e.g. 1.4x), not your ×3 |
| Bandwidth | QPS × payload; text → usually skip aloud; media → CDN, direct upload |
| CDN or cache | Origin load = (1 − hit ratio) × total |
| Cache size | Working set, not dataset; then capacity vs throughput vs availability |
| Servers | peak ÷ (per-server × ~60%), then zones |
| Threads, connections, uploads in flight | Little's law: L = λ × W |
| Slow dependency | L grows with W → timeouts and bulkheads |
| Nines | Downtime = (1 − A) × period; 99.9% ≈ 8.76 h/yr, 43 min/month |
| Series / parallel | Multiply A / multiply (1 − A), if independent |
| Shards | max(storage, writes, reads) per shard at the horizon |
| An implausible total | Divide by users and days; compare with one box's limits |
| Prompt gives the number | Use it; compute only its implications |

## Five mistakes to leave behind

1. **A number with no decision.** Every estimate ends in a sentence that changes the design, or says aloud that nothing changes.
2. **Skipping a rung.** GB↔TB and bits↔bytes. Climb one rung at a time, out loud.
3. **Sizing capacity for the average.** Peak for capacity; average for storage and cost.
4. **Forgetting the multipliers** (× 365, × years, × replicas), or applying ×3 to a managed object store.
5. **Stale numbers and silent skips.** Re-state the inputs for every axis; skip only aloud, with the reason.

**Keep it warm:** run the transfer test with a friend: the full sweep on a **URL shortener** (100M DAU, 10:1 read:write on redirects, 1M new URLs/day), 8 minutes, out loud, every axis ending in a conclusion sentence. Start by sanity-checking the prompt itself (Chapter 12 shows why). Then once more on **Twitter timeline reads**. Numbers may be rough; missing conclusion sentences, unit slips and stale numbers are not.

**Further study**

- *Designing Data-Intensive Applications* (Martin Kleppmann): chapter 1 (describing load and performance, percentiles, the Twitter fan-out example) and chapter 6 (partitioning) give the theory behind Chapters 2, 7 and 9.
- [Latency numbers every programmer should know](https://gist.github.com/jboner/2841832): the widely shared version of Jeff Dean's table; [Colin Scott's interactive version](https://colin-scott.github.io/personal_website/research/interactive_latency.html) shows how the numbers drift by year. Treat both as orders of magnitude.
- [Google SRE book: Embracing Risk](https://sre.google/sre-book/embracing-risk/): error budgets and why 100% is the wrong target; and its [Availability Table](https://sre.google/sre-book/availability-table/) appendix.
- [Little's law (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little%27s_law): the statement, its assumptions, and examples.
- [Gorilla: A Fast, Scalable, In-Memory Time Series Database](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol8/p1816-teller.pdf) (VLDB 2015): the compression behind "a byte or two per sample".
- [Fermi problem (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_problem): estimating from rough assumptions, and why errors partly cancel.

**Look these up yourself**

- **Queueing theory and the utilisation knee**: M/M/1 and Kingman's formula, and why latency climbs steeply past ~70–80% utilisation.
- **"The Tail at Scale"** (Dean and Barroso): how fan-out turns rare slow responses into common slow requests, and hedged requests as a fix.
- **Zipf's law**: the long-tailed popularity curve behind "20% of items serve 80% of reads".
- **Erasure coding versus replication**: Reed–Solomon and locally repairable codes; storage overhead versus repair cost.
- **Cloud pricing as an estimation axis**: storage, egress and request prices. Check current price sheets.
- **Amdahl's law and the Universal Scalability Law**: why throughput does not grow linearly with cores or nodes.
- **Percentiles versus averages**: why SLOs use p99, and why averaging percentiles across servers is wrong.
