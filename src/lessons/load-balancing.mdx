<h2 id="prologue">Prologue · The 502 minute</h2>

Here is a problem many teams live with. Every time the listings service deploys, the error graph shows a small spike: about one minute of `502 Bad Gateway` responses, then silence. It has happened forty times and everyone has learned to ignore it.

Now product announces a limited-edition sale that opens at noon, everywhere at once. Assumption for this lesson: **50,000 requests per second for the first ten minutes**, and deploys cannot be frozen because the sale pages change daily. At that rate, the one-minute spike is a few million failed requests.

Every system design starts with the same sketch: `client → LB → servers`. Interviewers let that box slide until they do not: *"What does your load balancer actually do?"* This lesson is the answer, taken far enough that follow-up questions do not reach the bottom of what you know.

A **load balancer** (LB) is a program or device that accepts requests on one address and hands each one to one of several identical servers. It makes **four promises**, and each part of the lesson is one of them:

1. **Spread the work** so no server drowns while another idles (Parts One and Two).
2. **Notice the dead and the sick** and stop sending them traffic (Part Three).
3. **Let servers come and go** (deploys, scaling, failures) without users noticing (Part Three).
4. **Never be the single thing that fails**, in one data centre or across the world (Part Four).

**A common slip** is saying "the load balancer" when the design needs an anycast edge, an L4 balancer, an L7 proxy and an API gateway. Those are four different boxes with four different jobs. A second slip is drawing the box without saying what it decides, and on what information. The habit to build: **every box earns its sentence.**

<h2 id="why-balance">Chapter 1 · How many servers</h2>

**What is it?** Horizontal scaling means running several copies of a server behind one address instead of buying a bigger machine. The balancer is the one address.

**Why do we need it?** Three reasons, and you should be able to say all three:

- **Capacity.** One machine has a ceiling. Many machines behind one address act as one bigger machine.
- **Availability.** When one machine dies, the balancer stops sending it traffic and users see nothing (or one retried request).
- **Change.** Deploys, scaling up and down, and hardware replacement all mean servers leaving and joining. The balancer makes that invisible.

All three rest on one assumption: **any server can serve any request.** That is the stateless rule from the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/networking/`}>networking lesson</a>. Chapter 8 covers what happens when you break it.

**How many servers do you need?** Assumption for this example: a load test shows one server saturates at 1,000 requests per second, and you choose to run at 70% utilisation.

```text
servers = peak RPS ÷ (per-server capacity × target utilisation)

50,000 ÷ (1,000 × 0.70) = 71.4  →  72 servers, each at about 694 req/s
```

**Why 70% and not 100%?** Latency is not flat as a server fills. In the simplest queueing model (M/M/1), time in the system is the service time divided by `(1 − utilisation)`:

| Utilisation | Time in system vs. an idle server |
|---|---|
| 50% | 2× |
| 60% | 2.5× |
| **70%** | **3.3×** |
| 80% | 5× |
| 90% | 10× |

Real servers have several cores and are less extreme, but the shape holds: the curve bends sharply somewhere past 70 to 80%. The 30% headroom pays for four things that will happen: **bursts** inside the peak second, **imperfect balancing** (the busiest server is always above the average, see Part Two), **deploys** taking servers out of rotation, and **failures**.

**The zone loss is the one people forget.** An availability zone (AZ) is an isolated data centre inside a region. Put the 72 servers in three zones, 24 each, and lose one at noon:

```text
50,000 ÷ 48 survivors = 1,042 req/s each  →  104% of capacity. Overload.
```

At 70% a server can absorb at most `1 ÷ 0.7 ≈ 1.43×` its load. Losing one of three zones multiplies each survivor's load by `3 ÷ 2 = 1.5×`. So size for the failure you must survive:

```text
two zones must carry 50,000 at ≤ 90%:   50,000 ÷ 900 = 55.6 → 56 servers → 28 per zone
three zones × 28 = 84 servers; normal utilisation = 50,000 ÷ 84,000 ≈ 60%
```

**Failure planning, not taste, sets your everyday utilisation.** With three zones, normal utilisation must stay under two thirds of whatever you tolerate during a zone loss. (The <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/estimation/`}>estimation lesson</a> reaches the same 60% for the same reason.)

<Figure caption="Three zones of 28. Lose any one and the remaining 56 run at about 89%: hot, but serving. With 72 servers, the same loss pushes survivors past saturation.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart TB
    LB["Load balancer\n50,000 req/s"] --> ZA["Zone A\n28 servers\n~595 req/s each"]
    LB --> ZB["Zone B\n28 servers\n~595 req/s each"]
    LB --> ZC["Zone C (lost)\n0 servers"]
    ZC -.->|"its share moves"| ZA
    ZC -.->|"its share moves"| ZB`} />
</Figure>

A deploy is a smaller, planned version of the same loss. Rolling 10% of 84 servers at a time takes 8 out of rotation: `50,000 ÷ 76 ≈ 658` req/s each, about 66%. Fine. With 72 servers the same rollout leaves 65 at about 77%. Still fine, until you add a burst.

<Trace title="Count the servers" steps={capacity} takeaway="Servers = peak ÷ (capacity × target utilisation), then check the failure you must survive. Three zones means survivors carry 1.5x, so plan for it." />

**When is this enough?** For a small service with a known ceiling, one balancer and N servers sized this way is the whole design. Autoscaling comes on top, not instead.

**A common slip:** "We'll autoscale." Autoscaling reacts in minutes; a zone dies in a second and a sale opens at exactly noon. Say the number of servers at peak, how they are spread, and what each runs at after losing a zone.

**Interview takeaway.** "50K RPS at 1K per server and a 70% target is 72 servers. Across three zones I want any two to carry the load at 90%, so 84 servers, about 60% normally."

<h2 id="l4-l7">Chapter 2 · What the balancer can read</h2>

**What is it?** Balancers are named by the network layer they work at. **L4** means the transport layer (TCP and UDP). **L7** means the application layer (HTTP, gRPC). The <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/networking/`}>networking lesson</a> introduced the idea; here is the depth interviewers dig for. Rough picture: L4 reads the envelope, L7 opens the letter.

**An L4 balancer** sees the **5-tuple**: source IP, source port, destination IP, destination port, protocol. It sees nothing inside the payload. When a connection's first packet arrives it picks a backend and records the choice in a **connection table** (or recomputes it from a consistent hash, Chapter 12), so every later packet of that connection goes to the same place. Because it never parses the bytes, it handles any protocol, millions of connections and very high packet rates on modest hardware. Public examples: Linux IPVS, Google's Maglev (published in a paper) and Meta's Katran (open source).

There are three common ways packets travel through an L4 balancer:

| L4 mode | How | Client IP at the backend | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NAT** | Rewrites the destination address to the backend's; replies come back through the balancer to be rewritten again | Usually preserved, but replies must route via the balancer | Balancer carries both directions |
| **Full proxy (TCP mode)** | Ends the client's TCP connection and opens its own to the backend | Lost, unless the PROXY protocol is used | Two connections per client, still no HTTP parsing |
| **Direct server return (DSR)** | Forwards only inbound packets; the backend replies straight to the client from the shared virtual IP | Preserved | Only the inbound direction (usually the small one) crosses the balancer |

<Figure caption="Direct server return. Requests come in through the balancer; responses, usually much larger, go straight back. The backend must accept traffic for the virtual IP itself.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    C["Client"] -->|"1 · request to VIP"| LB["L4 balancer\n(DSR)"]
    LB -->|"2 · forwarded, VIP unchanged"| S["Backend\nVIP on loopback"]
    S -->|"3 · response from VIP\nbypasses the balancer"| C`} />
</Figure>

**An L7 balancer** is a full reverse proxy for HTTP or gRPC. It ends the client's connection, reads each request (method, path, host, headers, cookies) and **chooses a backend per request** over its own pool of backend connections. That lets it route `/listings/*` and `/chat/*` to different services, retry a failed idempotent request on another server, add `X-Forwarded-For`, compress, cache, rate limit, and balance gRPC calls. The price is CPU per request and memory per connection: it does real work for each request and holds two connections for each client.

| | **L4 (transport)** | **L7 (application)** |
|---|---|---|
| Sees | IPs and ports, TCP/UDP packets | Full HTTP: method, path, host, headers, cookies |
| Balances | Connections | Requests |
| Can do | Spread connections, any protocol, very high throughput | Route by path, end TLS, retry, rate limit, rewrite headers |
| TLS | Passthrough (some can end it, but still balance per connection) | Terminate, optionally re-encrypt |
| Speed | Extremely fast, simple | Slower per request, smarter |
| Examples | AWS NLB, IPVS, Maglev, Katran | AWS ALB, nginx, Envoy, HAProxy in HTTP mode |

**TLS: who holds the key?** Three modes, and you should be able to choose between them out loud.

- **Termination.** The balancer holds the certificate, decrypts, routes, and talks plain HTTP to the backends. Simplest, and what makes L7 routing possible. Certificates live in one place.
- **Passthrough.** The balancer forwards encrypted bytes; backends hold the certificates. The balancer cannot read HTTP, but it can still read the **SNI** (the hostname the client sends unencrypted at the start of the TLS handshake) and route by hostname. Use it when the backend must own the TLS session (end-to-end client certificates, strict key custody).
- **Re-encryption** ("TLS bridging"). Terminate, inspect and route, then open a new TLS connection (often **mutual TLS**, where both sides present certificates) to the backend. Every hop is encrypted and the balancer is still smart. The cost is a second handshake per backend connection, which connection pooling makes rare.

**Example: what each layer can route on.** One hostname `shop.example.com`, HTTPS only:

```http
GET /listings/42 HTTP/1.1
Host: shop.example.com
Cookie: cart=abc123
```

An L4 balancer in passthrough mode sees only an encrypted stream to port 443 (and the SNI `shop.example.com`). It cannot tell `/listings/42` from `/chat/7`. An L7 balancer that terminates TLS reads the path and can send listings to one pool and chat to another.

<Reveal q="If the L7 balancer terminates TLS, how do backends know the client’s real IP address and that the original request was HTTPS?">

The balancer adds headers: `X-Forwarded-For` (or the standard `Forwarded`) with the client's address, and `X-Forwarded-Proto: https`. Backends must trust these **only from your own balancer**, because a client can send its own fake `X-Forwarded-For`. So the edge overwrites or strips any value the client supplied. An L4 full proxy cannot add HTTP headers; it uses the PROXY protocol, a short preamble sent at the start of the backend connection.

</Reveal>

**When should I use which?** The interview default: **L7 at the edge**, because you almost always want path routing and TLS termination. Use **L4** for raw throughput or protocol transparency: millions of persistent connections, non-HTTP traffic, UDP games and voice. Big systems run both: an L4 tier holding a stable address in front of a fleet of L7 proxies.

**A common slip:** "The load balancer handles TLS" without saying where TLS ends and what travels after it. Say: *"TLS terminates at the L7 balancer, which re-encrypts to services with mTLS"*, or *"plain HTTP inside the private network"* and why that is acceptable here.

**Interview takeaway.** "L7 at the edge for routing, TLS termination and per-request balancing. L4 in front of it, or alone, when I need raw throughput or non-HTTP protocols."

<h2 id="connections">Chapter 3 · Connections versus requests</h2>

**What is it?** The question that decides how traffic really spreads: does the balancer choose **once per connection**, or **once per request**?

- **L4 decides per connection.** Every request on that connection follows the first decision.
- **L7 decides per request** (for HTTP/1.1, per request over its own backend connections; for HTTP/2 and gRPC, per stream).

**Why does it matter?** With many short connections the two look the same. They diverge when connections are **few, long-lived and busy**.

- **HTTP/1.1 keep-alive** lets one client connection carry many requests. With thousands of clients, connection-level balancing still averages out.
- **HTTP/2 and gRPC** multiplex *everything* from one client onto **one connection** that can live for hours. A connection-level balancer sees ten clients as ten units of work, no matter that one client sends a thousand calls a second.

**Example.** Ten gRPC clients, ten pods, an L4 balancer choosing a pod at random per connection, each client sending 1,000 calls per second:

1. The chance that every pod gets exactly one client is `10! ÷ 10¹⁰ ≈ 0.04%`. Usually some pods carry two or three clients and some carry none.
2. **Scaling does nothing.** New pods receive only *new* connections. Existing ones stay pinned, so an autoscaler adds capacity nobody can reach.
3. **It never heals.** A Kubernetes Service (kube-proxy) is an L4 balancer: it picks a pod per connection, then steps aside.

<Figure caption="Each client’s single HTTP/2 connection lands on one pod. The balancer never sees the calls inside, so the new pod gets nothing.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    C1["Client 1\n1,000 calls/s"] ==>|"one HTTP/2 connection"| P1["Pod 1\n2,000 calls/s"]
    C2["Client 2\n1,000 calls/s"] ==>|"one HTTP/2 connection"| P1
    C3["Client 3\n1,000 calls/s"] ==>|"one HTTP/2 connection"| P2["Pod 2\n1,000 calls/s"]
    P3["Pod 3\n0 calls/s"]
    P4["Pod 4 (new)\n0 calls/s"]`} />
</Figure>

**Four fixes, in the order you would say them:**

| Fix | How | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **L7 balancing that speaks HTTP/2** | A proxy (Envoy, for example) holds connections to every pod and assigns each call to a pod | One extra proxy hop |
| **Client-side balancing** | The gRPC client resolves all pod addresses, keeps a connection to each, picks per call | A library per language; service discovery must feed it (Chapter 14) |
| **Service mesh sidecar** | The same L7 balancing, done by a proxy beside each client | Sidecars to run (Chapter 14) |
| **Maximum connection age** | The server closes connections after a few minutes (gRPC servers send GOAWAY); clients reconnect and land somewhere new | Partial: rebalances every few minutes, never instantly |

**How many connections does a balancer hold?** Little's law says requests in flight = arrival rate × time in system. At 50,000 req/s with an assumed 50 ms average response time:

```text
in flight = 50,000 req/s × 0.05 s = 2,500 requests at any moment
per server (84 servers) ≈ 30 requests in flight
```

So the L7 tier needs at least 2,500 busy backend connections over HTTP/1.1 (one request per connection at a time), plus a pool for bursts, or far fewer over HTTP/2. The **client side** is a different number. Suppose 2 million phones keep a keep-alive connection open during the sale. At an assumed 20 KB per TLS connection (measure your own proxy), that is `2,000,000 × 20 KB = 40 GB` across the L7 tier, mostly idle connections. **Idle connections cost memory; active requests cost CPU.** Size both.

**Ports, the quiet limit.** A proxy talking to one backend address can only hold as many simultaneous connections as it has source ports. Linux's default ephemeral range is 32768 to 60999, so **28,232 ports**. The side that closes a TCP connection also keeps its port in **TIME_WAIT for 60 seconds** on Linux. A proxy that opens and closes 1,000 connections per second to one backend needs `1,000 × 60 = 60,000` ports and runs dry after about 28 seconds. The fix is **keep-alive connection pools**. More source IPs or more backend addresses raise the ceiling too, because the limit applies per source-IP, destination-IP and destination-port combination.

<Reveal q="HTTP/1.1 behind an L4 balancer also pins each connection. Why is it usually fine there but not with gRPC?">

Because of how many connections there are and how much each carries. A public HTTP/1.1 service sees thousands of client connections, each idle much of the time, and browsers open several per host, so the law of large numbers evens it out. An internal gRPC client opens **one** connection and pushes all its traffic down it, and there may be only a handful of clients. Few connections with huge, unequal traffic per connection is a lottery.

</Reveal>

**When should I use which fix?** Mesh or client-side balancing when many internal services speak gRPC. A max connection age as the cheap first step. A shared L7 proxy when you do not control the clients.

**A common slip:** "We'll put the gRPC service behind the load balancer." Which balancer, at which layer? If it is L4, including a plain Kubernetes Service, you have just described the five-hot-pods incident.

**Interview takeaway.** "L4 balances connections, so one long-lived HTTP/2 connection pins a whole client to one pod. For gRPC I balance per call with an L7 proxy, a sidecar or client-side balancing."

<h2 id="rotation">Chapter 4 · Taking turns</h2>

**What is it?** **Round robin** sends request 1 to server 1, request 2 to server 2, and so on around the list. **Random** picks a server uniformly at random. Both need no information about the servers, which makes them cheap, stateless and hard to fool.

**Why is that not always enough?** Say new servers twice the size of the old ones join the pool. Round robin gives every server the same share, so the old ones run hot while the new ones idle at half capacity. Round robin is fair to servers, not to the work. **It is the right choice when requests cost about the same and servers are identical**, which describes a lot of stateless CRUD traffic.

**Weighted round robin** gives each server a share proportional to its weight, so a 2x server gets weight 2. The naive version sends a server all its turns in a row (A A A A A B C), which creates bursts. **Smooth weighted round robin** (the method nginx uses) interleaves the turns:

1. Each turn, every server adds its weight to a running score.
2. The server with the highest score wins the turn.
3. The winner subtracts the sum of all weights from its score.

```python
def smooth_wrr(weights: dict[str, int], n: int) -> list[str]:
    score = {s: 0 for s in weights}
    total = sum(weights.values())
    picks = []
    for _ in range(n):
        for s, w in weights.items():
            score[s] += w
        winner = max(score, key=score.get)
        score[winner] -= total
        picks.append(winner)
    return picks

smooth_wrr({"A": 5, "B": 1, "C": 1}, 7)   # A A B A C A A
```

  <ArrayStrip name="Request" values={['A', 'A', 'B', 'A', 'C', 'A', 'A']} tones={{ 2: 'done', 4: 'done' }} />
</Figure>

Weights are a static description of the servers. They do not react to what is happening: a server stuck behind a slow query keeps its full share. Weights are also how **canary** releases work: give the new version weight 1 against 99 for the old one to send it about 1% of traffic.

<Trace title="Take turns well" steps={rotation} takeaway="Uniform work and identical servers: round robin or random. Different-sized servers: weights. Different-sized work: you need to look at current load." />

**When should I use it?** Stateless, similar-cost requests on similar servers; canaries via weights. If you can only say "round robin" without those two conditions, say something else.

**A common slip:** naming round robin with no reason. Defend the choice by the workload: *requests are uniform and servers are identical.*

**Interview takeaway.** "Round robin or random when work and servers are uniform, weights for different sizes and canaries, and a load-aware algorithm when request costs vary."

<h2 id="least-loaded">Chapter 5 · Ask who is busy</h2>

**What is it?** **Least connections** (in HTTP proxies, more precisely **least outstanding requests**) sends each request to the server with the fewest requests in flight. "In flight" means the server has received the request and not yet replied.

**Why do we need it?** Suppose most requests take 10 ms but some are 10-second report exports. With round robin, every server eventually gets an export, and while it runs, quick requests queued behind it time out. A server stuck on an export holds an open request, so least connections sends new work elsewhere. **When request costs vary wildly, balance on current load, not on turns.**

<Figure caption="Least connections: B has the fewest requests in flight, so it gets the next one.">
  <Bars heights={[12, 3, 7]} pointers={[{ at: 0, label: 'A' }, { at: 1, label: 'B ← next', tone: 'green' }, { at: 2, label: 'C' }]} tones={{ 1: 'done' }} />
</Figure>

**Least response time** goes further: track each server's latency and favour the fast ones. Raw samples are noisy, so balancers keep an **EWMA** (exponentially weighted moving average):

```text
estimate = α × new sample + (1 − α) × old estimate

α = 0.2, old 20 ms, sample 100 ms  →  0.2 × 100 + 0.8 × 20 = 36 ms
```

A larger α reacts faster and jitters more. "**Peak EWMA**" variants (Twitter's Finagle and Linkerd are public examples) jump to a high sample immediately and decay slowly, so a server that suddenly slows is avoided at once. A common score is *latency estimate × (requests in flight + 1)*.

**Three ways least-loaded goes wrong:**

1. **Errors are fast.** A server answering every request with an instant `500` holds no requests and has superb latency. Least connections and least response time both pour traffic into it, a **black hole**. Count errors as a penalty and eject outliers (Chapter 10).
2. **New servers get flooded.** A new server has zero connections, so least connections sends it everything until it catches up, while its caches and JIT are cold. That is why slow start exists (Chapter 11).
3. **Stale information herds.** Twenty balancer instances each pick "the least-loaded server" from a load table refreshed once a second. They all see the same minimum, and for a whole second all send everything to it. Next second they stampede somewhere else. A deterministic choice plus stale data makes a herd.

| Algorithm | Needs | Good for | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Round robin / random** | Nothing | Uniform requests, identical servers | Blind to slow or sick servers |
| **Weighted round robin** | Static weights | Mixed server sizes, canary percentages | Weights do not react to load |
| **Least connections / requests** | Current in-flight counts | Mixed request costs, long requests | Fast-failing servers, new-server floods, herding on stale counts |
| **Least response time (EWMA)** | Latency history | Mixed costs, noisy or mixed servers | Same black-hole risk; tuning α |

<Trace title="Read the load" steps={leastLoaded} takeaway="Least-loaded beats turns when costs vary. But errors are fast, new servers look empty and stale data herds. Penalise errors, warm up newcomers and add randomness." />

**When should I use it?** Mixed request costs, long-running requests, or uneven servers, with one balancer or fresh counts. With many balancers or stale data, go to the next chapter.

**A common slip:** "Least connections, because it's smarter." Smarter with what information, how fresh, and from how many balancers? A design with 30 balancer instances and a global least-connections choice has a herd built in.

**Interview takeaway.** "Least outstanding requests when costs vary, with an error penalty and slow start. It needs fresh counts, so with many balancers I would add randomness."

<h2 id="two-choices">Chapter 6 · Pick two, keep the better</h2>

**What is it?** **The power of two random choices:** for each request, pick **two** servers at random and send the request to the less loaded of the two (fewer requests in flight, or a lower EWMA score). Envoy's least-request balancer samples two servers by default (when weights are equal), and nginx offers `random two least_conn`.

```python
import random

def pick_two_choices(servers, in_flight):
    a, b = random.sample(servers, 2)
    return a if in_flight[a] <= in_flight[b] else b
```

**Why does it work so well?** The classic result (balls into bins; Azar, Broder, Karlin and Upfal, and Mitzenmacher's thesis) is about n requests placed on n servers:

- **One random choice:** the busiest server ends with about `log n ÷ log log n` requests.
- **Two choices:** about `log log n ÷ log 2`. That is an **exponential** improvement from one extra probe.
- **d choices:** about `log log n ÷ log d`. Going from two to three barely helps. The jump is from one to two.

Intuition: with one choice, a server is overloaded whenever luck sends it many requests. With two, a request lands on a busy server only if *both* samples are busy. The probability that both of two random servers are among the busiest is the square of the probability for one, and squaring a small number makes it tiny.

**Worked numbers.** An illustrative simulation, 300 runs each, requests placed on the same number of servers:

| Requests on servers | One random choice: busiest server | Two choices | Three choices |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 on 1,000 | usually 5 (4 to 9) | **3 in every run** | 2 or 3 |
| 10,000 on 10,000 | usually 7 (6 to 10) | 3 (sometimes 4) | usually 3 |

**Why it beats "least loaded" in real systems.** It is **robust to stale and partial information.** Go back to twenty balancers with a stale load table. Server S7 looks emptiest to all of them. With "pick the global minimum", S7 gets 100% of new requests until the table refreshes. With two random choices, S7 wins only when it is one of the two sampled: about `2 ÷ 72 ≈ 2.8%` of requests in a 72-server fleet, against a fair share of `1 ÷ 72 ≈ 1.4%`. **Stale data now costs a mild bias instead of a stampede.**

It also needs very little: no global table, no coordination, just two lookups of local counters. That is why it fits client-side balancing and sidecars, where each balancer sees only its own traffic.

<h2 id="affinity">Chapter 7 · Same key, same server</h2>

**What is it?** Sometimes you *want* the same key to reach the same server: a local cache, a WebSocket fleet, a game session, a per-user counter. That is **affinity**, and it is a hashing problem.

**Why do we need it?** Say each listings server keeps an in-memory cache of the hottest listings. With round robin every server caches every hot listing, so each cache holds only a fraction of what it could. If listing 42 always went to the same server, that server would always have it warm.

**IP hash**, `hash(client IP) mod N`, is the oldest form. It has two problems. Changing N remaps almost everyone. And **NAT concentrates users**: a mobile carrier can put tens of thousands of phones behind one public address, all hashing to one server. Hash on what identifies the user or the object (user id, listing id, cookie), not on the network address.

**Why mod N fails.** After growing from N to N + 1, a key stays put only if `hash mod N = hash mod (N + 1)`. From 10 to 11 servers that holds for 10 of every 110 hash values, so **10/11, about 91% of keys move**: every local cache goes cold at once.

**Consistent hashing** puts servers and keys on the same ring of hash values. Each key belongs to the **next server clockwise**. Adding a server takes over only the arc between it and its predecessor, about **1/N** of the keys. Removing one hands its arc to its neighbour. With few servers the arcs are uneven, so each server is placed at many points on the ring (**virtual nodes**), which evens things out and lets bigger servers own more. The <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-partitioning/`}>partitioning lesson</a> builds the ring in full.

<Figure caption="A ring of positions 0 to 99, read left to right and wrapping around. Each key goes to the next server clockwise. Add S4 at position 60 and only keys between 40 and 60 move: L58 goes from S3 to S4. Everything else stays.">
  <ArrayStrip name="Before" values={['S1 · 12', 'L31', 'S2 · 40', 'L58', 'S3 · 71', 'L93']} indices={false} pointers={[{ at: 1, label: '→ S2', tone: 'gold' }, { at: 3, label: '→ S3', tone: 'gold' }, { at: 5, label: '→ S1 (wraps)', tone: 'gold' }]} />
  <ArrayStrip name="After" values={['S1 · 12', 'L31', 'S2 · 40', 'L58', 'S4 · 60', 'S3 · 71', 'L93']} indices={false} pointers={[{ at: 1, label: '→ S2', tone: 'gold' }, { at: 3, label: '→ S4 (moved)', tone: 'red' }, { at: 6, label: '→ S1', tone: 'gold' }]} />
</Figure>

```python
import bisect, hashlib

def h(s: str) -> int:
    return int(hashlib.md5(s.encode()).hexdigest(), 16)

class Ring:
    def __init__(self, servers, vnodes=100):
        self.points = sorted((h(f"{s}#{i}"), s) for s in servers for i in range(vnodes))
        self.keys = [p for p, _ in self.points]

    def lookup(self, key: str) -> str:
        i = bisect.bisect(self.keys, h(key)) % len(self.points)
        return self.points[i][1]
```

Balancers offer this directly: Envoy's ring-hash and Maglev policies, HAProxy's consistent hashing, nginx's `hash $request_uri consistent`.

**The hot key problem.** Consistent hashing balances *keys*, not *load*. If one listing gets 30% of all traffic, its server takes 30% of all traffic. **Consistent hashing with bounded loads** (Mirrokni, Thorup and Zadimoghaddam) fixes this: give every server a capacity of `⌈c × average load⌉` for some `c > 1`. If a key's server is full, walk clockwise to the next server with room.

```text
10 servers, 1,000 requests in flight → average 100
c = 1.25 → capacity ⌈1.25 × 100⌉ = 125 per server
the hot listing fills S3 to 125; the overflow goes to the next server clockwise with room
```

No server exceeds 125% of the average, most keys still go home, and the overflow goes to a *predictable* neighbour so that neighbour's cache warms up for the hot key too. HAProxy exposes this as a balance factor on consistent hashing.

<Trace title="Hash for affinity" steps={affinity} takeaway="Hash on the key that owns the state, not the client IP. Mod N moves almost everything; consistent hashing moves 1/N. Bounded loads stop one hot key from melting one server." />

**Trade-offs.** Affinity costs you hot spots, uneven load and reshuffles on scaling. You pay it *only* when servers hold per-key state worth keeping warm. The rule: **uniform CRUD requests, round robin. Long-lived uneven work, least connections. Server-local state, consistent hash on the key.**

**A common slip:** "Consistent hashing on user id" for a stateless service. It buys nothing and adds hot spots.

**Interview takeaway.** "Consistent hashing on the key that owns the state, with virtual nodes and bounded loads. mod N would move about 91% of keys on a 10-to-11 resize."

<h2 id="sticky">Chapter 8 · Sticky sessions versus stateless services</h2>

**What is it?** **Sticky sessions** (session affinity) make the balancer send a user's requests to the same server every time. Two common ways:

- **A cookie** the balancer sets (or reads from the app) naming the server. It survives the user changing networks.
- **Source-IP** affinity. It breaks when a phone switches from Wi-Fi to cellular, and piles whole NATs onto one server.

**Why does it hurt?** Say checkout keeps each cart in server memory and the balancer pins each user to a server with a cookie. A hot-fix deploy restarts every server once. Tens of thousands of carts vanish, one server at a time. The balancer did what it was told: it sent each user back to their server, which no longer remembered them.

<Figure caption="Sticky sessions tie a user's state to one server's lifetime. When that server restarts, the balancer faithfully sends the user back to an empty room.">
  <Mermaid code={`sequenceDiagram
    participant U as User
    participant LB as Load balancer
    participant S3 as Server 3
    U->>LB: POST /cart (cookie: srv=3)
    LB->>S3: add item (cart in memory)
    Note over S3: deploy restarts server 3
    U->>LB: GET /checkout (cookie: srv=3)
    LB->>S3: GET /checkout
    S3-->>U: cart is empty`} />
</Figure>

**What stickiness costs:**

- **State dies with the server.** Deploys, crashes and scale-in all lose sessions.
- **Load gets uneven.** Heavy users stay wherever they landed, however hot it gets. The balancer's algorithm is overridden.
- **Draining takes forever.** A server cannot leave until its sticky users do (Chapter 11).
- **Scaling out helps slowly.** New servers only get new users.

**The stateless alternative.** Put session state somewhere every server can reach: a **signed token** (a JWT carrying identity and expiry), a **shared session store** (Redis, with the session id in a cookie) or the database. Then any server can serve any request, and every promise the balancer makes comes back.

```text
SET sess:9f3c1a '{"user_id":42,"cart_id":"c-77"}' EX 1800
GET sess:9f3c1a
EXPIRE sess:9f3c1a 1800       # sliding expiry: refresh on activity
```

**Sticky versus stateless at a glance:**

| | Sticky sessions | Stateless (token or shared store) |
|---|---|---|
| State lives | In one server's memory | In the token, Redis or the database |
| Server restart | Users lose state | No effect |
| Load spread | Uneven; heavy users stay put | Balancer free to choose per request |
| Extra dependency | None | Redis or key management (a store to keep available) |
| Good for | Rebuildable state: warm cache, live connection | Sessions, carts, anything that must survive |

**When is affinity fine?** When per-server state is a **performance optimisation that can be rebuilt**: a warm local cache, a reused connection, a WebSocket that will reconnect. Losing it costs speed, not correctness. That is the consistent-hashing case from Chapter 7, and the connection-fleet case in the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-realtime/`}>realtime lesson</a>.

<h2 id="health">Chapter 9 · Alive, or ready?</h2>

**What is it?** A **health check** is how a balancer decides whether a server may receive traffic. It learns in two ways:

- **Active checks.** The balancer probes each server on a schedule (a TCP connect, or `GET /healthz` every few seconds) and counts results. A server is marked **down after N consecutive failures** (the unhealthy threshold) and **up after M consecutive passes** (the healthy threshold).
- **Passive checks.** The balancer watches **real traffic**: connection failures, timeouts, `5xx` responses. No extra requests, and it notices problems on the paths users actually use (Chapter 10).

**What should a check check?** Say someone makes `/health` "smarter" by also checking the database connection. Then the database pauses for five seconds during a failover. Every server's check fails in the same five seconds, and the balancer removes every server. The database was back in five seconds; the site took four minutes.

- **Shallow** ("the process is up and can answer HTTP") can pass while the server's database connection is dead, so traffic routes into a black hole.
- **Deep** ("I can reach my dependencies") is honest but **correlated**. When a shared dependency blips, *every* server fails together and a partial problem becomes total unavailability.

**The standard answer: shallow checks decide routing; deep checks feed alerting.** A server should leave the pool for *its own* problems (out of memory, wedged, overloaded, disk full), not because a dependency everyone shares is having a bad moment. Let dependency failures surface as errors the app handles (timeouts, fallbacks, circuit breakers) and as pages to a human.

**Readiness versus liveness.** Kubernetes makes the distinction explicit, and it helps outside Kubernetes too:

| Probe | Question | On failure | Should it check dependencies? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Liveness** | "Is this process wedged beyond recovery?" | Container is **restarted** | No. A slow database would restart every pod at once |
| **Readiness** | "Should I receive traffic right now?" | Removed from endpoints; **not** restarted | Only things specific to this instance: warming up, draining, overloaded |
| **Startup** | "Have I finished starting?" | Liveness and readiness wait for it | None |

A liveness probe that calls the database turns a 30-second slowdown into a **restart storm**: every pod restarts, comes back cold and hits the struggling database with warm-up traffic.

A typical balancer configuration (HAProxy syntax, illustrative):

```text
backend app
    balance leastconn
    option httpchk GET /healthz
    default-server check inter 5s fall 3 rise 5 slowstart 60s
    server app1 10.0.1.11:8080
    server app2 10.0.1.12:8080
```

`inter 5s` probes every 5 seconds, `fall 3` marks down after 3 failures, `rise 5` marks up after 5 passes, and `slowstart 60s` ramps a returning server's weight (Chapter 11).

**How long does detection take?** Probes every 5 s with 3 failures needed is up to about **15 seconds** before ejection, plus the probe timeout. At 50,000 req/s over 72 servers one server carries about 694 req/s, so roughly `694 × 15 ≈ 10,400` requests reach it before active checks notice. Two things shrink that: **passive checks**, which notice within a few failed requests, and **retries on another server** for requests that are safe to retry (idempotent, or never sent).

Active checks cost something at scale too. In a mesh where 1,000 sidecars each check 72 backends every 5 s, that is `1,000 × 72 ÷ 5 = 14,400` checks per second, one reason meshes lean on passive detection.

**Flapping.** A server under garbage-collection pressure passes, fails, passes, fails. With thresholds of 1 and 1 it is added and removed every few seconds, and each re-entry floods it again. **Hysteresis** fixes it: require several failures to eject and *more* passes to return (say 3 down, 5 up). Then only a steadily sick server leaves and only a steadily well one returns, eased in with slow start.

**Fail open.** If every server looks unhealthy, the likelier explanation is that the *check* is wrong. Many balancers then **ignore health and route to everyone**. Envoy calls this the **panic threshold** (by default, with fewer than half the hosts healthy it balances across all). Serving something beats serving nothing.

    [*] --> Starting
    Starting --> Warming: passes healthy threshold
    Warming --> Healthy: slow-start window ends

<h2 id="outliers">Chapter 10 · The server that fails fast</h2>

**What is it?** **Gray failures** are partial breakage: a server slow on one path, or failing one request in twenty. They slip past active checks because the check is one cheap request and the failure lives somewhere else. The balancer, though, sees *every real response*. **Outlier detection** (passive health checking, outlier ejection) uses that.

**Why do we need it?** Say a server's disk fills up. `/healthz` does not touch the disk, so it answers every probe with 200, while answering 5% of real users with 500. A seller complaining that one image upload in twenty fails is how you find it. The check was a doctor who only took a pulse. This one had a pulse.

**How it works:**

- **Consecutive errors.** Eject a server after, say, five `5xx` responses or connection failures in a row.
- **Error-rate or latency outliers.** Eject a server whose success rate or latency is far worse than the rest of the fleet's.
- **Ejection time grows.** The first ejection lasts, say, 30 s; a server ejected again stays out longer. A briefly sick server returns quickly; a chronically sick one stays out.
- **Ejection is capped.** Never eject more than a set fraction of the pool (Envoy defaults to 10%). If *everything* fails, the problem is not one server, and ejecting all of them turns "every request errors" into "no request is answered".

Outlier ejection is a **circuit breaker per backend**: stop sending to the one that is failing, probe it later. The gateway's circuit breaker (Chapter 15) is the same idea per *dependency*.

| Signal | Catches | Misses |
|---|---|---|
| Active check (shallow) | Crashed, wedged, unreachable servers | Partial failures on paths the check does not use |
| Active check (deep) | Broken dependencies | Nothing useful for routing: fails all servers together |
| Passive / outlier ejection | Gray failures on real traffic, fast-failing black holes | Idle servers with no traffic to judge |

<Trace title="Catch the quiet failure" steps={outliers} takeaway="Real responses are the best health check. Eject outliers with growing timeouts, and cap ejection so a fleet-wide problem fails open instead of empty." />

**When should I use it?** Always as the second layer beside active checks, whenever the balancer sees real responses (L7, or a mesh).

**A common slip:** relying on active checks alone. Name passive ejection as the second layer, and the cap that stops it ejecting the whole pool.

**Interview takeaway.** "Active checks find dead servers; passive outlier ejection finds sick ones from real responses. Ejection is capped, so a global problem fails open."

<h2 id="deploys">Chapter 11 · Leaving quietly, arriving slowly</h2>

**What is it?** Two halves of server lifecycle. **Connection draining** (AWS calls it deregistration delay, 300 seconds by default on an ALB target group) is how a server leaves. **Slow start** is how a server arrives.

**Why do we need them?** Back to the 502 minute. During a deploy the pipeline tells the balancer to remove server 3 *and* kills the process in the same second. Two hundred requests are halfway through. They become 502s. Then the new server 3 joins a least-connections pool with zero connections, receives a thousand requests in the next second while its JIT compiler is cold, slows down, fails its health check, is ejected, rejoins and is flooded again. We kick servers out of a moving car and throw new ones into the deep end.

**Draining, step by step:**

1. **Stop sending new requests.** Remove the server from the pool, or fail its readiness check.
2. **Let in-flight requests finish**, up to a deadline (say 30 s). Close idle keep-alive connections; for HTTP/1.1 send `Connection: close`; for HTTP/2 send GOAWAY so clients move new streams elsewhere.
3. **Then stop the process.**

**The Kubernetes race.** When a pod is deleted, Kubernetes removes it from the Service endpoints *and* sends SIGTERM at about the same time. Proxies across the cluster learn about the endpoint change a moment later. A process that exits straight away on SIGTERM still gets requests from proxies that have not heard. The usual fix is a short **preStop hook** (sleep a few seconds) so the removal propagates, then stop accepting, finish in-flight work and exit, all inside the **termination grace period** (30 seconds by default).

<Figure caption="Draining done right. The pause lets every proxy forget the pod before it stops accepting work; in-flight requests finish before exit.">
  <Mermaid code={`sequenceDiagram
    participant K as Kubernetes
    participant LB as Proxies / balancer
    participant P as Pod
    K->>LB: remove pod from endpoints
    K->>P: preStop hook (sleep 5 s)
    Note over LB: proxies stop sending new requests
    K->>P: SIGTERM
    P->>P: stop accepting, finish in-flight
    P-->>K: exit (within 30 s grace period)`} />
</Figure>

The same idea inside an app (Python, sketch):

```python
import signal, threading, time

draining = threading.Event()

def drain_and_stop():
    time.sleep(5)           # let every proxy notice before we stop accepting
    server.shutdown()       # stop accepting; in-flight handlers finish
    # then wait for in-flight work with a deadline shorter than the grace period

def on_sigterm(*_):
    draining.set()          # /ready now returns 503 so the balancer stops routing here
    threading.Thread(target=drain_and_stop).start()   # not inline: see below

signal.signal(signal.SIGTERM, on_sigterm)
server.serve_forever()      # main thread; returns once shutdown() is called
```

Do not call `server.shutdown()` inside the handler itself. Python runs the handler on the main thread, which is the thread running `serve_forever()`. `shutdown()` waits for that loop to stop, so the program waits on itself and hangs. Starting a second thread avoids it.

**Long-lived connections do not drain on their own.** A WebSocket server holding 40,000 connections might wait forever. Set a drain deadline, close connections deliberately (a WebSocket close frame), and have clients **reconnect with jitter** so 40,000 reconnects do not arrive in the same second.

**Slow start.** A new or returning server starts with a small fraction of its normal weight and ramps up over a window (say 60 s). Envoy, HAProxy (`slowstart`) and AWS's application balancers offer this. On an AWS ALB, slow start mode works with round robin only, not with least outstanding requests. Without it, least-connections and least-response-time balancing *especially* flood a newcomer: it has zero connections and no latency history. Cold JIT, empty caches and unopened pools make a server's first seconds its slowest, exactly when it receives the most traffic.

**How deploys use the balancer:**

| Strategy | How the balancer is used | Rollback |
|---|---|---|
| **Rolling** | Replace a few servers at a time (Chapter 1: 10% of 84 out leaves about 66% utilisation) | Roll forward or back the same way |
| **Blue-green** | Run the new version as a complete second pool, flip the balancer from blue to green | Flip back |
| **Canary** | Weighted routing: 1%, then 5%, then 25% to the new version while watching errors and latency | Set the weight to 0 |

**Interview takeaway.** "Drain with a deadline, pause before shutdown in Kubernetes, slow-start newcomers. Canaries use weights, blue-green flips a pool."

<h2 id="lb-ha">Chapter 12 · Who balances the balancer?</h2>

**What is it?** The question every interviewer eventually asks: *"All your traffic goes through this one box. Isn't the load balancer a single point of failure?"* The short answer: **balancers run as redundant pairs or fleets behind a floating virtual IP, several balancer addresses can be published in DNS, and at planet scale anycast announces one address from many places.** Here is what sits behind that sentence.

**Active-passive with VRRP (keepalived).** Two balancers share a **virtual IP (VIP)**, an address not tied to either machine. The **master** holds it and sends VRRP advertisements on the local network about once a second. The **backup** listens. If it misses roughly three advertisements in a row (plus a small priority-based skew) it declares itself master, takes the VIP, and sends a **gratuitous ARP** so switches learn the address now lives on a different machine. Clients never change the address they use.

- **Failover takes a few seconds**, and **connections open on the dead master are lost** unless connection state was synced to the backup (conntrackd does this on Linux; most designs let clients reconnect).
- **Split brain.** If the link carrying advertisements fails but both boxes are healthy, both claim the VIP. Use redundant advertisement paths, and health scripts so a box that cannot see the network steps down.
- **Half your capacity is idle.** That is the price of simplicity.

**Active-active with ECMP.** The router announces the VIP to several balancer instances and spreads flows across them by hashing each packet's 5-tuple (**equal-cost multi-path** routing). All instances serve at once. If one dies, the router rehashes and some flows arrive at a different instance. For those connections to survive, **every instance must pick the same backend for the same flow**: consistent hashing plus connection tracking, the design Google described for Maglev. This is how L4 tiers scale out instead of up.

**Cloud balancers are fleets.** A managed balancer's name resolves to several addresses across zones, and the provider runs, replaces and scales the machines behind them. "The ALB" is not one box. It still has limits (connection rates, how fast it scales for a sudden surge). AWS says an ALB scales automatically to the traffic it detects, and for a known spike like a sale you can reserve a minimum capacity ahead of time (LCU reservation, at least 100 LCUs) instead of waiting for it to scale.

<h2 id="global">Chapter 13 · Choosing a region</h2>

**What is it?** **Global load balancing** chooses a region (or edge site) before any local balancer sees the request. The <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/networking/`}>networking lesson</a> explained DNS and anycast as mechanisms; here they are balancing tools.

**The tools:**

- **GeoDNS.** The authoritative DNS server answers with a different region's address depending on where the query came from. Cheap and universal. Its knowledge is the **resolver's** location, not the user's, partly fixed by EDNS Client Subnet when resolvers send it.
- **Latency-based routing.** The DNS service answers with the region that has the **lowest measured latency** from the resolver's network, not the nearest on a map.
- **Weighted DNS.** Send 5% of answers to a new region, or shift traffic off a region gradually.
- **Health-checked failover.** Stop returning a dead region's address. Only as fast as cached TTLs let the change spread. The **TTL** (time to live) is how long resolvers may cache an answer.
- **Anycast.** One IP address announced from every site using BGP (the internet's routing protocol). Routing delivers each packet to the nearest site in routing terms. Failover happens as fast as routes withdraw, with no client caches involved, and attack traffic spreads across every site. Many global edge networks run anycast at the front and forward to regions over their own backbone.

| | DNS (Geo / latency / weighted) | Anycast |
|---|---|---|
| Steers by | The resolver's location or network | The network path of each packet |
| Failover speed | Bounded by TTLs, and by clients that cache longer | Seconds to minutes, as routes update |
| Fine control | Easy: weights, per-region answers | Coarse: routing decides; you can withdraw or prepend routes |
| Usual role | Choosing among regions | The edge's front door; stateless or short-lived traffic |

**The failover you must pay for.** Assumption for this example: 60,000 req/s over three regions, 20,000 each. One region fails:

```text
60,000 ÷ 2 surviving regions = 30,000 req/s each = 1.5× normal
```

With N regions, each must carry `N ÷ (N − 1)` of its normal share: 1.5× for three, **2× for two**. It is Chapter 1's zone arithmetic at continental scale. And it is not only app servers: databases, caches and queues in the survivors must absorb it too, and the survivors' caches are cold for the failed region's users.

**What failing over a region really requires**, say at least the first two:

1. **Capacity** in the survivors (above).
2. **Data** that is already there: replicated and recent enough. Failing over stateless traffic is easy; failing over a database is a consistency decision (the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/databases/`}>databases lesson</a> covers replication and failover).
3. **A trigger you trust**: health checks of the whole region, not one server, plus a human or automated decision that avoids flapping between regions.

<h2 id="placement">Chapter 14 · Where the decision lives</h2>

**What is it?** Inside the system, one service calling another still needs balancing. The decision can live in three places: a central proxy, the calling client, or a proxy beside each instance.

**Why does it matter?** Say a company has 400 internal services in Java, Go and Python. Each team has wired its own retries, timeouts and balancing. A central proxy fleet is the box that, if it sneezes, everyone gets the flu. Where the decision lives sets your latency, your blast radius and what every team must maintain.

      CP["Control plane"] -.-> E1
    end`} />

| | **Server-side (central proxy)** | **Client-side (library)** | **Sidecar (service mesh)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extra hops | One shared hop | None | Two local hops (one each side) |
| Language support | Any | One library per language | Any |
| Sees | All traffic to the service | Only this client's requests | Only this instance's requests |

- **Client-side** balancing needs a list of backends from **service discovery** (DNS, a registry, or a control-plane protocol like Envoy's xDS, which gRPC can also speak directly). Each client sees only its own requests, which is where two random choices with a latency estimate (Chapter 6) shine.
- **A service mesh** (Istio with Envoy sidecars, Linkerd with its own proxy) moves balancing, retries, timeouts, outlier ejection and mutual TLS into a proxy beside every instance, configured from a central control plane.
- **Server-side** is still right at the **edge**, where clients are phones and browsers you do not control.

<h2 id="gateway">Chapter 15 · The front door</h2>

**What is it?** An **API gateway** is an L7 reverse proxy that owns the **cross-cutting API concerns**, so every service does not reimplement them:

- **Authentication.** Validate the token (JWT, API key) once at the edge; services trust the gateway's verdict.
- **Rate limiting and quotas.** Per API key, per user, per IP as a coarse backstop. The <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/rate-limiter/`}>rate-limiter walkthrough</a> designs this component end to end.
- **Routing** to the right service, versioning (`/v1`, `/v2`), request or response transformation.
- **Circuit breaking.** Stop hammering a failing downstream: fail fast, recover by probing later.
- **Observability.** Request logs, metrics and a trace id on every request.
- Sometimes **aggregation**: one client call fanned out to several services (a backend-for-frontend).

<Figure caption="One front door that authenticates, limits, routes and protects the services behind it.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    C["clients"] --> GW["API Gateway\n· authN (validate JWT)\n· rate limiting\n· routing to services\n· circuit breaking\n· request logging"]
    GW --> S1["user svc"]
    GW --> S2["order svc"]
    GW --> S3["media svc"]`} />
</Figure>

**Why do we need circuit breaking at the front?** Say two minutes before a sale, bots triple traffic on the checkout endpoint. The order service slows. Every caller of the order service slows. Listing pages, which never touch checkout, slow too, because every request was let in, every request was allowed to wait, and every waiting request held a thread.

**Circuit breakers in one paragraph** (the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/core-concepts/`}>core concepts lesson</a> has the full state machine). Without one, when the order service slows, every caller waits its full timeout on every call; threads pile up and the failure cascades upstream. With one, after enough failures the breaker **opens**: calls fail immediately (or get a fallback) without touching the order service. After a cool-down it goes **half-open** and lets a few probe calls through; success closes it. It protects the **caller** (threads, latency budget) and the **struggling service** (no retry storm). *Fail fast, probe later.*

**The edge, layer by layer.** In a large system the front door is several layers, each rejecting what it can as early and cheaply as possible:

1. **Anycast edge and DDoS absorption.** Volumetric floods are spread across every site and filtered; SYN floods are handled with SYN cookies at L4.
2. **WAF (web application firewall).** L7 rules that block known attack shapes (SQL injection, malicious payloads, bad bots). The OWASP Core Rule Set is the common open baseline.
3. **Rate limiting.** Per key or user at the gateway, answering `429` with `Retry-After`.
4. **Gateway.** Authentication, routing, circuit breaking.
5. **Internal balancing** to healthy, warm, stateless servers.

**Trust at the gateway.** If services trust a user-id header the gateway adds, two things must hold. Services must be **reachable only through the gateway** (network policy, or mTLS identities checked by each service), and the gateway must **strip any such header a client sent**. Otherwise anyone who reaches a service directly, or forges the header, is whoever they claim to be.

```http
GET /orders/17 HTTP/1.1
Host: api.example.com
Authorization: Bearer eyJ...        ← validated at the gateway
X-User-Id: 42                       ← set by the gateway; a client-sent copy is stripped first
```

**When to draw which.** A monolith or a single service: "LB" is enough. Microservices with a public API: draw a gateway and **say two of its jobs out loud**. Do not draw both a gateway *and* a separate balancer per service unless asked about scale. Every box earns its sentence.

It is the same answer as Chapter 12, applied to a fleet: the gateway runs as many stateless instances behind an L4 tier or a managed balancer, across zones, with health checks and draining like any service. Its *state* (rate-limit counters, keys) lives in shared stores built to be redundant. The real risk is configuration: one bad route or auth rule pushed to every instance at once. <h2 id="retries">Chapter 16 · Retries that make it worse</h2>

**What is it?** A **retry** sends a failed request again. Balancers, gateways, meshes and client libraries all offer them, because most failures are brief and a second attempt usually works. They are also the most common way a small overload becomes a large outage.

**Why does it matter?** A retry is extra load added at the worst time. Take the numbers:

- A service is overloaded and fails some requests. Each failed request is sent again, so the offered load rises exactly when capacity is short. This feedback can hold a system down even after the original trigger is gone.
- **Retries multiply across layers.** If the gateway, the service and the database client each make up to 3 attempts, one user request can become `3 × 3 × 3 = 27` attempts at the bottom when everything fails.
- Assumption: 50,000 req/s arrive and every request fails once with a 3-attempt limit. The fleet now sees up to 150,000 req/s, three times what it was already failing to serve.

**How it works, and the fixes:**

1. **Retry only what is safe.** A request that never reached a server (connection refused) is safe. A `GET` or other idempotent request is safe. A `POST` that may have run is not, unless it carries an **idempotency key** the server uses to ignore duplicates. nginx, for example, does not pass a POST, LOCK or PATCH to the next server once it has been sent to one, unless you add `non_idempotent` to `proxy_next_upstream`.
2. **Retry on a different server.** The next attempt should not go back to the instance that just failed.
3. **Limit attempts, and use a retry budget.** Allow retries only up to a fraction of normal traffic (say 10%) per client or per proxy. When the budget is spent, fail instead of retrying.
4. **Back off with jitter.** Wait longer after each failure and randomise the wait so clients do not retry in lockstep.
5. **Retry at one layer only.** Pick the layer closest to the user (or the one that has the most context) and let the others fail fast.
6. **Pass a deadline down.** If the user gave up after 2 s, nobody downstream should still be working.
7. **Shed load on the server.** Return a fast `503` with `Retry-After` when overloaded instead of queueing until timeouts fire, and respect that header on the client.

```python
import random, time

class RetryBudget:
    def __init__(self, ratio=0.1, cap=10.0):
        self.ratio, self.cap, self.tokens = ratio, cap, cap
    def on_request(self):
        self.tokens = min(self.cap, self.tokens + self.ratio)
    def try_spend(self) -> bool:
        if self.tokens >= 1:
            self.tokens -= 1
            return True
        return False

def call(send, budget, attempts=3, base=0.1, cap=2.0):
    budget.on_request()
    for n in range(attempts):
        try:
            return send()
        except ConnectionError:
            if n == attempts - 1 or not budget.try_spend():
                raise
            time.sleep(random.uniform(0, min(cap, base * 2 ** n)))   # full jitter
```

Every real request earns 0.1 of a retry. A retry costs 1. So in steady state retries are at most about 10% of traffic, and during a total outage the budget empties and retries stop.

**Retry versus hedge.** A *retry* waits for a failure. A **hedged request** sends a second copy after a short delay without waiting for failure, to cut tail latency. Both add load. Hedge only idempotent requests and only after a delay near the 95th percentile, so few requests are duplicated.

| | Retry | Retry with budget and backoff | Circuit breaker |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Any failure | Failure and budget left | Failure rate over a window |
| Extra load in an outage | Up to attempts × traffic | Capped near the budget (say 10%) | Near zero while open |
| Protects | Brief blips | Blips, without feeding an overload | The callee and the caller's threads |

  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    U["1 user request"] --> G["Gateway\n3 attempts"]
    G -->|"up to 3"| S["Service\n3 attempts each"]
    S -->|"up to 9"| D["Database client\n3 attempts each"]
    D -->|"up to 27"| DB[("Database")]`} />
</Figure>

**When should I use them?** Retries are right for brief, independent failures such as a connection reset during a deploy. They are wrong as the response to overload, which needs shedding, backoff and breakers.

**Interview takeaway.** "Retries only for safe requests, on another server, with backoff, jitter and a budget, at one layer. Overload is handled by fast shedding and circuit breakers, not by trying again."

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="2 · Which algorithm, and why not round robin?">

Round robin is right when requests cost the same and servers are identical. Ours vary, a 10 ms read next to a 10 s export, so I want load-aware: least outstanding requests. With several balancer instances, global least-loaded herds on stale data, so I sample two servers at random and pick the less loaded. That cuts the busiest server's load exponentially compared with random and needs no coordination. If servers held per-key state I would use consistent hashing with bounded loads instead.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="3 · A server dies. How quickly do you stop sending it traffic?">

Active checks every 5 s with a threshold of 3 means up to about 15 s, which at peak is roughly 10,000 requests to a dead server. So passive detection does most of the work: connection failures and consecutive 5xx eject it within a handful of requests, and idempotent requests are retried on another server. Ejection is capped and there is a panic threshold, so a fleet-wide problem fails open rather than emptying the pool.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="4 · Isn’t the load balancer a single point of failure?">

No single box. Within a site, balancers run as an active-passive pair sharing a virtual IP with VRRP (the backup takes over in a few seconds), or as an active-active fleet behind ECMP using the same consistent hash so flows that move between instances keep their backend. Managed cloud balancers are fleets across zones. Globally, DNS returns several addresses or anycast announces one address from many sites. And I check what all copies share: config pushes and certificates.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="5 · Your gRPC service has 20 pods, but 5 are hot and 15 idle. What is wrong?">

The balancer is counting connections. gRPC multiplexes every call from a client onto one long-lived HTTP/2 connection, so an L4 balancer, including a plain Kubernetes Service, pins each client to one pod, and new pods only get new connections. Fix it with per-request balancing: an HTTP/2-aware L7 proxy or mesh sidecar, or client-side balancing with service discovery. A server-side maximum connection age is a cheap partial fix that rebalances every few minutes.

</Reveal>

<h2 id="cheat-sheet">Cheat sheet</h2>

| When you see… | Say… |
|---|---|
| "What does the LB do?" | Spread work, notice the dead, let servers come and go, never fail alone |
| How many servers? | Peak ÷ (capacity × target utilisation); check a zone loss: survivors carry 1.5x with three zones |
| Path routing, auth, retries | L7: terminates TLS (re-encrypt if every hop must be encrypted) |
| Non-HTTP, huge connection counts, raw throughput | L4; DSR if responses dwarf requests |
| gRPC / HTTP/2 imbalance, new pods idle | Connection-level balancing; use L7 per request, client-side, mesh, or max connection age |
| Uniform requests, identical servers | Round robin or random |
| Different server sizes, canaries | Weighted (smooth) round robin |
| Request costs vary wildly | Least outstanding requests / EWMA latency |
| Many balancers, stale data | Power of two random choices |
| Server-local state (cache, sockets) | Consistent hashing on the key; bounded loads for hot keys |
| "Sticky sessions" | Only for rebuildable state; move sessions to tokens or a shared store |
| Health checks | Shallow for routing, deep for alerts; liveness never checks dependencies; hysteresis; panic threshold |
| A server that fails fast | Errors are fast: outlier ejection, capped |
| Deploy 502s | Drain (stop new, finish in-flight, then exit); preStop pause; slow start |
| "LB is a SPOF?" | VRRP pair with a floating VIP, or ECMP fleet with consistent hashing; DNS / anycast globally |
| Multi-region | GeoDNS / latency DNS (TTL-bound) or anycast; each region sized for N ÷ (N − 1) |
| Many services, many languages | Mesh sidecars; client-side for the hottest path; server-side at the edge |
| Public API, microservices | Gateway: auth, rate limits, routing, circuit breaking; say two jobs out loud |
| Overload, retries | Safe requests only, other server, backoff with jitter, retry budget, one layer, shed load |

## Six mistakes to leave behind

1. **"It distributes traffic."** Say what it sees (L4 or L7), what it balances (connections or requests), and how it chooses.
2. **gRPC behind an L4 balancer.** One connection per client means one pod per client. Balance per request.
3. **Deep health checks for routing.** A shared dependency's blip removes every server at once. Shallow for routing, deep for alerts.
4. **Deploys without draining or slow start.** The balancer cannot save requests on a process you kill mid-flight.
5. **A 70% target with no failure check.** Losing a zone of three multiplies survivor load by 1.5x. Size for the failure, not the average.
6. **Retries at every layer.** Attempts multiply. Retry once, in one place, with a budget.

**Further study**

- *Site Reliability Engineering* (Google, free online): [Load Balancing at the Frontend](https://sre.google/sre-book/load-balancing-frontend/) and [Load Balancing in the Datacenter](https://sre.google/sre-book/load-balancing-datacenter/). DNS and VIP balancing, subsetting and weighted least-loaded policies from people who run it at enormous scale. The same book has a chapter on addressing cascading failures, which covers retries.
- [Maglev: A Fast and Reliable Software Network Load Balancer](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi16/technical-sessions/presentation/eisenbud) (NSDI 2016): ECMP, consistent hashing and connection tracking in an L4 fleet, Chapter 12's active-active design.
- *The Power of Two Choices in Randomized Load Balancing* (Michael Mitzenmacher, IEEE TPDS 2001) and his survey with Richa and Sitaraman, *The Power of Two Random Choices: A Survey of Techniques and Results*: the theory behind Chapter 6, including stale information.
- [Consistent Hashing with Bounded Loads](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.01350) (Mirrokni, Thorup, Zadimoghaddam): Chapter 7's hot-key fix.
- [Envoy: load balancers](https://www.envoyproxy.io/docs/envoy/latest/intro/arch_overview/upstream/load_balancing/load_balancers) and [outlier detection](https://www.envoyproxy.io/docs/envoy/latest/intro/arch_overview/upstream/outlier): least request with two choices, ring hash, Maglev, panic thresholds and ejection, documented precisely.
- [gRPC load balancing](https://grpc.io/blog/grpc-load-balancing/) (gRPC blog): why connection-level balancing fails for gRPC, and the proxy versus client-side options.
- [Kubernetes: liveness, readiness and startup probes](https://kubernetes.io/docs/tasks/configure-pod-container/configure-liveness-readiness-startup-probes/) and [pod termination](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/pods/pod-lifecycle/#pod-termination): Chapters 9 and 11 from the source.
- [nginx upstream module](https://nginx.org/en/docs/http/ngx_http_upstream_module.html): weighted round robin, `least_conn`, `hash … consistent`, `random two`.
- [RFC 5798: VRRP version 3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5798): advertisements, priorities and master election.
- [Katran](https://github.com/facebookincubator/katran): Meta's open-source L4 balancer built on XDP, a readable modern take on the same ideas as Maglev.

**Look these up yourself**

- **Deterministic subsetting.** Why each client should talk to a subset of backends, not all of them, once fleets reach thousands of instances.
- **Join-the-shortest-queue and its cousins.** The queueing theory under least-loaded policies, and why JSQ is optimal only with fresh information.
- **Load reporting from backends (ORCA in gRPC and Envoy).** Servers telling balancers their real utilisation instead of balancers guessing from latency.
- **Proxyless gRPC with xDS.** Client-side balancing configured by a mesh control plane, with no sidecar.
- **Sidecar-less meshes** (Istio's ambient mode, per-node proxies). What changes when the proxy moves out of the pod.
- **QUIC load balancing.** Routing on QUIC connection IDs so a connection survives a client's address change.
- **Hedged and tied requests** ("The Tail at Scale", Dean and Barroso). Sending a second copy after a delay to cut tail latency, and the load it adds.
- **Metastable failures.** Systems that stay overloaded after the original trigger is gone, often because of retries.
- **Global server load balancing with capacity awareness.** Spilling traffic to the next-nearest region when the nearest is full, not only when it is down.
