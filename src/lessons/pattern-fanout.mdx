<h2 id="prologue">Prologue · One post, twelve million copies</h2>

Imagine a social app where one account has 12 million followers. She posts six photos in half an hour from a live event. Under the obvious design, every post is copied into the home feed of every follower: 12 million small writes per post, 72 million in total.

The background workers that do those copies can only manage 30,000 writes a second. The backlog takes 40 minutes to drain. Every ordinary post queued behind hers (a birthday photo, a lost cat) shows up 40 minutes late. Nothing is down. The system is just doing exactly what it was designed to do.

The root cause is a design decision: *which side of the system pays for copying a post to many people*. There are two sides, writing and reading, and you can choose which one pays. That choice is this whole lesson.

**The pattern in one sentence.** One user's action must appear to many users (followers, subscribers, channel members), and you must choose **when** the expensive many-copies work happens: at write time (**push**, also called fan-out on write), at read time (**pull**, fan-out on read), or split by who is writing (**hybrid**).

**Trigger words:** "feed", "timeline", "followers see", "subscribers", "notify all members", "home page shows posts from people you follow", "broadcast". When you hear one, the interviewer is waiting for three things:

1. The push/pull trade-off **with numbers**.
2. The **celebrity problem**, named before they raise it.
3. A **threshold**, said out loud.

**How it connects to other lessons.** The timeline is a cache, so it needs the four promises from <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/caching/`}>Caching</a> (key, TTL, invalidation, failure plan). The follow table is a many-to-many join, so say so. Fan-out is asynchronous, so it leans on <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/queues-streams/`}>Queues & Streams</a> (outbox, at-least-once, lag). The lesson ends with a full 45-minute round on the clock, because pacing matters as much as content.

<h2 id="two-strategies">Chapter 1 · Pay now or pay later</h2>

Start with the simplest question: when Alice opens the app, how does the system produce her home feed?

Assumptions for this lesson (illustrative, interview-scale): 200 million people open the app, 10 times a day each. 100 million posts a day. Everyone follows about 200 accounts.

**Pull (fan-out on read).** Each post is stored once, indexed by author. To build Alice's feed:

1. Look up the 200 accounts she follows.
2. Fetch each one's recent posts.
3. Merge them newest-first (a k-way merge, usually with a heap of size k).
4. Return the top 20.

**Push (fan-out on write).** Every user has a prepared **timeline**: a short list of post ids in a cache. When Bob posts:

1. Store the post once.
2. A background worker inserts Bob's post id into the timeline of every follower.
3. When Alice opens the app, reading is one range query on one key.

<Figure caption="One post and one read under each strategy. Push does its work between the post and the timeline. Pull does its work on every read.">

    W->>T: insert post id (push)

**Worked numbers.** The ÷10⁵ seconds-per-day rule is from <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/estimation/`}>Estimation</a>. Peak is taken as 3× average, which is an assumption, not a law.

| | Daily | Average/s | Peak/s |
|---|---|---|---|
| Timeline reads (200M DAU × 10 opens) | 2B | 20,000 | **60,000** |
| Posts written | 100M | 1,000 | **3,000** |
| **Pull:** per-author lookups (reads × 200 followees) | 400B | 4,000,000 | **12,000,000** |
| **Push:** timeline inserts (posts × at most 200 pushed followers) | 20B | 200,000 | **600,000** |

Push makes **twenty times fewer** operations (12,000,000 ÷ 600,000 = 20), and it moves them off the path the user is waiting on. The general rule: **move the work to the rare side of the ratio.** Here there are 20 timeline reads for every post written (2B ÷ 100M). Each read shows 20 posts, so every post written is displayed about 400 times.

**The tail-latency tax on pull.** A pull read waits for the slowest of its 200 lookups. Suppose each lookup is slower than its own p99 1% of the time. The chance a read hits at least one slow lookup is 1 − 0.99²⁰⁰ ≈ **87%**. Wide fan-in turns every backend's rare slow call into your common case.

| | Push (fan-out on write) | Pull (fan-out on read) |
|---|---|---|
| Write cost | O(followers) per post, done asynchronously | O(1) per post |
| Read cost | O(1) range read + hydration | O(followees) lookups + merge, on the user's clock |
| Freshness | Seconds behind (fan-out lag) | Always current |
| Storage | One timeline per active user (ids only) | None beyond the posts |
| Worst case | **Celebrity posts**: millions of inserts per post | **Heavy readers** who follow thousands: read QPS × followees |
| Good when | Reads ≫ writes, follower counts bounded | Writes ≫ reads, tiny scale, or no follow graph |

<Trace title="Count the work" steps={strategies} takeaway="Pull costs reads × followees; push costs posts × followers. Feeds are read far more than written, so push wins until one author's follower count explodes." />

<Reveal q="At the first 10,000 users, which would you build?">

Pull, and say why. With 10,000 users, a read that merges 200 followees is a single indexed query (`WHERE author_id IN (...) ORDER BY created_at DESC LIMIT 20`) on one database, comfortably fast. Push adds a queue, workers and a cache cluster for no benefit. Fan-out is a response to scale. Naming the point where you would switch ("when timeline reads start to dominate database load") is the senior move.

</Reveal>

**Common trap: "Pull, with a cache in front."** Every user's merged timeline is different, so caching the merged result per user *is* materialising a timeline. That is push with extra steps and worse invalidation. If you want the cache, say push.

<h2 id="celebrity">Chapter 2 · The celebrity problem</h2>

**The celebrity problem is write amplification.** Under push, one post costs one insert per follower, so the cost of a post depends on who posted it.

Using the opening example:

- 6 posts × 12,000,000 followers = **72,000,000 inserts**.
- With 30,000 spare inserts per second, that is 72,000,000 ÷ 30,000 = 2,400 s = **40 minutes** of backlog, and every ordinary post queued behind it waits too.

At interview scale: one post from a **50M-follower** account is 50,000,000 inserts. Against the 600,000 inserts/s peak budget from Chapter 1, that is 50,000,000 ÷ 600,000 ≈ **83 seconds of the entire fan-out fleet** for one post. It also writes 50M × 16 bytes = **800 MB** of timeline entries, most into timelines that will scroll past it.

Pure pull dies at read volume (12M lookups/s). So every real design converges on **hybrid**:

- **Ordinary accounts: push.** Below a follower **threshold** (say **100,000**, an assumption you would tune), fan out on write.
- **Celebrities: pull.** Above the threshold, do not fan out at all. Their posts go only into their own **author timeline** (`user_posts:{authorId}`, newest first, capped).
- **At read time**, merge: Alice's pushed timeline (one range read) plus the recent posts of the few celebrities she follows (one cached list each), then a merge of sorted lists, top 20.

<Figure caption="A hybrid read. The pushed part and the pulled part are both sorted newest-first, so the merge is cheap. The celebrity's list is the same object for millions of readers, so it lives in every feed server's memory.">

    participant A as Alice's app
    participant TS as Timeline service
    participant L1 as In-process cache

    TS->>R: newest 25 ids from tl:alice (pushed part)
    TS->>L1: recent posts of the celebrity she follows
    L1-->>TS: hit (refreshed about once a second)

    TS-->>A: 20 posts + nextCursor`} />
</Figure>

Here is the merge in Python. Both inputs are lists of `(score_ms, post_id)` sorted newest first, so `heapq.merge` with `reverse=True` is enough.

```python
import heapq
from itertools import islice

def merge_timeline(pushed, celebrity_lists, limit=25):
    # pushed: [(score_ms, post_id), ...] newest first, from Redis ZRANGE ... REV
    # celebrity_lists: one newest-first list per celebrity the viewer follows
    merged = heapq.merge(pushed, *celebrity_lists, reverse=True)
    return list(islice(merged, limit))
```

**Choosing the threshold.** Say a number and the reason: *"Above 100K followers, a single post's fan-out would take a noticeable slice of fleet time and delay everyone else; below it, push. I would tune it from the fan-out lag metrics."* At 100,000, the most expensive pushed post is 100,000 inserts, about 0.17 s of the 600,000/s fleet. The read side pays one extra lookup per followed celebrity, and those lookups are the hottest, best-cached keys in the system.

<Trace title="Break the celebrity" steps={celebrity} takeaway="Celebrity means write amplification. Hybrid: push below a stated threshold, pull above it, merge at read, and treat each celebrity's post list as a hot key." />

**Subtle difference: pushed-for-everyone vs hybrid, and push-cost vs pull-cost per author.** Per author, push costs `posts × followers` inserts and pull costs `reads × followers` lookups. So why a threshold on followers at all?

<Reveal q="If follower count cancels out of both costs, why threshold on followers?">

Over a whole day, for one author, push costs `p × F` inserts and pull costs `r × F` lookups (`p` posts per day, `r` timeline reads per follower per day), so `F` cancels. Three things the daily average hides:

1. **Burstiness.** Push spends all `F` inserts in the seconds after the post. At `F` = 50M that burst blocks the fleet and creates lag for everyone else. Pull spreads the cost over the day's reads.
2. **Caching collapses pull for celebrities.** The `r × F` pull lookups all read *the same* small list. With an in-process cache on 200 feed servers, Redis sees about 200 reads/s for it, not millions. An ordinary author's list gets no such sharing.
3. **Memory.** Push writes the celebrity's ids into tens of millions of timelines, crowding out posts from people those users follow more closely.

So the threshold is about burst size and cache sharing, not daily totals.

</Reveal>

| | Pure push | Pure pull | Hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50M-follower author posts | 50M inserts, minutes of fleet time | One list write | One list write |
| Reader follows 200 ordinary accounts | One range read | 200 lookups | One range read |
| Reader also follows 3 celebrities | One range read | 203 lookups | One range read + 3 cached list reads |
| Extra moving parts | Queue, workers | None | Both, plus a threshold and a merge |

**Refinements worth one sentence each:**

- **Hysteresis.** Use two thresholds (become "pulled" above 100K, return to "pushed" below 80K) so an account hovering near the line does not flip back and forth.
- **Celebrity superfans.** A user who follows 300 celebrities pays 300 extra lookups. That is rare, and you can cap the pulled set by recency.
- **Per-edge decisions.** Some designs push a celebrity's posts only to followers who interact with them often and pull for the rest. Mention it as a tuning idea, not the baseline.
- **Follower lists for celebrities.** The follower list itself can be huge. Store it across several shards (for example, partitioned by `(followee, follower_hash)`) so paging through it does not hammer one partition. This matters for pushed accounts near the threshold and for the rebuild paths.

**Common trap: "Queues absorb the spike."** They do, by holding a multi-hour backlog. Absorbing is not finishing, and every ordinary post waits behind it.

<h2 id="timeline-storage">Chapter 3 · Ids, not posts</h2>

A first push prototype often copies the whole post (text, author name, avatar URL) into every follower's timeline. It is fast to build. Then a user fixes a typo, and the old version is still in twelve thousand feeds. And twelve thousand copies of every post use a lot of memory.

**Timelines hold post ids, not post bodies.** Fan-out copies the cheap reference; the content lives in exactly one place. At read time the service **hydrates**: it fetches the 20 post bodies by id from a post cache (one multi-get), then the authors from a user cache, then counts from a counter cache. Edits and deletes take effect everywhere at once because there is only one copy to change.

<Figure caption="Hydration. The timeline is only a list of ids. Bodies, authors and counts come from shared caches, and the databases are touched only on misses.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    TS["Timeline service"] -->|"1 · ZRANGE 25 ids"| TL[("tl:userId\nsorted set")]
    TS -->|"2 · MGET post bodies"| PC[("Post cache")]
    TS -->|"3 · authors"| UC[("User cache")]
    TS -->|"4 · counts"| CC[("Counter cache")]
    PC -.->|"miss"| PDB[("Post store")]
    UC -.->|"miss"| UDB[("User store")]`} />
</Figure>

**The data structure: a Redis sorted set per user.**

```text
key     tl:{userId}                      e.g. tl:42
member  postId                           e.g. "1840221933092847616"
score   post time in milliseconds        e.g. 1791457200123

on fan-out:   ZADD tl:42 1791457200123 1840221933092847616
              ZREMRANGEBYRANK tl:42 0 -801         keep only the newest 800
on read:      ZRANGE tl:42 +inf -inf BYSCORE REV LIMIT 0 25 WITHSCORES
```

Why a sorted set and not a list:

| | List (`LPUSH` + `LTRIM`) | Sorted set (`ZADD` + `ZREMRANGEBYRANK`) |
|---|---|---|
| Order | By **arrival**; out-of-order fan-out misorders the feed | By **score** (post time), whatever order inserts arrive in |
| Retried insert | Duplicate entry | **No-op**: same member, same score |
| Insert cost | O(1) | O(log N) |
| Memory | Smaller | Larger (skiplist + hash table per entry) |
| Paging by cursor | By index (shifts as items arrive) | By score (stable) |

The sorted set costs a little more memory and buys **ordering and idempotency**, which the pipeline in Chapter 5 relies on. A list is defensible if your queue guarantees per-follower ordering and you dedupe at read time. Say that if you pick it.

**Two details that separate people who have run this:**

- **Do not use a 64-bit id as the score.** Redis scores are double-precision floats, exact only up to 2⁵³ (about 9 × 10¹⁵). Time-ordered 64-bit ids are around 10¹⁸, so they round, and neighbouring ids collide or misorder. Use a millisecond timestamp as the score (about 1.8 × 10¹², exact in a double) and the id as the member. Members with equal scores are ordered lexicographically, which matches numeric order for ids of the same length.
- **Cap the length.** Keep the newest **800** entries. `ZREMRANGEBYRANK tl:42 0 -801` removes ranks 0 through N−801, leaving the newest 800; if N ≤ 800 the range is empty. Nobody scrolls 40 pages. A reader who does falls back to a slow pull from author timelines.

Here is the worker's write in Python (redis-py). One pipeline per Redis shard, so a batch costs one round trip instead of 1,000:

```python
def write_batch(redis_for_shard, post_id, score_ms, follower_ids):
    pipe = redis_for_shard.pipeline(transaction=False)
    for uid in follower_ids:
        key = f"tl:{uid}"
        pipe.zadd(key, {post_id: score_ms})
        pipe.zremrangebyrank(key, 0, -801)
    pipe.execute()
    # Production caveat: a crash mid-batch leaves some timelines written.
    # That is fine because the batch is retried and ZADD of the same
    # member and score is a no-op.
```

**Worked numbers: how big is every timeline?** (All assumptions for this example.)

- Entry ≈ 8-byte id + 8-byte score = **16 bytes** raw. 800 entries = **12.8 KB** per user.
- Timelines only for users **active in the last 30 days** (Chapter 6). Assume **300M** monthly actives (200M daily actives is two-thirds of them): 300M × 12.8 KB = **3.84 TB** raw.
- Redis sorted sets beyond a small size spend several times the raw bytes per entry on pointers and hash tables. Budget **~4×** (benchmark it): about 15 TB, plus one replica is about **30 TB of RAM**, roughly **300 cache nodes** at ~100 GB usable each.
- Spread over 300 nodes, the 600,000 inserts/s peak is about **2,000 inserts/s per node**, which is light. Timelines are a **memory** problem, not a throughput problem. If memory dominates the bill, pack each timeline into a compact binary blob (16 bytes per entry, close to raw) at the cost of read-modify-write on insert.

**The four promises for the timeline cache** (from <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/caching/`}>Caching</a>; say all four):

1. **Key:** `tl:{userId}`, a sorted set of post ids scored by time, capped at 800.
2. **TTL:** none while the user is active; the key expires after **30 days without a read** (refresh `EXPIRE` on read), on the same clock fan-out uses to skip inactive users.
3. **Invalidation:** none needed for content, since ids are references. Deletes, edits and blocks are filtered at hydration (Chapter 7).
4. **Failure plan:** the cache is **load-bearing** (falling back to pull for everyone is 12M lookups/s). A lost shard means rebuilding its users' timelines by pull, rate-limited, serving a degraded feed (own posts + celebrity posts) meanwhile.

<Trace title="Shape the timeline" steps={storage} takeaway="Sorted set: score = millisecond time, member = post id, capped at 800. Ordering comes from the score, retries are no-ops, and the content lives elsewhere." />

**Common trap: "Store the feed in the database."** Say *where*: the post store is the source of truth; the timeline is a derived, rebuildable cache. If you cannot say what happens when the timeline is lost, you have not decided whether it is a cache or a database.

<h2 id="pagination">Chapter 4 · Cursors, not offsets</h2>

Imagine a user scrolls to page two of the feed and sees three posts they have already seen. Reproduction: three posts arrived while they read page one, and page two was `offset=20`. Everything shifted down by three.

**Feeds paginate with cursors, never offsets.** New posts arrive at the top while the user reads, so "skip 20" means something different a second later. A cursor names a **position in the data**: "posts older than the last one I showed you."

| | Offset (`?offset=20`) | Cursor (`?cursor=...`) |
|---|---|---|
| What it means | "Skip the first 20 items" | "Items after this position" |
| New post arrives at the top | Page 2 repeats items from page 1 | Page 2 is unchanged |
| Deletes | Items shift up; one is skipped | Unaffected |
| Cost on a deep page | Skips N rows | Seeks directly by score |
| Works with Redis sorted set | By rank, which shifts | By score, which is stable |

<Figure caption="One timeline, newest on the left. Page one ended at the post marked CURSOR. Three new posts arrived since; with a cursor, page two starts right after the cursor anyway.">
  <ArrayStrip name="tl:42" indices={false} values={['new', 'new', 'new', 'p1', '…', 'p20', 'p21', '…', 'p40']} tones={{ 0: 'ghost', 1: 'ghost', 2: 'ghost', 3: 'done', 4: 'done', 5: 'done', 6: 'hot', 7: 'hot', 8: 'hot' }} pointers={[{ at: 5, label: 'CURSOR', tone: 'blue' }]} regions={[{ from: 0, to: 2, label: 'arrived later', tone: 'dim' }, { from: 3, to: 5, label: 'page 1', tone: 'green' }, { from: 6, to: 8, label: 'page 2', tone: 'gold' }]} />
</Figure>

**The cursor** is an opaque, encoded `(scoreMs, postId)` of the last item shown: the timestamp plus a tie-breaker, as in <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/delivery-framework/`}>the framework's API chapter</a>. The next page:

```text
GET /timeline?limit=20&cursor=eyJ0IjoxNzkxNDU3MjAwMTIzLCJpZCI6IjE4NDAyMjE5MzMwOTI4NDc2MTYifQ
  decode → (t = 1791457200123, id = 1840221933092847616)
  ZRANGE tl:42 1791457200123 -inf BYSCORE REV LIMIT 0 25 WITHSCORES
  drop entries with score = t whose id is not smaller than the cursor id (already shown)
  merge with celebrity posts older than the cursor, hydrate, filter, return 20 + nextCursor
```

The score bound is **inclusive** so posts sharing the cursor's millisecond are not lost. The id tie-breaker removes the ones already shown. The same cursor applied to each celebrity's author timeline keeps the merge consistent.

**Three more read-path habits:**

- **Pull to refresh** uses the opposite cursor: "newer than the first item I have" (`since`). The client prepends.
- **Cache-first reads.** The common read touches no database: timeline (Redis), post bodies (post cache, `MGET`), authors (user cache), counts (counter cache). The database sees only cache misses, and the post cache's hot set is small: two days of posts at 100M/day × ~500 bytes ≈ **100 GB**.
- **Read your own writes.** The author must see their own post immediately, even if fan-out is behind. The post service inserts the id into the author's own timeline synchronously (one `ZADD`) before returning, or the read path merges the author's latest posts. Pick one and say it.

**Over-fetch for filtering.** Read 25 ids for a 20-item page. Hydration will drop some (deleted posts, blocked authors; Chapter 7), and over-fetching keeps pages full. If a page still comes back short, fetch again from the new cursor rather than returning a half page.

**When a user follows someone new.** The follow service adds the edge. For a pushed account, the feed will not have that person's older posts, so a **backfill** (a recommendation, not a standard) copies their last 10 to 20 post ids into the follower's timeline with the original scores. `ZADD` makes it safe to repeat. For a pulled (celebrity) account, nothing is needed because the read merge picks it up.

<Reveal q="The user scrolls past entry 800, the end of the cached timeline. What now?">

Fall back to pull: for that rare deep scroll, query the author timelines of their followees for posts older than the cursor and merge, accepting a slower response. Say how rare it is ("nobody reads 40 pages; we would measure") and that it shares the rebuild code from Chapter 6. Do not grow every timeline to serve a handful of deep scrollers.

</Reveal>

**Common trap: using the post id alone as the cursor** while sorting by a separate timestamp. If the two orders disagree even slightly (ids issued on different machines), pages skip or repeat. The cursor must be the **same key you sort by**, plus a unique tie-breaker.

<h2 id="pipeline">Chapter 5 · The fan-out pipeline</h2>

Imagine an operations graph with one line per fan-out worker. Most are flat. One shows a plateau lasting eleven minutes. Cause: a single author with 90,000 followers. One message, one worker, one partition. Everything behind it on that partition waits for one account's fan-out to finish.

**The write path, end to end.** Posting is synchronous only up to durability; fan-out is asynchronous. The user's request returns as soon as the post is stored and the event is published.

<Figure caption="The fan-out pipeline. Two queues: one event per post, then many small batches of follower ids, so one big author is spread across the whole worker pool.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    C["Client"] -->|"POST /posts"| PS["Post service"]
    PS -->|"1 · insert"| PDB[("Post store\nsource of truth")]
    PS -->|"2 · own timeline"| TL[("Timeline cache\nRedis sorted sets")]
    PS -->|"3 · PostCreated\n(outbox)"| Q1[["posts topic\nkeyed by author"]]
    Q1 --> EX["Fan-out planner"]
    EX -->|"follower pages"| G[("Follow graph\nfollowers by followee")]
    EX -->|"batches of ~1,000 ids"| Q2[["timeline-writes\nkeyed by cache shard"]]
    Q2 --> FW["Fan-out workers × N"]
    FW -->|"pipelined ZADD + trim"| TL`} />
</Figure>

1. **Store, then publish.** The post service writes the post to the post store and publishes `PostCreated` through a transactional outbox, so a crash cannot store the post and lose the event (see the outbox chapter in <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/queues-streams/`}>Queues & Streams</a>).
2. **Plan.** A planner consumes `PostCreated`, checks whether the author is above the celebrity threshold (if so, stop: nothing to push), then pages through the author's followers, filters to active ones, and emits **batches** of about 1,000 follower ids.
3. **Write.** Workers take batches, group ids by Redis shard, and send pipelined `ZADD` + `ZREMRANGEBYRANK` commands: one round trip per shard per batch instead of one per follower.

**Why two queues.** A single "fan out this post" message processed by one worker makes a big author's fan-out **serial** and blocks the partition behind it, which is the eleven-minute plateau above. Splitting into batches spreads one author across every worker, and small authors no longer wait behind big ones. For extra isolation, route authors with large follower counts to a **separate lane** with their own workers, so ordinary posts always have capacity.

**Ordering.** You do not need global order, and you should not pay for it. Timeline order comes from the **score** (post time), so batches can land in any order and the feed still reads newest-first. Per-author order in the posts topic (keyed by author id) keeps a post's create and later delete events in sequence.

**Deduplication.** Delivery is at-least-once: a worker that crashes after writing 600 of 1,000 entries gets the batch again. `ZADD` of an existing member with the same score is a **no-op**, so the retry is harmless. This is why Chapter 3 chose the sorted set: idempotency by data structure, not by bookkeeping.

**Lag and back-pressure.** **Fan-out lag** is the time from post stored to last timeline written. That is what users feel, so that is the SLO and the alert: *"p99 fan-out lag under 5 seconds for authors below the threshold"* (the 5 seconds is an assumption to tune).

- **Back-pressure** comes for free with a pull-based log: if Redis slows, workers fetch more slowly and lag grows instead of anything falling over. Lag *is* the back-pressure signal (<a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/queues-streams/`}>Queues & Streams</a>, "The line grows").
- **Drain time** = backlog ÷ (capacity − incoming). With 72M inserts against 30,000/s spare, that is 40 minutes. Double the workers to 60,000/s spare and it is 20 minutes, if Redis can absorb it.
- **Shed when behind.** If lag passes a ceiling, drop work that will never be read: skip followers inactive for more than a week, or treat the author as pulled for this post. Users rebuilding on login will still see it.
- **Posting never waits for fan-out.** The post is durable and visible on the author's profile and in their own timeline; followers see it late. A delay, not an outage.

<Trace title="Run the line" steps={pipeline} takeaway="Split fan-outs into batches across a shared pool, make inserts idempotent, order by score not arrival, alert on lag in seconds, and never fail a post because fan-out is slow." />

<Reveal q="A follower unfollows Bob while Bob's fan-out batch for her is still in the queue. Bob's post lands in her timeline after the unfollow. Is that a bug?">

A tiny one, and you fix it at read time rather than in the pipeline. The worker could re-check the follow edge before every insert, but that adds a graph lookup per follower to the hottest write path. Instead, hydration drops posts whose author the viewer no longer follows (Chapter 7's filtering). Say: *"The pipeline is eventually consistent with the follow graph; the read path enforces it."*

</Reveal>

**Common trap: "Fan-out is a for-loop in the post service."** Then a 90K-follower post holds an HTTP request open for minutes, and a crash halfway loses the rest. The loop belongs on a queue, in batches, with retries that are safe to repeat.

<h2 id="inactive">Chapter 6 · Inactive and new users</h2>

Imagine you query the fan-out logs and find that of every hundred timeline inserts last week, forty went to people who had not opened the app in over a month. The system is delivering mail to empty houses, very quickly.

Follower lists include everyone who ever pressed Follow, including accounts that left years ago. Pushing to them costs writes and memory for timelines nobody will read.

- **Skip inactive followers.** Keep a last-seen flag per user (an "active in the last 30 days" bitmap or set beside the follower lists). The planner filters on it. If 40% of follower edges point at inactive users, fan-out writes drop by **40%** compared with pushing to every follower: at interview scale, 240,000 of the 600,000 inserts/s peak.
- **Let their timelines expire.** The timeline key's 30-day idle expiry (Chapter 3) runs on the same clock, so memory goes only to people who read.
- **Rebuild on return.** When an inactive user logs in, the timeline read misses. The service **rebuilds by pull**: fetch the followee list, read each followee's recent posts from their author timeline, merge, write the newest 800 into `tl:{userId}`, mark the user active, serve page one. From then on they receive pushes like everyone else.
- **New users** take the same path: a new account has no timeline, so the first read rebuilds it from whatever they followed during sign-up (or from popular posts, if nothing).

<Figure caption="The rebuild path. It is also used after a cache shard is lost and for deep scrolls past the 800-entry cap, so it has to exist anyway.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    R["Read tl:userId"] -->|"hit"| OK["serve page"]
    R -->|"miss"| L["Rebuild guard\none rebuild per user"]
    L --> F["Followee list"]
    F --> A["Author timelines\nbatched by shard"]
    A --> M["Merge newest 800"]
    M --> W["ZADD into tl:userId"]
    W --> OK`} />
</Figure>

**The rebuild must be protected.** A user following 2,000 accounts costs 2,000 author-timeline reads. Batch them by shard, run them in parallel, and return page one as soon as the first 20 are known while the rest fills. After a cache-shard loss, thousands of users rebuild at once. That is a **cache stampede**: many requests missing at once and all hitting the backing store. The two usual fixes are a per-key lock so only one rebuild runs per user, and a global rate limit on rebuilds with a degraded page (own posts plus celebrity posts) served to users waiting for their turn. This is the timeline cache's failure plan from Chapter 3, made concrete.

<h2 id="deletes">Chapter 7 · Deletes, edits and blocks</h2>

Imagine a user posts something she regrets and deletes it within thirty seconds. Fan-out already copied it into 40,000 timelines. Do you chase 40,000 copies, or make sure nobody can see them?

**Lazy filtering at read time is the primary mechanism.** Because timelines hold ids, the post store is the only source of truth. Hydration checks every post it fetches and drops what the viewer should not see.

<Figure caption="Hydration as a filter. The ids in the timeline are only candidates. The truth is checked at the moment of display.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    IDS["25 candidate ids"] --> H["Hydrate\nMGET bodies"]
    H --> D{"deleted or\nauthor private?"}
    D -->|"yes"| DROP["drop"]
    D -->|"no"| B{"author in viewer's\nblocked set?"}
    B -->|"yes"| DROP
    B -->|"no"| U{"viewer still\nfollows author?"}
    U -->|"no"| DROP
    U -->|"yes"| KEEP["keep, take 20"]`} />
</Figure>

| Change | Source of truth | Read-time filter | Optional async cleanup |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Delete** | Post marked deleted (a tombstone) in the post store; post cache entry deleted | Hydration finds a tombstone, drop | A "delete fan-out" job removes the id from timelines when convenient |
| **Edit** | Post row updated; post cache key deleted | Nothing: hydration fetches the current body | None needed |
| **Block / mute** | Viewer's blocked set (cached, small) | Drop posts whose author is in it | Fan-out also checks blocks for new posts |
| **Unfollow** | Follow edge removed | Drop posts whose author is not followed (unless promoted or recommended) | Optional removal job |
| **Account private / suspended** | Author state in the user store | Hydration checks visibility, drop | None |

**Lazy vs eager deletion**, the difference that matters:

| | Eager (remove from every timeline) | Lazy (filter at read) |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of delete | 40,000 writes on the delete's critical path | One tombstone write plus one cache delete |
| Race with in-flight fan-out | Post can be inserted after you deleted it | None; truth is checked at display |
| Visibility after delete commits | Gradual, as writes land | Immediate for every later read |
| Extra read cost | None | One tombstone check per hydrated post, in the same multi-get |

- **The cost is small and bounded.** Over-fetching 25 ids per page absorbs the drops.
- **Correctness on the slow path too.** The delete must also reach the caches that hold the body: delete the post-cache key on write (delete, do not update; this is the caching lesson's rule) and purge CDN copies of any media.

**Common trap: promising that a deleted post vanishes "instantly everywhere".** It vanishes from every *read that happens after the delete commits*. A client that already rendered it keeps it until refresh, and a push notification already sent cannot be recalled. Say the honest promise.

<h2 id="ranking">Chapter 8 · Ranked feeds</h2>

Imagine product data showing that users who follow more than 500 accounts open the app less, not more: their newest-first feed is a firehose, and posts they care about scroll away before they look. The request becomes: make the feed know what each user cares about. The first engineering question is not "which model", it is: where do the posts come from before anyone ranks them?

**Chronological vs ranked.**

| | Chronological | Ranked |
|---|---|---|
| Order | Newest first, by score | By predicted interest (engagement, affinity, recency) |
| Paging | Stable cursor on `(time, id)` | Needs a **snapshot** per session (below) |
| Cost per read | Range read + hydrate | Candidate fetch + features + model scoring, hundreds of candidates |
| Explainability | Obvious | Opaque; needs tuning, experiments, guardrails |
| Good when | Small follow counts, live events, "Following" tabs | Large follow graphs, engagement-driven products |

**A ranked feed is three stages**, and fan-out is only the first:

<Figure caption="Ranking reuses the fan-out pattern as candidate generation. The recommender adds out-of-network candidates; the ranker orders everything; the blender applies product rules.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    T["Pushed timeline\nin-network ids"] --> CG["Candidate generation\n~1,000s of ids"]
    CP["Celebrity pulls"] --> CG
    RS["Recommender\nout-of-network"] --> CG
    CG --> F["Feature hydration\naffinity, engagement,\nrecency, media type"]
    F --> LR["Light ranker\ncheap, prunes to ~100s"]
    LR --> HR["Heavy ranker\nprecise model"]
    HR --> B["Blender\ndiversity, filters,\nads slots, freshness"]
    B --> P["Page of 20\n+ session snapshot"]`} />
</Figure>

1. **Candidate generation** (recall, cheaply). In-network candidates come from exactly what Parts One and Two built: the pushed timeline plus celebrity pulls. Out-of-network candidates ("posts you might like") come from the recommender's nearest-neighbour retrieval. <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/recsys/`}>Recommendation systems</a> is the full funnel.
2. **Scoring** (precision, expensively). Hydrate features (how often you interact with this author, the post's engagement so far, its age, its media type), run a light model to prune, then a heavier model on the survivors. Stay inside a latency budget (say 150 ms for the whole request, an assumption).
3. **Blending** (product rules). No more than two posts in a row from one author; drop posts already seen (a per-user seen set, often a Bloom filter); insert ads at fixed slots; boost very fresh posts; apply policy filters.

**Paging a ranked feed.** Rank order is not time order, and scores change as engagement arrives, so a "posts older than X" cursor repeats and skips. **Rank once per session**, store the ordered ids (a few hundred) with a short TTL (minutes), and make the cursor an offset into that **snapshot**. Pull-to-refresh starts a new snapshot.

| | Time cursor (chronological) | Snapshot offset (ranked) |
|---|---|---|
| Cursor holds | `(score_ms, post_id)` | Snapshot id + position |
| Stable because | The sort key never changes | The ordered list is frozen for the session |
| New posts appear | On refresh or `since` fetch | Only in the next snapshot |
| Storage | None extra | A few hundred ids per active session, minutes of TTL |

**Precompute or rank at read?** Ranking at read time uses the freshest engagement signals and costs CPU per request. Some designs pre-rank in the background (when the user is likely to open the app) and cache the result. A reasonable default: *"Rank at read for freshness; cache the ranked snapshot per session; pre-rank only if the model blows the latency budget."*

<Trace title="Rank and page" steps={ranking} takeaway="Fan-out generates in-network candidates; the ranker orders; the blender applies rules. Ranked pages need a session snapshot, not a time cursor." />

**Common trap: "We will use machine learning to rank the feed."** That is a box with no inputs. Name where candidates come from, how many, the latency budget, and how paging stays stable.

<h2 id="counters">Chapter 9 · Hot counters</h2>

Imagine a viral photo. One row in the likes table takes fifty thousand updates a second, and every update waits for the same row lock.

Every post shows counts (likes, replies, reposts, views), and those counts are a fan-**in** problem: many writers, one number. A naive `UPDATE posts SET likes = likes + 1` serialises every like on one row.

**Separate the fact from the count.**

- **The fact:** a `likes` row keyed by `(postId, userId)`. That is what answers "did I already like this?" and stops double-likes (the primary key dedupes). It shards cleanly by post id.
- **The count:** a derived number that may lag a second or two. Users do not notice 41,207 vs 41,213.

**Sharded counters.** Split the counter into N sub-counters; each increment picks one at random; a read sums all N.

<Figure caption="A sharded counter. Writers spread across sub-counters; a reader sums them and caches the total for a second or two.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    W1["like"] --> C1["counter:post:7:0"]
    W2["like"] --> C2["counter:post:7:1"]
    W3["like"] --> C3["counter:post:7:...63"]
    C1 --> S["sum on read"]
    C2 --> S
    C3 --> S
    S --> CACHE["cached total\n1 to 2 s"]`} />
</Figure>

- Viral post: **50,000 likes/s** (assumption). One contended row manages a few thousand updates/s at best (check your own database; this varies a lot).
- With **64 shards**: 50,000 ÷ 64 ≈ **781 updates/s per shard**, comfortable.
- Reads sum 64 values; cache that sum for 1 to 2 seconds so hydration (1.2M post reads/s at peak) does not hammer it.
- Make it adaptive: most posts get one shard; a post whose like rate crosses a line gets more.

In Redis the two pieces look like this:

```text
SADD likes:post:7 user:42          # fact: returns 1 if new, 0 if already liked
INCR counter:post:7:13             # count: shard chosen at random in 0..63
```

Both commands are not atomic as a pair. If the process dies between them, the counter and the set disagree. That is acceptable for a display count and one more reason not to treat it as authoritative. For large sets use a database table for the fact and a Redis counter for the display.

**Alternatives worth naming:**

| Approach | How | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Sharded counters** | N rows/keys, random increment, sum on read | Read cost × N; cache the sum |
| **Write coalescing** | Buffer increments in memory per server, flush every second | A crash loses up to a second of counts |
| **Stream aggregation** | Like events into a log, windowed aggregator, periodic counter write | Seconds of delay; the stream design from <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-timeseries/`}>Time-series</a> |
| **Redis `INCR`** | Atomic increment on one key | One key is one shard, a hot key again; shard the key |
| **HyperLogLog** (unique viewers) | Probabilistic distinct count | At most about 12 KB per key, about 0.81% standard error in Redis; cannot answer "did X view?" |

<Trace title="Count without contention" steps={counters} takeaway="Keep the fact (who liked) separate from the count (how many). Shard hot counters, cache the sum, and use HyperLogLog when uniques can be approximate." />

**Common trap: making the displayed count authoritative.** If a payout or a ranking signal depends on exact counts, compute it from the facts (the likes rows or the event log) in a batch job, never from the fast, approximate counter.

<h2 id="notifications">Chapter 10 · Notifications</h2>

Suppose the product asks: "When a celebrity goes live, tell her followers on their phones." All fifty million of them? That is the fan-out pipeline from Chapter 5 again, with one change: the timeline cache becomes a row of phones, inboxes and email servers that you do not own. Same shape, except now **the last hop can say no**.

**A notification system is fan-out to devices.** One event ("a celebrity is live", "Alex liked your post", "Your code is 482913") becomes deliveries on one or more **channels**: mobile push (through Apple's APNs and Google's FCM), email, SMS, and an in-app inbox. Everything in Part Two applies. Three things are new:

1. **The last hop is someone else's system** with its own rate limits, failures and costs (SMS costs real money per message).
2. **Users have preferences** (per type, per channel, quiet hours) and a legal right to opt out.
3. **Not all messages are equal.** A login code must arrive in seconds; a weekly digest can wait an hour.

<Figure caption="The notification pipeline. Checks happen once, before the split; each channel gets its own priority queues, workers, rate limits and retries.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    SRC["Producers\nlikes, follows, live,\nsecurity, marketing"] --> API["Notification API\nvalidate + idempotency key"]
    API --> EXP["Expand + enrich\nsegment → users,\ntemplate, locale"]
    EXP --> CHK["Preferences, quiet hours,\nper-user rate limit,\ndedup + aggregation"]
    CHK --> PQ[["push: high / low"]]
    CHK --> EQ[["email: high / low"]]
    CHK --> SQ[["sms: high"]]
    CHK --> IN[("In-app inbox\nper-user list")]
    PQ --> PW["Push workers"] --> APNS["APNs / FCM"]
    EQ --> EW["Email workers"] --> ESP["Email provider"]
    SQ --> SW["SMS workers"] --> SMS["SMS provider"]
    PW -.->|"failures"| DLQ[["retry with backoff → DLQ"]]
    APNS -.->|"invalid token"| DT[("Device tokens")]`} />
</Figure>

**The pieces, each one sentence in an interview:**

- **Per-channel pipelines.** Each channel has its own queue and workers, so a slow email provider cannot delay push notifications. Same isolation idea as Chapter 5's separate lanes.
- **Priorities.** Separate queues per class (transactional/security ahead of social ahead of marketing), each with reserved workers. A single FIFO cannot honour priority: a 20M-message marketing blast at 9:00 would sit in front of every login code.
- **Preferences.** Look up the user's settings once, after expansion and before the channel split: per notification type × channel, quiet hours in the user's time zone, unsubscribe. Cache them; they change rarely.
- **Rate limits, both directions.** Per **user** (no more than a few pushes an hour from social events; aggregate the rest) and per **provider** (a token bucket sized to the provider's documented throughput; check current docs). <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/rate-limiter/`}>Rate limiter</a> has the algorithms.
- **Dedup and aggregation.** An idempotency key per `(notificationId, channel, device)` stops redelivered messages from buzzing a phone twice. Social events inside a short window collapse into one: *"Alex and 41 others liked your post."* Push services also offer collapse identifiers so a newer notification replaces an older one on the device.
- **Retries.** Retry transient failures (timeouts, 5xx, 429) with **exponential backoff and jitter**, a capped number of attempts, then a **dead-letter queue**. Do not retry permanent failures: an "unregistered device" response means delete that token.
- **Device registry.** A user has several devices; each app install registers a token. Fan-out to a user means fan-out to their live tokens.
- **Tracking.** Record sent / delivered / opened per message (from provider callbacks and app opens), for debugging, for "why did I not get it?" support tickets, and for measuring whether notifications help or annoy.
- **The in-app inbox is a feed.** A per-user list of notification ids, capped, paged by cursor: push fan-out exactly as in Part Two.

Here is the send step with its idempotency check in Python. It shows the shape; the comment names the remaining race.

```python
def deliver(notification_id, device, payload):
    key = f"sent:{notification_id}:{device.id}"
    if not redis.set(key, "1", nx=True, ex=86400):   # SET key 1 NX EX 86400
        return "duplicate-skipped"
    try:
        provider.send(device.token, payload)
    except TransientError:
        redis.delete(key)          # allow the retry to send
        raise                      # queue retries with backoff + jitter
    except UnregisteredDevice:
        tokens.delete(device.id)   # permanent: stop sending to this token
    # Race: a crash after provider.send but before we return leaves the key set,
    # which is right. A crash between SET and send loses the notification.
    # For security codes prefer the opposite order (send, then record).
```

**Subtle difference: priority queue vs a single FIFO with a priority field.** A FIFO processes in arrival order whatever the field says. A marketing blast ahead of a login code delays the code. Separate queues with reserved workers make priority a property of the layout. Per-channel queues are a different axis: they isolate **providers** from each other, not priorities from each other. You need both.

**Worked numbers** (assumptions for this example):

- Volume: 200M DAU × 5 notifications a day = 1B/day, **10,000/s** average, **30,000/s** peak.
- Broadcast: "a celebrity is live" to **50M** followers. If our sending path to the push providers sustains 50,000/s (an assumption to measure, not a vendor limit), the blast takes 50,000,000 ÷ 50,000 = **1,000 s ≈ 17 minutes**. For a live event that is too slow for the last followers, so prioritise the followers most likely to care, and accept that a broadcast is minutes, not seconds.
- **The notification stampede.** If 5% of 50M open the app within a minute, that is 2,500,000 ÷ 60 ≈ **41,667 extra timeline reads/s**, two-thirds of the normal 60,000/s peak, on top of it. Staggering the send over several minutes is load management for your *own* read path.

<Reveal q="Exactly-once notifications: can you promise them?">

No. The queue delivers at least once, and the provider call and your "sent" record cannot be one atomic step: a crash between them means you do not know whether it went out. For security codes, a duplicate is harmless and a missing one is not, so you bias toward retrying. **0:08 · The bridge**

> **Candidate:** "So: post, follow, and a paginated home timeline; 60,000 timeline reads a second at peak against 3,000 posts; a few seconds of staleness is fine; the follower distribution has a very long tail. The numbers say: precompute timelines, and handle the tail separately. Does that match what you had in mind?"
>
> **Interviewer:** "Yes. Go on."

**0:09 · Core entities**

> **Candidate:** "Four nouns. **User**. **Post**: id, author, text, media references, created time. **Follow**: a follower and a followee. That's many-to-many, a self-join on User, so I'll store it as an edge table with an index in each direction: who-follows-X for fan-out, who-does-X-follow for rebuilds. That's about 100 billion edges. And the **Timeline**: not a source of truth, a derived per-user list of post ids. I'll keep it in a cache."

**0:11 · API**

```http
POST   /posts              { text, mediaIds? }  Idempotency-Key: <uuid>  → 201 { postId }
PUT    /follows/{userId}                                                   → 204
DELETE /follows/{userId}                                                   → 204
```

> **Candidate:** "The user always comes from the auth token. Posting takes an idempotency key, because a retried post on a flaky connection would otherwise publish twice, visibly. Follow is a PUT to a known URL, so it's idempotent by definition. The timeline is a live list, so cursor pagination: the cursor encodes the last post's timestamp plus its id as a tie-breaker, because offsets would shift as new posts arrive."

**0:15 · High-level design, one requirement at a time**

> **Candidate:** "Posting. Client to load balancer to a stateless post service. It writes the post to the post store, a wide-column or sharded relational store partitioned by post id with an author index, and publishes a PostCreated event through an outbox to a log, keyed by author. It also adds the post id to the author's own timeline so they see it instantly. Then it returns 201."
>
> "Following writes an edge into the follow graph, both directions."
>
> "Fan-out. A fan-out service consumes PostCreated, pages through the author's followers from the graph, keeps the ones active in the last 30 days, and pushes the post id into each follower's timeline: a Redis sorted set per user, score is the post time in milliseconds, member is the post id, capped at 800."
>
> "Reading. The timeline service reads the newest 25 ids from the user's sorted set, hydrates the bodies from a post cache with one multi-get, the authors from a user cache, filters anything deleted or blocked, and returns 20 with a cursor. Cache misses fall through to the stores."

> **Candidate:** "That serves all three features. Now the hard part: it doesn't survive the follower tail."

**0:25 · Deep dive 1: the celebrity problem**

> **Candidate:** "Pure push fails at celebrities. An account with 50 million followers posting once means 50 million inserts. At our 600,000-a-second fleet, that's about 83 seconds of all fan-out capacity, and every normal post waits behind it. Pure pull fails at reads: 60,000 reads a second times 200 followees is 12 million lookups a second, and each read waits for the slowest of 200 calls."
>
> "So hybrid. Accounts under 100,000 followers are pushed; over it, they're not fanned out at all, and their posts live only in their author timeline. At read time, the timeline service takes the pushed sorted set, fetches the recent posts of the few celebrities the user follows, and merges. Both lists are sorted, so it's cheap. At 100,000, the worst pushed post costs about a sixth of a second of fleet time. The celebrity lists are the hottest keys in the system, so every timeline server holds them in a one-second in-process cache; Redis sees a few hundred reads a second for each instead of millions. I'd use two thresholds, 100K up and 80K down, so accounts near the line don't flap, and tune the number from fan-out lag."

**0:30 · Deep dive 2: the timeline cache**

> **Candidate:** "Four promises for this cache. Key: tl colon user id, sorted set, score in milliseconds, not the 64-bit id, because scores are doubles and would lose precision. The member is the post id, capped at 800 with a rank trim after each insert. Ids, not bodies, so edits and deletes need no fan-out. TTL: none while active; the key expires after 30 idle days, the same clock the fan-out uses to skip inactive followers. Invalidation: deletes, blocks and unfollows are filtered at hydration, against the source of truth; I over-fetch 25 for a page of 20. Failure: it's load-bearing, since falling back to pull for everyone is 12 million lookups a second, so a lost shard's users are rebuilt by pull, rate-limited, with a degraded page meanwhile. A returning inactive user takes the same rebuild path on login."
>
> "Size: 300 million monthly actives times 800 entries times 16 bytes is 3.84 TB raw; with sorted-set overhead around 4× and one replica, about 30 TB of RAM, roughly 300 nodes. Spread over them, 600,000 inserts a second is about 2,000 per node. Memory is the cost driver, not throughput."

**0:34 · Deep dive 3: the fan-out pipeline**

> **Candidate:** "Fan-out is two stages so big authors don't block small ones: one event per post, then a planner emits batches of about a thousand follower ids onto a second queue that all workers share, grouped by Redis shard so each batch is one pipelined round trip per shard. Delivery is at-least-once, and that's fine: a re-sent ZADD with the same member and score is a no-op, so retries can't duplicate. Order doesn't matter in the queue, because the score orders the timeline. The metric that matters is fan-out lag, from post stored to last timeline written, with an SLO of five seconds at p99 and an alert on it. If lag spikes, the post itself is unaffected; followers see it late. Past a ceiling we shed: skip followers inactive for a week, who'll rebuild anyway."

**0:38 · Wrap-up**

> **Candidate:** "To recap: posting is a durable write plus an event; the timeline is a precomputed sorted set of ids per active user; celebrities are pulled and merged at read; reads are cache-only in the common case, well under 200 ms. At 10×, the first pressure is timeline memory, about 300 TB of RAM, so I'd lower the cap, keep timelines only for weekly actives, or pack entries into compact blobs; fan-out at six million inserts a second scales with workers and shards. If a Redis shard dies, its users' feeds rebuild by pull at a controlled rate. I'd monitor fan-out lag p99, timeline read p99, timeline cache hit rate, rebuild rate, and celebrity-merge latency. With more time: ranking, where the pushed timeline becomes candidate generation for a ranker, and notifications."

**0:41.** The interviewer's note afterwards: raising the celebrity problem unprompted at minute five, rather than waiting for the question at minute twenty, is what separates strong answers.

**Check the numbers yourself:** 200M × 10 = 2B ÷ 10⁵ = 20,000/s, ×3 = 60,000/s. 100M ÷ 10⁵ = 1,000/s, ×3 = 3,000/s. 2B ÷ 100M = 20 reads per post; × 20 posts per page = 400 displays per post. 3,000 × 200 = 600,000 inserts/s; 60,000 × 200 = 12,000,000 lookups/s. 50M ÷ 600,000 ≈ 83 s; 100,000 ÷ 600,000 ≈ 0.17 s. 100M × 500 B = 50 GB/day × 365 = 18.25 TB. 300M × 800 × 16 B = 3.84 TB; × 4 × 2 ≈ 30.7 TB; ÷ 100 GB ≈ 300 nodes; 600,000 ÷ 300 = 2,000/s per node. <h2 id="interview-script">Say it in the room</h2>

When the prompt is "Design a news feed", say the ratio, the two strategies, the tail, and a number, in that order. Pick your depth with the tabs below: the 30-second version for an opening, 2 minutes when the interviewer asks you to expand, and the detailed version for a deep-dive slot.

<AnswerModes id="fanout-news-feed" q="Design a news feed.">

<Fragment slot="short">

Feeds are read far more than written: 60,000 timeline reads a second against 3,000 posts at peak. So I precompute. Fan-out on write puts each post id into followers' Redis sorted-set timelines, and reads become one range read plus hydration. Pure push breaks on celebrities, since 50M followers means 50M inserts for one post, so it is hybrid: above about 100,000 followers, pull from a cached author list and merge at read.

</Fragment>

<Fragment slot="medium">

Feeds are read far more than written: at 200 million daily users opening the app ten times a day and 100 million posts, that is 60,000 timeline reads a second at peak against 3,000 posts. So I **precompute**: fan-out on write into a per-user timeline, a Redis sorted set of post ids scored by post time and capped at 800, and hydrate the bodies from a post cache at read time. Ids, not bodies, so edits and deletes need no fan-out; deletes, blocks and unfollows are filtered at hydration.

Fan-out runs **asynchronously**: the post is stored and an event published; workers push to active followers in batches, idempotently, and I alert on fan-out lag at five seconds p99. Pure push breaks on **celebrities**, so it is **hybrid**: accounts above 100,000 followers are not fanned out; their recent posts are pulled from a hot in-process cache and merged at read. Inactive users are skipped and rebuilt by pull when they return. Pagination is by cursor on time plus id.

</Fragment>

<Fragment slot="deep">

Numbers first: 60,000 reads/s and 3,000 posts/s at peak, 200 follows. Pull is 12M lookups/s and each read waits on the slowest of 200 calls; push is 600K inserts/s off the user path. So push for ordinary accounts.

**Storage.** `tl:{user}` sorted set: score is millisecond time (not the 64-bit id, scores are doubles), member is the post id, capped at 800 with `ZREMRANGEBYRANK`. About 3.84 TB raw, about 30 TB of RAM with overhead and a replica. The post store is the source of truth.

**Pipeline.** Outbox event, planner batches of about 1,000 follower ids, workers pipeline `ZADD` per shard. At-least-once is safe because the same `ZADD` is a no-op. Lag is the SLO and the back-pressure signal; past a ceiling, shed inactive followers. Posting never waits on fan-out.

**Tail and failure.** Above 100K followers (hysteresis 100K up, 80K down) skip fan-out and merge a one-second cached author list at read. A lost shard rebuilds by pull, rate-limited and single-flight per user, with a degraded page meanwhile. Reads use a `(time, id)` cursor and over-fetch 25 for 20. Ranking changes only the read path: pushed timeline plus celebrity pulls become candidates, paged by a session snapshot.

</Fragment>

</AnswerModes>

**Five follow-ups you will get:**

<Reveal q="1 · Why not just pull? It’s simpler and always fresh.">

Because reads dominate. Pull at 60,000 reads/s × 200 followees is 12M lookups/s, and every read waits for the slowest of 200 calls (1 − 0.99²⁰⁰ ≈ 87% of reads hit at least one p99-slow call). Push is 600,000 inserts/s, off the user's critical path. Pull is right at small scale, and for celebrities.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="2 · How did you pick 100,000 as the celebrity threshold?">

From fan-out lag: at 100K, the most expensive pushed post is about 0.17 s of a 600K/s fleet, so no single post can stall others for long. Above it, cost grows without bound (50M followers is about 83 s). Pull cost per celebrity is one lookup on a heavily cached list. It is an assumption I would tune from the lag metric, with hysteresis (100K up, 80K down) to stop flapping.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="3 · A user deletes a post that’s already in 40,000 timelines. What happens?">

The post is tombstoned in the post store and its cache key deleted. Timelines hold ids, so hydration sees the tombstone and drops it on every subsequent read; over-fetching 25 for 20 keeps pages full. An async job may clean the ids out later. No synchronous 40,000-write delete, and no race with an in-flight fan-out.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="4 · Redis loses a shard. What do users see?">

The timeline cache is load-bearing, so this is planned for: replicas fail over first. If the data is lost, affected users' timelines are rebuilt by pull on their next read, rate-limited so the post store is not flooded, and users waiting for a rebuild get a degraded page (their own and celebrity posts). Fan-out keeps writing to the new shard throughout.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="5 · Now make it ranked. What changes?">

The read path, not the write path. The pushed timeline plus celebrity pulls (plus recommender candidates if wanted) become candidates; features are hydrated; a light model prunes and a heavy model scores; a blender applies diversity, seen-filtering and ads. Paging changes: rank once per session, store the ordered ids briefly, and page by offset into that snapshot, because rank order is not time order.

</Reveal>

<h2 id="cheat-sheet">Cheat sheet</h2>

| When you see... | Say... |
|---|---|
| "Feed", "timeline", "followers see" | Fan-out: push vs pull vs hybrid, with the read:write ratio |
| Reads ≫ writes | Push: precompute timelines; reads become one range read |
| One author with millions of followers | Celebrity problem = write amplification, so hybrid, threshold about 100K, merge at read |
| Celebrity post lists read by millions | Hot key: in-process L1 cache, about 1 s TTL |
| What goes in the timeline | Post ids in a sorted set: score = ms time, member = id, cap 800 |
| Edits, deletes, blocks, unfollows | Lazy filtering at hydration; optional async cleanup |
| Paging a live feed | Cursor = (time, id) of last item; inclusive bound + tie-break |
| Paging a ranked feed | Per-session snapshot of ranked ids; cursor = offset into it |
| Fan-out implementation | Event via outbox, planner, batches of about 1,000, workers, pipelined per shard |
| Retries / redelivery | At-least-once + idempotent ZADD |
| "What if fan-out falls behind?" | Lag SLO (5 s p99), alert in seconds, shed inactive, posting unaffected |
| Inactive or new users | Skip fan-out; rebuild by pull on first read |
| Like / view counts | Facts (who liked) separate from counts; sharded counters; HyperLogLog for uniques |
| "Design notifications" | Per-channel, per-priority queues; preferences; rate limits; idempotency per device; backoff + DLQ; stagger broadcasts |
| TikTok-style feed | Pull from a recommender + rank; no follow-graph fan-out |
| Sizing | Reads/s, posts/s, inserts/s = posts × followers, timeline RAM = actives × cap × entry |

## Five mistakes to leave behind

1. **Choosing push or pull without the ratio.** Say reads per second against posts per second first; the choice follows.
2. **Not naming the celebrity problem before the interviewer does.** Say it with a number and a threshold.
3. **Copying post bodies into timelines.** Ids, then hydrate.
4. **A timeline cache with no failure plan.** It is load-bearing; say how it rebuilds.
5. **One queue for every notification.** Priority and channel isolation are the design.

- [TAO: Facebook's Distributed Data Store for the Social Graph](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc13/technical-sessions/presentation/bronson) (USENIX ATC 2013): how a social graph with both edge directions is stored and cached at scale.
- [Redis sorted sets](https://redis.io/docs/latest/develop/data-types/sorted-sets/): `ZADD`, range queries by score, rank trimming, and how equal scores are ordered.
- [Redis HyperLogLog](https://redis.io/docs/latest/develop/data-types/probabilistic/hyperloglogs/): memory bound and error rate for approximate unique counts.
- [Apple: sending notification requests to APNs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/usernotifications/sending-notification-requests-to-apns) and [Firebase Cloud Messaging](https://firebase.google.com/docs/cloud-messaging): collapse identifiers, priorities, and error responses such as unregistered tokens. Check current docs for limits.

**Look these up yourself**

- **"Feeding Frenzy" (Silberstein et al., SIGMOD 2010):** choosing push or pull per follower edge from producer and consumer rates; the formal version of Chapter 2's break-even question.
- **Snowflake-style id generation:** time-ordered 64-bit ids, and why they suit cursors and timelines (and why they do not fit in a double).
- **Bloom filters for "already seen" sets:** keeping ranked feeds from repeating posts across sessions within a fixed memory budget.
- **Count-min sketch:** approximate per-item counts and heavy hitters, the cousin of HyperLogLog for trending and abuse detection.
- **Exponential backoff with jitter:** why "full jitter" spreads retries better than fixed delays when thousands of clients fail together.
- **Notification fatigue and send-time optimisation:** how products decide *whether* and *when* to notify, not only how to deliver.
- **Edge-level hot partitions in the follow graph:** storing a celebrity's follower list across many shards so fan-out paging does not hammer one.
