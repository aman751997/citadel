<h2 id="prologue">Prologue · Ticks that lied</h2>

<Scene chapter="Prologue" title="Ticks that lied" mood="storm">

Kestrel Messages launched on a Tuesday. On Thursday at 21:00, a routine deploy restarted one gateway server, and Dev narrated what happened next in the incident channel, one line at a time.

*"Gateway 7 restarted. 500,000 sockets closed."* — *"All 500,000 clients reconnected at 21:00:01."* — *"Message store at 100% CPU. Every phone is asking for its history at once."* — *"Support says some messages show up twice. Some show two ticks and were never received."*

The next morning Mara draws two phones on the whiteboard, Alice on the left, Bob on the right, and a long arrow between them. She puts the marker on the arrow.

*"A message is somewhere on this line at every instant,"* she says. *"Tell me where it is. Tell me what happens if the box it's in dies right now. And tell me what each tick on Alice's screen is promising."*

</Scene>

**Realtime** is any product where the server has news before the client asks: a new message, a driver who moved, a document someone else edited, a score that changed. Plain HTTP is client-asks-server, so the pattern has three problems stacked on top of each other:

1. **Server push** — a way for the server to speak first (polling, SSE, WebSockets).
2. **Connection state** — once connections are long-lived, *which server holds Bob* matters, and those servers become stateful, fragile and expensive to restart.
3. **Delivery guarantees** — the network drops, duplicates and reorders, phones go into drawers for a week, and the product still promises "it got there, once, in order, and the ticks are true".

**Trigger words in a problem statement:** "live", "real-time", "instantly see", "chat", "presence / online status", "typing…", "watch together", "collaborative", "notify me when".

The whole lesson hangs on one sentence. Say it in every realtime design:

> **Push is the fast path. The store is the truth.** A message is persisted before anyone is told; push is an optimisation that usually gets it there in milliseconds; and every device can always recover what it missed by reading from the store after its own cursor. A design where a message exists only in a socket is broken.

**Why this lesson matters to you.** Chat is the most-asked realtime question, and it is the one where a plausible diagram hides the most hand-waves. Your gap register shows up here in disguise. **Silent skips**: "and then it's delivered" skips five decisions. **Decomposition from scratch**: this lesson is pattern-first, so that Slack, live comments, collaborative cursors and Uber's moving car all fall to the same machinery. **Cache hand-waves**: the connection registry is a cache of where devices are, and it needs the same four promises — key shape, TTL with a reason, invalidation, failure plan — or it will lie to you. **Pacing**: the walkthrough at the end runs on the 9 · 15 · 25 · 38 clock.

<h2 id="transport">Chapter 1 · Which pipe?</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 1" title="Which pipe?" mood="dawn">

Iris has three requests on one sticky note. Order status should update without a refresh. The match-day score widget should be live. And chat should feel instant, with "typing…" under the name.

You write WEBSOCKET three times. Mara crosses out two of them. *"How often does each one change? Who talks back? How many people are watching?"*

</Scene>

The transport choice is covered in depth in <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/networking/#realtime`}>Networking, Chapter 7</a>. Here is the recap you need to make the call fast:

| | How | Latency | Cost | Pick when |
|--|-----|---------|------|-----------|
| **Polling** | client asks every N sec | ≤ N sec | N× wasted requests, mostly empty | updates are rare/slow; simplicity wins (order status page) |
| **Long polling** | client asks; server holds the request until news or a timeout | near-instant | one held request per client + one request per message | push needed but sockets are blocked; fallback transport |
| **SSE** (server-sent events) | one long-lived HTTP response, server streams events down | instant | one connection per client; **one-way only** | server→client only: notifications, live scores, LLM token streams |
| **WebSocket** | full-duplex persistent TCP connection | instant | one connection per client + connection state; more infra | two-way chat, games, collaborative editing, live location |

The decision rule you can say verbatim: **"Poll if you can, SSE if the server only pushes, WebSocket only when the client also sends in real time."** Interviewers respect starting simple; jumping to WebSockets for a thing polling handles is box inflation. Long polling — "ask and hold until there's news" — is polling's smarter cousin; mention it as the historical middle step, and as the fallback when a corporate proxy kills WebSockets.

**Three questions pick the pipe:** *How often does it change? Who talks back? How many are watching?* Order status: four changes in forty minutes, nobody talks back → poll every 10–15 seconds. Scores: constant changes, nobody talks back, millions watching → SSE. Chat: frequent, both directions (messages, typing, receipts) → WebSocket.

**Worked numbers.** Kestrel has 20 million phones connected at the evening peak.

- Polling every 5 s for new messages: `20,000,000 / 5 = 4,000,000` requests per second, nearly all empty, and messages still arrive up to 5 s late.
- WebSockets: 20 million open connections, near-zero requests when idle, heartbeats every 30 s ≈ `20,000,000 / 30 ≈ 667,000` tiny frames per second — handled at the gateways, never forwarded deeper.

<Trace title="Pick the pipe" steps={transport} takeaway="How often, who talks back, how many watching. Rare: poll. One-way: SSE. Two-way and frequent: WebSocket — and accept the stateful tier." />

**Catch the trap:** "Real-time, so WebSockets." Real-time is a latency requirement, not a transport. Saying *"polling is enough here"* for an order page is a flex, not a cop-out — and it buys you time for the parts that need depth.

<h2 id="gateway">Chapter 2 · Servers that remember</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 2" title="Servers that remember">

Theo looks at the architecture diagram and taps the box labelled "API servers (WebSocket)". *"So when you deploy the API,"* he says, *"you disconnect everyone?"*

Nobody had thought of that. Mara draws a line down the middle of the box and writes GATEWAY on one side and CHAT SERVICE on the other. *"One side holds sockets. The other side thinks. Why would you want them apart?"*

</Scene>

A WebSocket or SSE server holds live connections, so unlike your stateless API fleet, **it matters which server a user is connected to.** That makes the connection tier the most fragile thing in the design, and the fix is to make it as dumb as possible.

**Split the tier in two:**

- **Gateways** (connection servers) terminate TLS, authenticate the socket once at connect time, hold the connection, run heartbeats, and pass frames inward and outward. They hold **no durable state** and almost no business logic. They change rarely, so they are deployed rarely.
- **The chat service** is stateless request-handling: validate, assign order, persist, route. It deploys ten times a day without dropping a single socket.

<Figure caption="The routing problem. Alice’s message lands on gateway 7; Bob is on gateway 22. Something has to know that.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    A["Alice's phone"] --- WS1["Gateway 7\n(holds Alice's conn)"]
    B["Bob's phone"] --- WS2["Gateway 22\n(holds Bob's conn)"]
    WS1 --> S["Chat service\n'deliver msg to Bob'"]
    S --> PS[("Connection registry\nor pub/sub")]
    PS --> WS2`} />
</Figure>

**The routing problem** — "a message for Bob arrives; which of my 40 gateways has Bob?" — has two standard answers:

1. **Connection registry**: a Redis map `user_id → device_id → gateway_id + connection_id`, written on connect, deleted on disconnect. Message for Bob → look up → send directly to that gateway (an internal RPC, or a per-gateway queue).
2. **Pub/sub**: every gateway subscribes to a channel per connected user (`user:{id}`) or per room (`room:{id}`) in Redis pub/sub (or similar); the chat service publishes; whichever gateway holds the connection gets it. No lookup, at the cost of subscription churn on every connect and disconnect, and broker fan-out traffic.

| | Registry + direct send | Pub/sub per user or room |
|---|---|---|
| Lookup cost | One read per delivery | None — publish and forget |
| Churn | One write per connect/disconnect | One subscribe/unsubscribe per connect/disconnect |
| Stale data risk | Entry can outlive the connection (Chapter 3) | Subscription dies with the gateway — self-cleaning |
| Big rooms | Look up every member | One publish; every subscribed gateway gets one copy |
| Delivery | Your RPC — you see failures | Redis pub/sub is fire-and-forget (at-most-once) — fine, because the store is the truth |

Either is fine — **choosing one out loud** is what counts. A common senior answer: the registry for 1:1 and small groups (precise, debuggable), topic subscriptions per gateway for big rooms and channels (Chapter 9).

**The registry is a cache. Give it the four promises.**

- **Key shape:** `conn:user:{userId}` → a hash of `deviceId → "gw-22:c-81f3"`. One key per user, one field per device, so multi-device (Chapter 10) is free.
- **TTL and why:** a TTL on every entry, refreshed on every heartbeat, sounds tidy and costs `20,000,000 / 30 ≈ 667,000` Redis writes per second at Kestrel's peak, all to say "still here". Cheaper: entries have no per-heartbeat refresh; instead **each gateway holds one liveness lease** (`gw:22:alive`, TTL ~10 s, renewed every few seconds). A router that finds an entry naming a gateway without a live lease treats it as stale.
- **Invalidation:** the gateway deletes the entry on disconnect — **conditionally**: only if the field still says *this* gateway and *this* connection id. Otherwise a slow cleanup on the old gateway deletes the fresh entry the reconnected device just wrote on another one.
- **Failure plan:** the registry is a routing hint, not the truth. If it's down, sends still persist and ack; pushes degrade; devices catch up by sync (Chapter 7) or wake by push notification.

**Sticky routing, three meanings — say which one you mean:**

1. **The connection itself** sticks to one gateway for its whole life. A **layer-4** load balancer (or a WebSocket-aware layer-7 one) is the front door; balance by **least connections**, not round robin, because connections live for hours and a restarted gateway should refill from new connections rather than stay empty while others overflow (<a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/load-balancing/`}>Load balancing</a>).
2. **Fallback transports** that span many HTTP requests (long polling) need sticky sessions so every request of one session reaches the gateway holding its state.
3. **Conversations stick to an owner** in the chat service, so one instance assigns the order for each conversation (Chapter 5). That is consistent hashing on `conversation_id`, not on users.

**Worked numbers: how many connections per gateway?** Assume roughly **10 KB of memory per idle connection** (kernel socket buffers, TLS state, a little app state — measure it for your stack; it varies several-fold with buffer tuning). A 64 GB gateway giving half its memory to connections could hold `32 GB / 10 KB ≈ 3.2 million`. Memory is rarely the limit. **Blast radius** is: every connection on a gateway reconnects when it dies. So pick a target — say **500,000 per gateway** (5 GB of connection memory) — and Kestrel's 20 million peak connections need `20,000,000 / 500,000 = 40` gateways, plus headroom to lose a zone.

<Trace title="Find Bob" steps={routing} takeaway="Registry or pub/sub — pick one out loud. Registry entries are deleted conditionally, and liveness comes from a gateway lease, not from every heartbeat." />

<Reveal q="Why not just let the chat service hold the WebSockets and skip the gateway tier?">

Because then every chat-service deploy, crash or autoscale event disconnects users and triggers a reconnect wave, and every bug in business logic shares a process with half a million sockets. Separating them lets the part that changes daily be stateless and the part that holds connections be small, boring and rarely deployed. It also lets you scale them independently: connections scale with concurrent users, chat logic with messages per second.

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** "The load balancer will route Bob's messages to his server." The load balancer chose a gateway when Bob connected; it doesn't answer "where is user X" for your services. The routing problem is yours to solve — and naming it is the deep dive the interviewer is waiting for.

<h2 id="heartbeats">Chapter 3 · Is anyone there?</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 3" title="Is anyone there?" mood="night">

Dev replays the incident timeline. Gateway 7 restarted at 21:00:00. Every client library had the same reconnect logic: wait one second, try again. *"So at 21:00:01,"* he says, *"half a million phones knocked on the door at once. TLS handshakes, auth, registry writes, and then every one of them asked the message store what it missed. The store said no."*

Mara writes one word on the board: *jitter*. Then a second: *heartbeat*. *"One is for when the line dies loudly. The other is for when it dies quietly."*

</Scene>

**Heartbeats: detecting the quiet death.** A phone that walks into a lift doesn't close its TCP connection; it just stops answering. TCP can take a very long time to notice (default keepalive timers on many systems are measured in **hours**), and the gateway keeps "delivering" into a dead socket. Meanwhile mobile carriers and home routers drop idle NAT mappings, often within minutes. So:

- Client and gateway exchange a **heartbeat** every ~30 s. WebSocket has ping/pong control frames (RFC 6455), but the browser API doesn't let your code send pings, so many apps send a tiny application-level heartbeat message instead.
- **Two missed heartbeats → the gateway closes the socket** and removes the registry entry. Detection is bounded at about a minute.
- Heartbeats **end at the gateway.** At 20 million connections they are ~667,000 frames per second fleet-wide — `500,000 / 30 ≈ 16,700` per gateway, trivial locally, ruinous if forwarded to Redis.

**Reconnect with backoff and jitter: surviving the loud death.** When a gateway dies, all of its clients learn at the same instant. If they all retry after the same fixed delay, they arrive together — a **thundering herd**, the same math as a cache stampede. Each reconnect costs a TCP and TLS handshake, an auth check, a registry write, and a **catch-up sync** against the message store. The fix lives in the client:

```text
delay(attempt) = random(0, min(cap, base × 2^attempt))      # "full jitter"
base = 1 s, cap = 30 s
attempt 0 → 0–1 s, attempt 1 → 0–2 s, attempt 2 → 0–4 s, … attempt 5+ → 0–30 s
```

Exponential growth backs off a struggling fleet; the randomness spreads clients that failed together; the cap keeps a user on a healthy network from waiting minutes.

**Worked numbers.** Gateway 7 held 500,000 connections; 39 gateways survive.

| Reconnect policy | Arrival rate | Per surviving gateway |
|---|---|---|
| Everyone after exactly 1 s | 500,000 in one second | ≈ 12,800 handshakes + syncs in one second each |
| Full jitter over a 30 s window | `500,000 / 30 ≈ 16,700` per second | ≈ 430 per second |

Same total work, spread thirty-fold. The message store sees ~16,700 catch-up queries per second instead of half a million in one.

**Planned restarts: drain, don't drop.** A deploy should never look like a crash. Take the gateway out of the load balancer (no new connections), then send each client a "please reconnect" control frame **at a randomised time** over a drain window — say 5 minutes: `500,000 / 300 s ≈ 1,700` reconnects per second, invisible to users. Roll gateways a few at a time.

    LB->>G7: stop sending new connections

<Trace title="The reconnect wave" steps={reconnect} takeaway="Crashes are loud: jittered exponential backoff spreads the herd. Silence is quiet: heartbeats find dead sockets. Deploys are neither: drain with randomised reconnects." />

<Reveal q="A whole region’s gateways go down and 20 million clients reconnect. Is jitter enough?">

Jitter spreads the wave, but 20 million reconnects over 30 seconds is still ~667,000 per second — too much for a fleet that's itself recovering. Add **server-side protection**: the gateways (or the load balancer) cap new connections per second and reject the excess quickly with a retry-after hint, which clients honour as the floor of their next backoff. Catch-up sync is rate-limited per device and paginated, so a phone that was offline for a week doesn't pull its whole backlog in one request. Clients should also widen the jitter window after repeated failures, which full-jitter backoff does naturally.

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** "Clients just reconnect." That sentence hides the herd. Say *"exponential backoff with full jitter, a cap, and server-side admission control"* — and say what each reconnect costs, because the catch-up read is what actually hurts.

<h2 id="message-flow">Chapter 4 · One message, end to end</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 4" title="One message, end to end" mood="dawn">

Mara hands you the marker and points at the two phones. *"Alice types 'see you at 8' and taps send. Walk it across. Every box it enters, tell me if it's safe there. If it isn't, tell me what happens when the box dies."*

You draw the first arrow. She stops you. *"The phone. Is it safe on the phone?"*

</Scene>

Here is the whole path. Every step has a reason; learn the reasons and you can rebuild it under pressure.

<Figure caption="A 1:1 message, both users online. The first tick appears only after the store has it; the second only after Bob’s phone says so.">
  <Mermaid code={`sequenceDiagram
    participant A as Alice phone
    participant G7 as Gateway 7
    participant CS as Chat service
    participant DB as Message store
    participant R as Registry
    participant G22 as Gateway 22
    participant B as Bob phone
    A->>G7: send(clientMsgId=m-91, conv=c-5, text)
    G7->>CS: forward with Alice's user id
    CS->>DB: insert conv c-5 seq 43 if m-91 is new
    DB-->>CS: ok
    CS-->>G7: ack(m-91, seq 43)
    G7-->>A: ack - one grey tick
    CS->>R: where is Bob
    R-->>CS: phone on gw-22
    CS->>G22: deliver(c-5, seq 43)
    G22->>B: message seq 43
    B-->>G22: ack(c-5, seq 43)
    G22->>CS: Bob's phone delivered up to 43
    CS->>G7: receipt - delivered 43
    G7->>A: two grey ticks`} />
</Figure>

**Step by step, with what makes each one safe:**

1. **Compose.** Alice's phone gives the message a **client message id** (a UUID) and stores it locally as *pending*. If the app dies now, it resends on next launch. *Safe: it's on the phone's disk.*
2. **Send over the socket** to gateway 7, which attaches Alice's authenticated user id (the client never asserts who it is per message) and forwards to the chat service.
3. **Order and persist.** The conversation's owner in the chat service assigns the next **per-conversation sequence number** (43) and writes the message to the store. The write is **idempotent**: `(conversation_id, client_msg_id)` is unique, so a retried send finds the existing row and gets the same seq back. *Safe: replicated storage.*
4. **Ack the sender** with the seq. Alice's phone shows **one grey tick** ("sent") and moves the message from *pending* into its sorted place.
5. **Route.** Look up Bob in the registry (or publish to his channel). For each of his online devices, send to that gateway. If he has none, append to his inbox and send a push notification (Chapter 7).
6. **Push.** Gateway 22 writes the message to Bob's socket.
7. **Device ack.** Bob's phone stores the message, then acks `(conversation, seq)`. This advances his **delivered cursor** and produces the **two grey ticks** receipt back to Alice (Chapter 6).
8. **Read.** When Bob opens the conversation, his phone sends `read up to 43`, which becomes **blue ticks**.

**The one rule that matters most:** **ack after persist, never before.** If the chat service acked first and then crashed, Alice would see a tick for a message that exists nowhere. If it persists and crashes before acking, Alice retries with the same client id and gets the same seq — no loss, no duplicate.

**Where the message is, at each instant:** on Alice's phone (pending) → in flight → in the store (durable, ordered) → in flight to Bob → on Bob's phone. The only boxes it passes through that can lose it — the gateways, the socket buffers, the network — are the ones you never trust.

<Trace title="Walk the message" steps={flow} takeaway="Ack after persist. Make sends idempotent with a client message id. Offline? It is already in the store; the push notification is only a doorbell." />

<Reveal q="Why route through the chat service at all? Gateway 7 could look Bob up and send straight to gateway 22.">

Because then nothing persists or orders the message before it's delivered — Bob could see it, Alice's ack could fail, and the store would never know it existed. The chat service is where the three guarantees are made: durable (persist), ordered (seq), idempotent (client id). Gateways stay dumb so they can be disposable. (Ephemeral signals like "typing…" are the exception: they can skip persistence entirely — see Chapter 8.)

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** drawing the arrow straight from sender to recipient and saying "then it's delivered". Interviewers mark the missing persist step every time. Say *"persist, then ack, then route"*, out loud, in that order.

<h2 id="delivery">Chapter 5 · Once, and in order</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 5" title="Once, and in order">

A support ticket with a screenshot. In a group chat, one person's phone shows *"Are you coming?"* above *"Yes!"*. Another person's phone shows them the other way around. Iris prints it and pins it to the board under the words *THIS CANNOT HAPPEN.*

Theo reads the code. *"We sort by the timestamp on the sender's phone,"* he says. *"One of those phones thinks it's four seconds in the future."*

</Scene>

Two promises, and neither comes free over a network that drops, duplicates and reorders.

**Promise 1: each message appears once.** The honest semantics are **at-least-once delivery with deduplication at both ends** — the same idea as <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/queues-streams/#delivery`}>at-least-once messaging with idempotent consumers</a>:

- **Sender side.** Retries are inevitable (a lost ack looks exactly like a lost send). The client message id makes a retry harmless: the server returns the original seq.
- **Recipient side.** The gateway retries a push whose ack didn't arrive, and catch-up sync may re-send something push already delivered. The device **drops any seq it already has**, and re-acks it so the retries stop.
- The result is **exactly-once effect** on screen. Exactly-once *delivery* over an unreliable network isn't on offer, and saying so is a senior signal.

**Promise 2: everyone sees the same order.** Clocks can't give you this: phones drift, users set their clocks by hand, and two messages in the same millisecond still need an order. So **one authority per conversation hands out sequence numbers** — 41, 42, 43 — at persist time, and every device sorts by seq. Timestamps are for display only.

How to make "one authority per conversation":

| Option | How | Tradeoff |
|---|---|---|
| **Owner per conversation** | Route each conversation to one chat-service instance by consistent hashing on `conversation_id`; it allocates seqs in order and only advances after a successful write | Fast and gap-free; on ownership change the new owner must read the last persisted seq, and writes need a fence (insert-if-absent on `(conversation_id, seq)`) so a zombie owner can't overwrite |
| **Conditional insert** | Any instance inserts `seq = last + 1` only if that row doesn't exist; on conflict, retry with the next | Simple and correct anywhere; costs a conditional write per message (in some wide-column stores that means a consensus round — expensive) |
| **Atomic counter** | `INCR conv:{id}:seq` in Redis, then write | One extra hop; a counter value burned by a failed write leaves a gap clients must tolerate |
| **Partitioned log** | Append to a log partition keyed by `conversation_id`; partition order is conversation order | Durable and ordered, but adds a hop and a consumer before the sender can get a seq back |

Per-conversation throughput is tiny — even a busy group sends a few messages a second — so a single owner is never the bottleneck. **Order only within a conversation.** There is no global order across conversations, and nobody needs one.

**Gap detection.** Because seqs are dense (no gaps, if allocated as above), a device that has applied up to 42 and receives 44 *knows* it missed 43. It asks the server for "everything in this conversation after 42" — the store is the truth — and applies 43 then 44. Push lost something; sync repairs it.

<Scene title="The interview room" mood="night">

The Interviewer leans back. *"Two people in a group send at the same instant. What does everyone see?"* The candidate before you said "it depends on the network". You say: *"The same thing, because order is the sequence number the conversation's owner assigned at persist time. **The sender's own message.** Alice sees her message instantly (optimistic UI) at the bottom, marked pending. When the ack returns with seq 43, her phone moves it into seq order — usually where it already was; occasionally one slot up, if Bob's message got 43 and hers 44. Every device converges.

<Trace title="Order and duplicates" steps={ordering} takeaway="Order comes from a per-conversation seq assigned at persist time. A gap means sync from the store. A repeat means drop and re-ack." />

<Reveal q="“Why not use a Snowflake-style id (timestamp + machine + counter) as the order?”">

It's roughly time-ordered, which is great for storage and paging, but two ids generated on different machines a few milliseconds apart can come out in either order relative to when they were persisted, and there are gaps between consecutive ids, so a device can't tell "I missed one" from "there was none". A dense per-conversation seq gives both an agreed order and gap detection. Many systems use both: a globally unique message id for identity and dedup, and a per-conversation seq for order.

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** "Kafka guarantees ordering." Only within a partition, only if you key by the conversation, and only up to the consumer. And none of it survives into a phone that reconnects through a different gateway. The order users see has to be carried *in the message* (the seq), not inherited from a pipe.

<h2 id="receipts">Chapter 6 · Two grey ticks</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 6" title="Two grey ticks" mood="dusk">

The worst bug from the incident: messages showing two ticks that Bob never received. Dev found it in an hour. The gateway sent the "delivered" receipt the moment it wrote to Bob's socket — and Bob's socket had been dead for three minutes.

*"A tick is a promise to a human,"* Mara says. *"Who is allowed to make each one?"*

</Scene>

Each tick is a claim made by exactly one party:

| Tick | Means | Who may claim it | Trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| ✓ **Sent** | The server has it durably and will deliver it | The chat service | Ack after persist |
| ✓✓ **Delivered** | A recipient device has stored it | The **recipient's device** | Device ack of `(conversation, seq)` |
| ✓✓ blue **Read** | The recipient has seen it | The recipient's device (if read receipts are on) | Conversation opened and scrolled to it |

**Receipts are cursors, not per-message flags.** "Bob's phone has delivered up to 43 in c-5" covers 41, 42 and 43 in one small event. Cursors are **monotonic** (only move forward — take the max), **idempotent** (applying 43 twice changes nothing), and **coalescable** (a phone that comes back with 200 new messages sends one "delivered up to N", not 200 receipts). Store them per `(conversation, user)` — and per device for "delivered" — in a small table.

**Receipts travel like messages, in reverse.** Bob's ack goes through his gateway to the chat service, which updates the cursor and routes a receipt event to Alice's devices (or, if she's offline, she picks up the new cursor on her next sync). They need no seq of their own: the cursor value is the state.

**Groups.** In a group of 200, "delivered" and "read" are per member. The per-message info screen ("read by 37 of 199") is computed from member cursors: a member has read message 43 if their read cursor ≥ 43. "Delivered to everyone" is `min(delivered cursors) ≥ 43`. Don't push every member's receipt to every member — 200 members each reading would mean 200 × 199 events. Send group receipts to the **sender** of the message only, coalesced, and compute the rest on demand.

**Catch the trap:** the gateway claiming "delivered" when it writes to the socket. A dead socket accepts writes. Only the device can say it has the message.

<h2 id="offline">Chapter 7 · The phone in the drawer</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 7" title="The phone in the drawer" mood="night">

Iris goes camping for a week and turns her phone off. When she comes back, 1,400 messages across 60 conversations are waiting. She opens the app in a taxi with one bar of signal. *"It should just… be there,"* she says. *"Like it was always there."*

Mara asks: *"How does her phone know which 60 conversations to ask about — without asking about all 500 she's in?"*

</Scene>

**The cursor is the contract.** Every device remembers, for every conversation, the highest seq it has applied. "Catch up" means: for each conversation with news, fetch messages after my cursor. The store answers from the truth, so it doesn't matter what push did or didn't deliver.

**The inbox tells the device where the news is.** Asking about all 500 conversations on every reconnect is wasteful. So the server keeps a small **per-device inbox**: a set of `conversation_id → highest seq with news`, upserted when a message is fanned out to that device and deleted when the device acks up to that seq (conditionally — only if no newer message arrived meanwhile). Its size is bounded by **conversations with news**, not by messages. Reconnect becomes:

```text
1. GET /sync                                  → [{ conv: c-5, upTo: 1043 }, { conv: c-9, upTo: 77 }, …]
2. for each: GET /conversations/{c}/messages?afterSeq={myCursor}&limit=100   (paginated)
3. ack (conv, seq) as each page is stored     → server trims the inbox, emits "delivered" receipts
```

<Figure caption="One conversation, two devices, two cursors. The laptop is current; the phone was in a drawer. Each catches up from its own cursor; the store doesn’t care which.">
  <ArrayStrip name="conv c-5" values={[40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46]} indices={false} pointers={[{ at: 2, label: 'phone cursor', tone: 'blue' }, { at: 6, label: 'laptop cursor', tone: 'green' }]} tones={{ 0: 'done', 1: 'done', 2: 'done', 3: 'hot', 4: 'hot', 5: 'hot', 6: 'done' }} regions={[{ from: 3, to: 6, label: 'phone fetches after 42', tone: 'gold' }]} />

**Push notifications are a doorbell, not a delivery van.** When Bob has no live connection, the chat service sends a notification through APNs (Apple) or FCM (Google). Treat it as a **wake-up**:

- Delivery is **best-effort**: the platform can delay, coalesce or drop notifications, and you don't get a reliable per-message receipt from the app.
- Payloads are small (on the order of 4 KB — check current docs), and the OS may throttle apps that send too many.
- So the notification says "you have something", and possibly a preview; the app then connects and **syncs from its cursor**. The message is already in the store either way. If every notification were lost, Bob would still get every message the next time he opened the app.

**Retention is a product decision; say which one you chose.**

- **Store-and-forward**: the server keeps a message until every recipient device has acked it, then deletes it (or keeps it only for a bounded window). Small storage, strong privacy story; history lives on devices, and a new device needs history transferred from an old one or from an encrypted backup.
- **Server-kept history**: the server keeps every message (Slack-style), so any device can scroll back forever and search works. Storage grows without bound (Chapter 12 has the numbers).

<Reveal q="Bob was offline for six months and the store-and-forward window is 30 days. What happens?">

Messages older than the window are gone from the server, so his catch-up returns only what's left — and the honest design says so out loud: undelivered messages expire after N days, the sender sees them stay at one tick, and the product decides whether to tell the sender. With server-kept history there is no window, but you still paginate the catch-up (latest page first, older pages on scroll) so a long-absent device doesn't pull a year of messages before showing anything.

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** "Offline users get the message by push notification." That makes a best-effort wake-up service your delivery guarantee. Say *"it's in the store; the device syncs from its cursor; the notification only wakes it."*

<h2 id="presence">Chapter 8 · The green dot</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 8" title="The green dot" mood="dawn">

Iris wants a green dot next to everyone who's online, and "last seen 5 minutes ago" for everyone who isn't. Dev puts a number on the board before anyone draws a box: the presence prototype, in staging, generated more traffic than chat itself.

*"Who actually looks at a green dot?"* Mara asks.

</Scene>

**Presence is soft state.** "Online" means *a live connection exists on a live gateway*, which the system already knows: the registry plus the gateway leases (Chapter 2). "Last seen" is one timestamp written when the last device disconnects. Presence needs no extra per-heartbeat writes — the heartbeat's job is to make disconnects happen promptly (two missed beats), and the disconnect is the event.

**The problem is never computing presence. It's telling people.**

**Worked numbers.** Kestrel: 50 million daily users, each going online/offline about **20 times a day**, about **100 contacts** each.

| Who gets told | Events per day | Per second (÷10⁵) |
|---|---|---|
| Every contact, every change | `50M × 20 × 100 = 100 billion` | ≈ 1,000,000 |
| Chat messages, for comparison | `50M × 40 = 2 billion` | ≈ 20,000 |
| Only people with a screen open that shows this user (assume ~2) | `50M × 20 × 2 = 2 billion` | ≈ 20,000 |

Naive presence costs **fifty times** the traffic of the messages it decorates. Three moves cut it down:

1. **Lazy presence.** Fetch the current state when a screen opens (the chat header, the contact list) — one batched read.
2. **Subscribe only while visible.** The open screen subscribes to those users' presence; closing it unsubscribes. Changes are pushed only to subscribers. Most contacts are not looking at you, so most changes go nowhere.
3. **Debounce.** Mark a user offline only after a grace period (say 30–60 s), so a phone flapping in a tunnel doesn't blink five times — every suppressed blink is a fan-out you don't pay for.

**Presence is at-most-once data.** Lose a transition and the next one fixes it. Don't persist it durably, don't retry it, don't put it in the message store. **Typing indicators** are the same: ephemeral, never stored, sent at most every few seconds while typing and expired by the receiver after ~5 s of silence, so a lost "stopped typing" can't leave the dots on forever.

<Trace title="The cost of a green dot" steps={presenceDrill} takeaway="Presence is cheap to know and expensive to tell. Lazy fetch, subscribe only while visible, debounce flaps — and never treat it as durable." />

**Catch the trap:** "Presence: a Redis key per user with a TTL, refreshed on every heartbeat." That works at small scale, and you should say why it stops working: 20 million connections ÷ 30 s ≈ 667,000 writes a second of keepalive. Derive "online" from connections and gateway leases, and spend your effort on who gets told.

<h2 id="groups">Chapter 9 · The group of two hundred</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 9" title="The group of two hundred">

Iris, on Monday: *"Group chats. Up to 256 people."* Iris, on Thursday: *"Oh, and also — community channels. Some will have a hundred thousand members."*

Mara draws one message, then two hundred arrows out of it. Then she tries to draw a hundred thousand and gives up. *"Same feature,"* she says. *"Different architecture. Where's the line?"*

</Scene>

The question is the same one as in <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-fanout/`}>the fan-out pattern</a>: **do the work when the message is written, or when it is read?**

**Small groups: fan-out on write.** Store the message **once** in the conversation's log. Then, for each member device, upsert the inbox entry (Chapter 7) and push to online devices. A group of 200 means 200 small pointer writes plus pushes per message. Reconnect sync stays one cheap inbox read per device. This is the right default up to a few hundred members.

**Large channels: fan-out on read.** At 100,000 members and 10 messages a second, fan-out on write is `100,000 × 10 = 1,000,000` inbox writes a second — for one channel. Instead:

- Store once in the channel log. **No inbox writes.**
- Members keep **per-channel cursors**; on reconnect, the client asks for the latest seq of each joined channel (one batched read) and fetches what it needs — usually only the channels on screen.
- **Push only to who's watching, at gateway granularity.** Each gateway subscribes to the channel's topic if any of its connected users are in the channel *and active*. A message is published once; each subscribed gateway gets **one** copy and pushes it to its local members. With 40 gateways, that's at most 40 internal sends per message, not 100,000.

<Figure caption="Large-channel delivery. The chat service publishes once; only gateways with subscribed, online members receive it; each gateway does the last-hop fan-out locally.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    CS["Chat service\npersist once, seq 9120"] --> T(["Topic: channel 77"])
    T --> G1["Gateway 3\n1,200 members online"]
    T --> G2["Gateway 18\n950 members online"]
    T --> G3["Gateway 31\n40 members online"]
    G1 --> P1["local push × 1,200"]
    G2 --> P2["local push × 950"]

| | Fan-out on write (inboxes) | Fan-out on read (channel log + cursors) |
|---|---|---|
| Write cost per message | One pointer per member device | One append |
| Reconnect cost | One inbox read | Latest-seq check per joined channel |
| Unread badges | Free (inbox entry exists) | Computed: latest seq − my read cursor |
| Fits | 1:1 and groups up to a few hundred | Channels, communities, broadcast lists |

**Slack and Discord in one paragraph.** They are the same pattern with channels as the default. Users belong to many channels; most are muted or off-screen. So: gateway-level channel subscriptions, **selective delivery** (push full messages only for channels the client is viewing or has notifications on; send cheap "channel 77 has news" hints for the rest), per-channel cursors for unread state, and connection sharding by user across the gateway fleet.

<Trace title="Where the work goes" steps={groups} takeaway="Small groups: write pointers to inboxes. Large channels: store once, cursors per member, push once per gateway." />

<Reveal q="A message is sent to a 256-member group. Do you encrypt it 256 times?">

With end-to-end encryption, the naive way is yes — once per member device, pairwise. A common optimisation (used by Signal-protocol messengers for groups) is **sender keys**: each sender shares a symmetric sender key with every member once, pairwise-encrypted; after that each message is encrypted once and the server fans out the same ciphertext. Membership changes force new keys. Look up the details rather than improvising them in an interview — the high-level idea is enough.

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** one fan-out strategy for every group size. Name the threshold, say why (inbox writes per message), and say what changes above it.

<h2 id="multi-device">Chapter 10 · Phone, laptop, tablet</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 10" title="Phone, laptop, tablet" mood="dusk">

Theo sends a message from his laptop, then picks up his phone. The conversation on the phone doesn't show what he just said. *"I'm talking to myself,"* he says, *"and only half of me is listening."*

</Scene>

**Every device is its own reader.** The registry holds one entry per device; the inbox and cursors are per device. Delivering a message means:

- to **every device of every recipient**, and
- to **every other device of the sender** — your own sent messages must sync too. Treat the sender's user as a recipient with the originating device excluded from push (it already has the message).

**Which cursors are per device, which per user?**

| State | Scope | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Delivered cursor | Per device | Each device must catch up independently |
| Inbox entries | Per device | Each device has its own backlog |
| Read cursor | **Per user** | Reading on the laptop should clear the badge on the phone; the read event syncs to the user's other devices |
| "Delivered" tick shown to the sender | Product decision | Usually: when the **first** recipient device acks |

**Adding a new device.** It has no history. With server-kept history it simply pages backward from the store. With store-and-forward or E2EE, history comes from another device (a device-to-device transfer) or an encrypted backup the user controls — the server can't help, because it either deleted the messages or can't read them.

**Catch the trap:** a registry keyed `user → gateway`. The moment a user has two devices, one overwrites the other and half their messages stop arriving in real time. Key it `user → device → gateway` from the start.

<h2 id="e2ee">Chapter 11 · The server that can’t read</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 11" title="The server that can’t read" mood="night">

Legal asks for end-to-end encryption. Iris asks whether search still works. Theo asks whether the database schema changes. Dev asks whether he can still debug anything.

Mara draws a padlock on Alice's phone and another on Bob's, and leaves every box in the middle exactly where it is. *"Most of the system,"* she says, *"doesn't notice. Find the parts that do."*

</Scene>

**End-to-end encryption (E2EE)** means only the endpoints can read message content. At the level an interview needs:

- **Keys live on devices.** Each device generates its own key pairs; private keys never leave it. The server runs a **public-key directory**: devices upload public keys, senders fetch recipients' public keys.
- **The sender encrypts for each recipient device** (or uses sender keys for groups — Chapter 9). The server stores and routes **ciphertext**; the envelope it can read holds only routing data — conversation, recipients, seq, timestamps.
- The protocol most messengers build on is the **Signal Protocol** (an initial key agreement, then a "double ratchet" that changes keys with every message so that one leaked key exposes very little). Know that it exists and what it gives you; don't improvise its internals in an interview. Signal publishes its specifications.
- Users can verify they're talking to the right keys by comparing a fingerprint ("safety number"), which defends against a server that hands out the wrong public key.

**What changes in the design — this is the part to say:**

| Feature | Without E2EE | With E2EE |
|---|---|---|
| Routing, ordering, receipts, presence | Server-side | **Unchanged** — they use metadata, not content |
| Search | Server-side index | On-device only |
| Notification previews | Server writes the text | Generic text, or the app decrypts on device before display |
| Link previews, spam/abuse scanning | Server-side | On device, or via user reports |
| Multi-device | Send once | Encrypt per device; adding a device means new key setup |
| History on a new device | Read from server | Device-to-device transfer or encrypted backup |
| What the server still sees | Everything | **Metadata**: who talks to whom, when, how often, message sizes |

**Catch the trap:** "With E2EE the server knows nothing." It knows the social graph and the timing. Say so — interviewers who ask about E2EE are usually testing whether you know the difference between content and metadata.

<h2 id="storage">Chapter 12 · Billions a day</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 12" title="Billions a day">

Theo refuses to look at the architecture diagram. *"Tell me the three queries,"* he says. *"Then tell me the partition key. Then I'll look."*

You write: *the latest 50 messages of a conversation; everything in a conversation after seq N; one message by id.* He nods. *"Then there is exactly one right answer for the key."*

</Scene>

**The access pattern decides the store.** Messages are append-only, almost never updated, read by conversation in seq order, and written at hundreds of thousands per second at scale. No joins. That is the textbook fit for a **wide-column / key-value store** (Cassandra, ScyllaDB, HBase, DynamoDB-style): writes spread across nodes, and each conversation's messages sit together, sorted.

```text
messages
  partition key:  (conversation_id, bucket)      -- bucket = seq / 10,000 for very busy conversations
  clustering key: seq DESC
  columns:        message_id, client_msg_id, sender_id, sent_at, body (or ciphertext), media_ref

inbox                       -- per device: where the news is (Chapter 7)
  partition key:  device_id
  clustering key: conversation_id   → up_to_seq

cursors                     -- receipts (Chapter 6)
  primary key:    (conversation_id, user_id)   → delivered_seq, read_seq   (+ per-device delivered)

conversations / members     -- Conversation ↔ User is many-to-many: a membership table, both directions indexed
```

- **"Latest 50"** and **"after seq N"** are single-partition range reads, already sorted.
- **Buckets** stop one huge, ancient channel from growing a single unbounded partition; reading across a bucket boundary is a second query. A 1:1 conversation will usually live in one bucket forever.
- **Dedup:** `client_msg_id` must be unique per conversation. In a store without secondary unique constraints, keep a small `(conversation_id, client_msg_id) → seq` lookup with a TTL of a few days — retries happen within minutes, not months.
- **Media** never goes in this table: upload the photo to object storage, put a reference in the message (<a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/blob-cdn/`}>Blob storage & CDN</a>).
- A **sharded relational database** keyed by `conversation_id` also works, and some large chat products run on one. Say the access pattern first; the product follows.

| Store | Fits because | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| Wide-column (Cassandra/ScyllaDB/HBase) | Partition by conversation, sorted by seq, horizontal writes | Large partitions; conditional writes are expensive; tombstones from deletes |
| Sharded relational | Unique constraints, transactions per shard, familiar | You run the sharding (or a layer like Vitess); cross-shard queries |
| Redis alone | Fast | Not the truth. Memory-priced; use it for registry, presence, recent hot pages |

**Worked numbers (Kestrel today).** 50M daily users × 40 messages = **2 billion messages a day**. At ~200 bytes stored per text message (body plus ids, seq, timestamps, overhead): `2 × 10⁹ × 200 B = 400 GB` a day, × 3 replicas = **1.2 TB/day** of raw disk — about **440 TB a year** if history is kept forever. With store-and-forward, the server holds only undelivered messages: a few days of backlog for offline devices, a small fraction of that.

<Trace title="Where messages live" steps={storage} takeaway="Partition by conversation, cluster by seq, bucket the giants. Then multiply by replicas and decide retention on purpose." />

**Catch the trap:** "Store messages in Postgres" or "store them in Cassandra" with no key. Theo's three questions — the reads, the key, the size — are the answer. The product name is the least interesting word in the sentence.

<h2 id="scale">Chapter 13 · The numbers</h2>

Iris has a board deck with a slide that says *500 million daily users*. *"Ten times bigger,"* she says. *"What breaks?"*

Mara writes the assumptions down the left side of the whiteboard before any arithmetic. *"Numbers without assumptions,"* she says, *"are just confident noise."*

**Assumptions:** 500M daily active users · 40 messages sent per user per day · peak = 3× average · 40% of daily users connected at the evening peak · ~200 bytes stored per message · 3 replicas · ~10 KB memory per connection · 500,000 connections per gateway · heartbeat every 30 s · each message delivered to ~3 devices on average (multi-device plus groups).

| Quantity | Arithmetic | Result | So what |
|---|---|---|---|

**What breaks first at 10×:** not memory, not bandwidth. The **reconnect wave** (a gateway now holds the same 500K, but there are ten times as many gateways to deploy and lose), the **presence fan-out** if it was ever naive, the **storage bill** if history is kept forever, and **hot channels** if large rooms were ever fanned out on write.

**Catch the trap:** "We'll need a lot of servers." How many, sized by what? *"Four hundred gateways at half a million connections each — sized by blast radius, not memory"* is a sentence an interviewer remembers.

<h2 id="failures">Chapter 14 · What breaks, and what users see</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 14" title="What breaks, and what users see" mood="storm">

Dev's version of the post-mortem has one column the others don't: *What did the user see?* *"Engineers write 'gateway restarted',"* he says. *"Users write 'my message vanished'. Write both."*

</Scene>

| Failure | What users see | Why nothing is lost | What you add |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gateway crash** | A few seconds of "connecting…" | Messages are in the store; device cursors drive catch-up | Jittered backoff, admission control, conditional registry cleanup |
| **Half-open connection** (phone on a train) | Messages arrive late until the gateway notices | Undelivered seqs stay above the device cursor | Heartbeats, two misses → close; push notification after a short ack timeout |
| **Partitioned user** (no network for hours) | One tick on the sender's side | Inbox + cursor; sync on return | Push as wake-up; retention window stated |
| **Duplicate delivery** (push retried, then sync) | Nothing, if the client dedupes | Device drops seqs it has | Dedup by seq / message id; re-ack |
| **Sender retry after lost ack** | Nothing | `(conversation, client_msg_id)` unique → same seq | Idempotent send |
| **Chat service crash after persist, before ack** | Message briefly "pending", then ticks | Client retries; dedup returns the original seq | Ack after persist, always |
| **Registry stale or down** | Slower delivery for some users | Persist path doesn't need it | Gateway leases; fall back to sync/push |
| **Conversation owner fails over** | A brief pause in one conversation | New owner reads the last persisted seq; insert-if-absent fences the old one | Ownership epochs |
| **Message store partition unavailable** | Sends fail for the conversations on it | Nothing was acked, so nothing was promised | Replication (e.g. quorum writes), client keeps pending messages and retries |

<Trace title="Name the failure" steps={failures} takeaway="Every failure in the connection tier costs latency, never messages — as long as the store is the truth and devices sync from cursors." />

<Reveal q="A whole region fails. What happens to conversations whose members are on two continents?">

Say what you'd promise. A common answer: each conversation has a **home region** that orders and persists it (one seq authority); users connect to the nearest gateway region, and gateways forward to the conversation's home. If a home region fails, its conversations fail over to a replica region — accepting either a short pause (if replication is synchronous) or the risk of losing the last moments of acked messages (if it is asynchronous). Multi-region ordering is genuinely hard; naming the home-region idea and the tradeoff is enough unless the interviewer pushes.

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** describing failures in engineer words only. For each one, say what the user sees, and why the data is safe anyway.

**0:25 · Deep dive 1 — the connection tier**

> **You:** "Two hundred million connections. At about 10 KB each that's 2 TB of memory fleet-wide — memory isn't the limit; blast radius is. I'll cap a gateway at 500 thousand connections, so 400 gateways, around 600 with zone headroom. Heartbeats every 30 seconds — 16,700 a second per gateway — and they stop at the gateway; two missed beats close the socket. The registry entry is written on connect and deleted on disconnect only if it still names this gateway and connection, so a slow cleanup can't erase a fresh reconnect. Liveness comes from one lease per gateway, not a TTL refresh per heartbeat, which would be 6.7 million Redis writes a second."
>
> "When a gateway dies, its 500 thousand clients reconnect with exponential backoff and full jitter over about 30 seconds — 16,700 a second instead of half a million at once — and the gateways cap new connections per second as a backstop. Deploys drain: stop new connections, then ask clients to reconnect at random times over five minutes. Nothing a gateway does can lose a message, because nothing is acked before it's stored."

**0:31 · Deep dive 2 — once, in order, and offline**

> **You:** "Ordering: I won't trust device clocks. Each conversation is owned by one chat-service instance through consistent hashing on conversation id; it hands out a dense sequence number and only advances after a successful write, and the insert is if-absent on conversation and seq so a stale owner after failover can't overwrite. Per-conversation traffic is tiny, so one owner is never a bottleneck. Delivery is at-least-once with dedup at both ends: the client message id makes sender retries return the original seq, and devices drop seqs they've already applied. Because seqs are dense, a device that sees 44 after 42 knows to fetch from the store. And push notifications are wake-ups — if every one were lost, the device would still get everything on next open."

**0:35 · Deep dive 3 — storage and big groups**

> **You:** "Storage: wide-column, partitioned by conversation id plus a bucket, clustered by seq descending — the latest page and 'after seq N' are single-partition reads. At 4 TB a day logical, 12 raw, retention decides the bill. I'll do store-and-forward: keep messages until every recipient device acks, with a 30-day cap, and devices keep history — which also fits end-to-end encryption. Groups are capped at 256, so fan-out on write is 256 pointer writes; if we added large channels I'd switch them to fan-out on read — one log, per-member cursors, gateways subscribed to the channel topic so each message is published once per gateway, not once per member."

**0:38 · Wrap-up**

> **You:** "To recap: send, offline delivery and receipts are all served; durability from ack-after-persist; order from per-conversation seqs; no visible duplicates from idempotent sends and client dedup. At 10×, the first pressure is operational — reconnect waves and deploys across thousands of gateways — then presence fan-out, which I'd keep lazy and subscription-based, only for screens that show it. If a store partition fails, sends to those conversations fail and stay pending on the phone; nothing acked is lost. I'd monitor p99 send-to-deliver latency, ack timeouts, reconnect rate per gateway, inbox backlog, and push-notification failure rates. On end-to-end encryption: keys stay on devices, the server routes ciphertext and keeps only metadata, and the design above doesn't change except search and previews move on device."

The four knobs that turn the chat design into every other realtime design:

1. **Transport** — poll, SSE or WebSocket (Chapter 1).
2. **Durability** — must every event survive (messages, notifications), or is the latest value all that matters (cursors, presence, locations)?
3. **Fan-out shape** — one reader, a few, or a huge audience (Chapter 9)?
4. **Order and dedup** — per conversation, per document, per object, or not at all?

| Bank question | How the pattern applies |
|---------------|------------------------|
| **WhatsApp / Messenger** | WS + registry + seq ordering + receipts + offline catch-up — the full showcase above |
| **Discord / Slack** | Same, channel-first: gateway-level topic subscriptions, selective delivery (full messages only for viewed channels), per-channel cursors, fan-out on read for big servers, connection sharding by user |
| **Live comments / sports scores** | SSE, one-way — don't pay for WebSockets. Huge audiences: comments are written once and broadcast through gateway (or edge) topic subscriptions; at 10,000 comments a second nobody can read them all, so sample or rank what each viewer gets |
| **Collaborative cursors (Google Docs, Figma-style)** | WS room per document. Cursors are latest-value, at-most-once, throttled to ~10–20 updates a second, never persisted. The *document edits* are durable and ordered — that's OT/CRDT, in <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-sync-collab/`}>Sync & collaboration</a> |
| **Notification system** | In-app: SSE or the existing socket when the app is open; a notification inbox with a cursor (the same catch-up pattern); APNs/FCM when it isn't. Multi-channel fan-out, retries and priorities are <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-fanout/`}>the fan-out pattern</a> |
| **Uber (location stream)** | Driver → server upstream every few seconds (about 4 s is a common figure) over a WS; rider watches the driver's position via WS. Latest value only — drop stale updates, no history needed for display. Meets the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-geospatial/`}>geospatial pattern</a> |
| **Order/delivery status page** | Polling every 10 s. Saying "polling is enough here" is a flex, not a cop-out |
| **Zoom (media)** | Signaling over WS; media itself is UDP/WebRTC via an SFU — one sentence, don't go deeper unprompted (but know the section below) |

**Zoom and video calls, in one more paragraph — only if asked.** The WebSocket carries **signaling**: who's in the call, offers and answers describing media, network candidates. The media goes over **WebRTC** (encrypted RTP, usually over UDP, because a late video frame is worthless — better to drop it than retransmit it). Topologies:

| Topology | How | Client upload | Server cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mesh** | Everyone sends to everyone | n − 1 streams — collapses past ~4 people | None |
| **MCU** (multipoint control unit) | Server decodes all streams, mixes one composite per participant, re-encodes | 1 stream | Very high CPU (decode + mix + encode) |
| **SFU** (selective forwarding unit) | Server forwards streams without decoding; each client receives the streams it needs | 1 stream (or a few simulcast layers) | Low CPU; bandwidth scales with forwarding |

**Simulcast**: each sender uploads the same video at two or three resolutions; the SFU forwards the layer each receiver can handle (a thumbnail gets the small one; a weak connection gets the small one). **STUN** tells a client its public address behind NAT so peers can try to connect directly; **TURN** relays the media when no direct path works (strict firewalls, some NATs) — reliable but it costs the operator bandwidth. **ICE** is the procedure that tries the candidates and picks a working path. Ten people on an SFU: each uploads one stream (or three simulcast layers) and downloads up to nine.

**Catch the trap:** treating every realtime feature as chat. Cursors, locations and presence are latest-value and lossy; persisting and acking them is wasted work. Messages and notifications are durable; dropping them is a bug. Say which kind each event is.

<h2 id="interview-script">Say it in the room</h2>

<Scene title="The interview room" mood="night">

The Interviewer reads from a card: *"Design a chat app."* Two months ago you'd have drawn a phone, an arrow labelled WEBSOCKET, and a database. Today you draw two phones and put the marker on the line between them.

</Scene>

**The 90-second answer** (adapt the nouns):

> "Messages flow both ways constantly — messages, acks, typing — so each device holds a **WebSocket** to a fleet of **gateways** that only hold connections; the logic lives in a stateless **chat service** behind them. A send carries a client message id; the chat service gives it the next **per-conversation sequence number**, **persists it, and only then acks** — that's the first tick. It finds the recipient's devices in a **connection registry** — Redis, user to device to gateway — and pushes through that gateway; the device's ack becomes the delivered tick, and opening the chat sends a read cursor. Delivery is **at-least-once with dedup at both ends**: retried sends return the same seq, devices drop seqs they have, and a gap in seqs means fetch from the store. **Offline devices** sync from their own cursor using a small per-device inbox; APNs or FCM is only a **wake-up**. **Push is the fast path; the store is the truth.** Gateways heartbeat every 30 seconds and clients reconnect with **jittered exponential backoff**, so a dead gateway costs seconds, never messages. Messages live in a wide-column store partitioned by conversation and clustered by seq. Groups fan out on write to member inboxes up to a few hundred; big channels fan out on read with gateway-level subscriptions. Presence is lazy and subscription-based, because naive presence costs fifty times the message traffic."

**Five follow-ups you will get:**

<Reveal q="1 · How do you guarantee ordering?">

Not with timestamps. One authority per conversation — the owning chat-service instance, chosen by consistent hashing on conversation id — assigns a dense sequence number at persist time, and every device sorts by it. The insert is if-absent on (conversation, seq), so after a failover a stale owner can't overwrite. Order is only promised within a conversation; nobody needs a global one.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="2 · Exactly-once delivery?">

Not over an unreliable network — but exactly-once *effect*, yes. At-least-once on the wire; the client message id makes sender retries idempotent (same seq returned); devices drop any seq they already have and re-ack it. Users see each message once.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="3 · A gateway with 500K connections dies. Walk me through the next minute.">

Clients detect the closed socket (or miss two heartbeats) and reconnect with exponential backoff and full jitter, spreading 500K reconnects over ~30 s — about 16,700 a second across 39 other gateways, which also cap admissions per second. Each reconnect writes its registry entry and syncs from its cursors, so anything that was in flight is fetched from the store. Gateway 7's stale registry entries are ignored because its lease expired, and its conditional cleanup can't delete the new entries. Users see a few seconds of "connecting…" and lose nothing.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="4 · How does presence scale?">

Knowing presence is cheap — it's the registry and gateway leases. Telling people is the cost: 50M users × 20 changes × 100 contacts is 100 billion events a day, fifty times the messages. So presence is fetched lazily when a screen opens, pushed only to subscribers whose screens show that user, debounced with a grace window, and never persisted or retried.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="5 · What changes for a 100,000-member channel?">

Fan-out on write would be 100,000 inbox writes per message. Switch to fan-out on read: store once in the channel log, members keep per-channel cursors, and gateways subscribe to the channel's topic so each message is published once per gateway with online, active members — at most a few hundred internal sends, with the last hop done locally. Unread counts come from latest seq minus the member's read cursor.

</Reveal>

## Five mistakes to leave behind

1. **"Then it's delivered."** Say persist → ack → route → device ack → receipt, in that order.
2. **Ordering by timestamp.** Clocks lie. Per-conversation sequence numbers from one owner.
3. **Push notifications as transport.** They're a doorbell. The store and the device's cursor are the delivery.
4. **"Clients just reconnect."** Without jitter, that's a self-inflicted stampede. Backoff, jitter, cap, admission control.
5. **Naive presence and naive big-group fan-out.** Both multiply by audience size. Lazy and subscribed for presence; fan-out on read for big rooms.

**Look these up yourself**

- **Sender keys** for encrypted group messaging — how membership changes force re-keying, and what that costs in a 256-member group.
- **MQTT** — a publish/subscribe protocol built for flaky mobile and IoT links, with built-in QoS levels; how its "at least once" maps onto this lesson's acks.
- **Connection draining and graceful shutdown** in your load balancer of choice — how long it will wait for long-lived connections, and what happens at the timeout.
- **Hybrid logical clocks** — when you do need cross-conversation ordering (e.g. a unified activity log), what they give you that wall clocks don't.
- **WebTransport** — a newer browser API over HTTP/3 with streams and unreliable datagrams; where it might replace WebSockets.
- **Large partitions and tombstones** in Cassandra-style stores — why deleting messages (store-and-forward) can hurt read performance, and how TTLs and compaction strategies help.
- **Multi-region chat** — home regions per conversation, and what you trade when the home region fails over asynchronously.
