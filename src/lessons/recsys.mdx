<h2 id="prologue">Prologue · A feed is not a sorted list</h2>

Say a short-video app launches a Discover tab. The first version sorts videos by likes in the last seven days. It takes four days to build.

Three weeks later the dashboard shows two things. Average session length is down 31%. And the top fifty videos have not changed in nine days. Every user, in every country, sees the same fifty. Those videos get likes because they are on top, and they stay on top because they get likes.

The team says "we need a real recommender" and draws a box labelled ML MODEL. A senior engineer would cross it out and ask five questions. Where do the candidates come from? How are they scored, and against what? What shapes the page? What does the model see, and is it what it trained on? How does the system learn without only learning from itself?

"Design TikTok's feed", "Design YouTube recommendations" and "Design Netflix's home page" are **not ML modelling interviews**. They are systems questions: serve a personalised ranking over a hundred million items in about 200 ms. The models are boxes with latency, freshness and data properties. Get the funnel right and you pass. Derive loss functions and you have answered the wrong question.

Two slips are common in this topic. People forget **cold start** and **feedback loops**, because nobody asks about them. And they say "a feature store" without saying that it is a cache with rules about keys, TTL, invalidation and failure. This lesson turns each into a sentence you can say without thinking.

**A recommender in your design is these five answers, said out loud:**

1. **Retrieval.** Which sources find a few thousand candidates out of the corpus, cheaply.
2. **Ranking.** What the model predicts for each candidate, and how the predictions combine into one score.
3. **Re-ranking.** Which rules turn a sorted list into a good page (diversity, freshness, policy).
4. **Features.** What the model sees, where each feature is computed, and how training sees the same values.
5. **The loop.** Which events become labels, how new users and items get a chance, how you avoid learning only from your own choices, and how you know a change helped.

All of it sits inside a **latency budget** you state with numbers.

<h2 id="funnel">Chapter 1 · Why a funnel: a hundred million items</h2>

A tempting first idea: "We already have a good engagement model. Run it on every video for every user and show the top ten." Do the multiplication before accepting it.

Assumptions for this example, stated out loud as you would in the room:

- **50M daily users**, **12 feed requests** each per day, **10 videos** per request.
- 50M × 12 = **600M requests/day**. A day is about 10⁵ seconds (86,400, rounded, the usual envelope shortcut; the true average is about 16% higher), so about **6,000 requests/s** on average and **18,000/s** at a 3× peak.
- 600M × 10 = **6B impressions/day**. (An impression is one video actually shown to a user.)
- **Corpus: 100M videos**, 2M new uploads a day.

**Score everything?** 18,000 requests/s × 100M videos = **1.8 × 10¹² scores per second**. Even at 10 µs per item, one request takes 100M × 10 µs = **1,000 seconds**. At 1 ms per item it is about 28 hours. Per request.

So every real system **funnels**: each stage is more expensive per item and sees fewer items.

| Stage | Items in → out | Cost per item | Optimises for | Budget |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Retrieval** (candidate generation) | 100M → ~2,000 | Sub-linear: an ANN search, KV lookups, precomputed lists | **Recall**: don't miss anything plausibly good | ~25 ms |
| **Light ranker** (pre-ranking) | ~2,000 → 400 | Small model, few dozen features | Keep the likely winners | ~15 ms |
| **Heavy ranker** | 400 scored | Big multi-task model, hundreds of features | **Precision**: exact order at the top | ~60 ms |
| **Re-rank / blend** | 400 → page of 10 | Rules, light models | Page quality, policy, exploration | ~10 ms |

*Recall* is the share of the good items you managed to include. *Precision* is how well the top of the list is ordered or how many of the shown items are good. Retrieval can afford to be sloppy about order but not about coverage, because the ranker can never rank an item that retrieval missed.

**The load the funnel leaves.** If one GPU server scores about 50,000 items/s with this model (an assumption: benchmark yours), the heavy ranker needs 7.2M ÷ 50,000 = **144 servers**. Large, but buildable. The whole corpus at 1.8 trillion a second is not.

The re-ranking stage after both is where product rules live. Mentioning it separates people who have shipped feeds from people who have read papers.

You have seen this shape before. Search retrieves with an inverted index and re-ranks the top hits (<a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/search/#relevance`}>Search · relevance</a>). RAG retrieves with ANN and re-ranks with a cross-encoder (<a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/rag/`}>RAG</a>). A Bloom filter says "definitely not" before the disk is touched. **Filter cheap, then expensive** is one of the most reusable moves in system design.

**Interview takeaway.** "We can't score 100M items per request, so it's a funnel: cheap retrieval for recall, a small ranker, a heavy ranker for precision, then re-ranking for page rules, with counts and milliseconds at each stage."

**Common slip:** "We train a model that recommends videos." A model is not a design. Say the stages, the counts, which stage optimises recall and which precision, and the milliseconds each gets.

<h2 id="candidates">Chapter 2 · Candidate sources: many doors, one room</h2>

One user watched eleven bread-baking videos in a row, then a cat video, then four more bread videos. The "top by likes" feed showed her no bread at all. There is no single right way to find candidates for her. Retrieval is usually **several independent sources**, each good at a different kind of "you might like this", each returning a few hundred to a thousand ids. Their union goes to the ranker.

**Collaborative filtering: "people like you liked this".** Picture a giant matrix: users down the side, videos across the top, a mark where someone engaged. It is almost entirely empty. Collaborative filtering fills in the blanks from patterns: users whose marks overlap with yours probably like what they liked. **Matrix factorisation** learns a short vector for every user and every item so that their dot product approximates the mark. That idea, affinity as a dot product of learned vectors, is the seed of two-tower retrieval in the next chapter.

**Item-to-item co-occurrence: "people who watched this also watched".** Offline, count how often two videos are engaged with in the same session. Normalise by popularity, or every video's top neighbour becomes the most popular video on the platform. A common choice is a cosine form, `co(i, j) / sqrt(n(i) × n(j))`. Store the top 200 neighbours per video in a key-value store under `i2i:v3:{videoId}`.

At request time, take the user's **last 20 interactions**, fetch their neighbour lists (20 × 200 = **4,000** raw ids), sum the similarity scores per id, and keep the top 500. The lists are precomputed, but *which* lists you read is decided per request, so this source reacts to the last two minutes instantly. That is what finds the bread. Size: 10M videos with enough engagement to have neighbours × 200 × 12 bytes (8-byte id, 4-byte score) = **24 GB**, a small sharded KV store refreshed daily.

**Content-based: "this looks like what you watched".** Embed each video from its frames, audio, caption and hashtags, and retrieve videos near the user's recent ones in that space. It needs **no engagement history**, which makes it the main door for new videos.

**Trending and popular: "everyone is watching this".** Windowed counters per region and language (the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-timeseries/`}>time-series pattern</a>), ranked by **velocity** (growth rate) rather than raw count, so yesterday's hit doesn't sit there for nine days. This is the honest version of the first launch: useful as one source among six, poor as the only one.

**Social graph: "from people you follow".** New posts from followed creators, and what friends engaged with. These in-network candidates come from the push/pull machinery of <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-fanout/#ranking`}>Fan-out · ranked feeds</a>, which treats ranking as its final stage. This lesson goes deeper on that stage.

**Fresh and under-exposed: "give this a chance".** A pool of recent uploads that have not had enough impressions to be judged. It exists for cold start and exploration (Chapters 11 and 12).

| Source | Finds | Needs | Computed | New user? | New video? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Two-tower ANN | Personal taste, broadly | User history (plus context) | Item vectors offline, user vector per request | Weakly, from context | Yes, if the item tower uses content features |
| Item-to-item | "More like what I just did" | Recent interactions | Lists offline, lookup per request | After a few swipes | No, needs co-engagement |
| Content-based | Similar topic, sound, look | Content embeddings | Offline per item | From onboarding interests | **Yes** |
| Trending | What is hot here, now | Nothing personal | Streaming counters | **Yes** | Only once it's hot |
| Social graph | Creators and friends you chose | Follows | Fan-out / pull | No follows yet | Yes, for followers |
| Fresh pool | Under-exposed new uploads | Nothing | Streaming index of recent uploads | Yes | **Yes**, its purpose |

**The merge.** Union and dedupe. Keep a **source tag** per id (a video found by three sources is a strong ranking feature). Apply a **quota** per source so a noisy one can't flood the pool. Then filter. The **seen filter** is usually a per-user Bloom filter of recently shown ids. A false positive merely hides one video the user hasn't seen, which is harmless, and it costs less memory than a full set per user.

<Reveal q="Why normalise co-occurrence counts by popularity?">

Raw co-occurrence is dominated by popular items. If 30% of sessions include the week's biggest video, it co-occurs with everything and becomes everyone's top neighbour: the same fifty videos again, through a side door. Dividing by `sqrt(n(i) × n(j))` asks "do these two appear together *more than their popularity predicts*?", which is the signal you wanted.

</Reveal>

<h2 id="two-tower">Chapter 3 · Two towers, embeddings and ANN search</h2>

**Two-tower** is the retrieval model interviewers name most. It is two neural networks:

- A **user tower**: user id embedding, the sequence of recently watched videos, onboarding interests, and context (country, language, device, hour). It outputs a **user vector**, say 128 numbers.
- An **item tower**: video id embedding, content embeddings (frames, audio, caption), creator, duration, language, age. It outputs an **item vector** of the same size.

An **embedding** is just a vector of numbers learned so that similar things end up close together. Training makes **engagement ≈ the dot product** of the two vectors. Positives are impressions the user engaged with. Negatives are commonly the *other* items in the same training batch (in-batch negatives). Popular items appear in many batches, so they get punished as negatives too often. Published work corrects for this sampling bias (the "logQ correction" in Google's 2019 two-tower paper, in Further study). You need the name, not the derivation.

**A tiny example.** Use 2 dimensions: (cooking, cats). A user who loves cooking is `(0.9, 0.1)`. A bread video is `(0.8, 0.0)` and a cat video `(0.1, 0.9)`. Dot products: bread = 0.9 × 0.8 + 0.1 × 0.0 = **0.72**. Cat = 0.9 × 0.1 + 0.1 × 0.9 = **0.18**. Bread is retrieved first.

**Dot product vs cosine.** If vectors are normalised to length 1, dot product equals cosine similarity and only direction matters. If not, a long vector scores high against everything, and popular items tend to get long vectors. Many systems normalise, or accept the popularity lift deliberately. Pick one and be consistent between training and the index.

**Two-tower vs matrix factorisation.** Matrix factorisation learns a free vector per user id and per item id, so a new id has no vector. A two-tower model computes vectors from *features* (history, content), so a new video or new user still gets a vector. That is why two-tower replaced it for retrieval at scale.

**The serving trick that makes it a systems answer:**

- **Item vectors precompute offline.** Items change slowly and there are 100M of them. They load into an **ANN index** (see below).
- **The user vector computes at request time.** One cheap forward pass, so it reflects what the user did five minutes ago.
- **Candidate generation = ANN lookup:** "the 1,000 items nearest this user vector, in a few milliseconds."

<Figure caption="Two-tower serving: the item side is computed offline and indexed; the user side is computed per request. The model version ties them together.">

**Why a dot product, of all things?** Because it is what makes ANN possible: item vectors can be indexed once and searched for any user vector. The price is that **the user and item never meet inside the model**. There are no "this user × this creator" cross features. That is why two-tower is a *retrieval* model and not the final ranker, which sees the pair and can use everything.

### Approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) search

**The problem.** Exact search compares the user vector with all 100M item vectors: 100M × 128 multiply-adds ≈ 12.8 billion operations per query. At 18,000 queries/s that is about 230 trillion operations a second. Too much.

**ANN** gives up a little accuracy (it may miss a true top-1,000 neighbour) for a very large speed-up. Two families come up most:

| | HNSW (graph) | IVF-PQ (clusters + compression) |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | A layered graph of neighbours. Search hops from a coarse layer down to the fine one, always moving to a closer node | Cluster vectors into cells; search only the nearest few cells; store each vector compressed |
| Memory | High: raw vectors (or quantised) plus graph links | Low: a 512-byte float32 vector can become about 16-64 bytes (illustrative) |
| Recall at low latency | Usually higher | Lower unless tuned; re-score the top hits with raw vectors |
| Updates | Incremental inserts are supported | Adding to an existing cell works, but clusters drift and need periodic retraining |
| Typical use | Fits in RAM, best quality | Billions of vectors, or tight memory |

Each has a knob that trades recall for latency: `efSearch` in HNSW, the number of cells searched (`nprobe`) in IVF. **Measure recall against exact search offline, then pick the knob from your latency budget.** Never tune it by feel.

**Filters are the hard part.** Retrieval must skip removed, blocked and region-restricted videos. If you search first and filter after, a strict filter can leave you with 40 results instead of 1,000. Options: over-fetch (ask for 3,000 to get 1,000), filter inside the index traversal if your index supports it (check current docs), or use separate indexes per big partition such as language.

**Worked numbers: the index.**

- Raw vectors: 100M × 128 dimensions × 4 bytes (float32) = **51.2 GB**. Quantised to int8: **12.8 GB**.
- HNSW graph links: with `M = 16`, the bottom layer keeps up to 32 neighbours per node × 4 bytes ≈ 128 bytes per vector ≈ **12.8 GB**, a little more for upper layers.
- So about **64 GB** with float32 vectors and graph, or about 26 GB quantised: one large machine holds a full replica. **Replicate for throughput** rather than shard. If a replica serves about 2,000 queries/s at your recall target (an assumption: benchmark it), 18,000/s at peak needs **9 replicas**, plus headroom. Shard only when a replica no longer fits in memory, and then you pay a scatter-gather per query.

**Keeping the index fresh.** New videos must be retrievable within minutes. HNSW supports **incremental inserts**: embed a new upload from its content features as soon as they are extracted, and insert it into the live index. Deletions are usually tombstones plus a filter, cleaned up by periodic rebuilds. A **full rebuild** happens whenever the model is retrained, because a new model means a new embedding space.

<Reveal q="Why not use the two-tower score as the final ranking?">

It was built to be cheap, not precise. The dot product can't model interactions between user and item features (this user's history *with this creator*). It is usually trained for one objective (engagement). And it is optimised to find good items among 100M, not to order the top 400 perfectly. The ranker is a different, slower model that sees cross features and predicts several outcomes. Using the retrieval score as a feature *in* the ranker is common and fine.

</Reveal>

**Interview takeaway.** "Item vectors are computed offline per model version and put in an HNSW index. The user vector is computed per request from recent behaviour. Retrieval is an ANN lookup, and the user tower and item index always deploy together by model version."

<h2 id="ranking">Chapter 4 · Ranking: guessing what a thumb will do</h2>

Say the first ranker is trained to maximise watch time. Within a week the feed fills with eleven-minute compilations and videos that say "wait for the end" in the first second. Watch time is up 9%. Hides are up 40%. The model did exactly what it was told. The real question is what you actually want.

**The ranker predicts what the user will do with each candidate.** Not one number but several, from one **multi-task** model with a shared body and one output head per outcome:

- `p(start)` or `p(click)`: will they engage at all (matters more where the user chooses, like a grid of thumbnails)
- `E[watch time]`, or `p(finish)`: how much of it will they watch
- `p(like)`, `p(share)`, `p(comment)`, `p(follow)`: explicit positive signals
- `p(skip fast)`, `p(hide)`, `p(report)`: explicit and implicit negative signals

A **value model** then combines them into one score. The simplest form is a weighted sum:

`score = 0.1 × E[watch seconds] + 10 × p(like) + 30 × p(share) − 50 × p(hide)`

**Worked numbers.** Three candidates, same user:

| Video | E[watch] | p(like) | p(share) | p(hide) | Watch term | Like | Share | Hide | **Score** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A · cooking, 60 s | 40 s | 0.02 | 0.002 | 0.01 | 4.0 | 0.2 | 0.06 | −0.5 | **3.76** |
| B · funny clip, 15 s | 12 s | 0.10 | 0.03 | 0.005 | 1.2 | 1.0 | 0.9 | −0.25 | **2.85** |
| C · "wait for the end" | 25 s | 0.01 | 0.001 | 0.06 | 2.5 | 0.1 | 0.03 | −3.0 | **−0.37** |

Ranked by watch time alone: A, **C**, B, so the bait is second. Ranked by the combined score: A, B, **C**, so the bait is last. The only change is that the product said what "good" means.

**Where the weights come from.** Not from the training loss. They encode product judgement ("a share is worth three likes") and are tuned by **online experiments**: try a weight change on 1% of users and measure retention and guardrails (Chapter 14). Two details that sound like production experience:

- **Normalise watch time**, or long videos win by being long. Predicting completion rate, or watch time relative to duration, is a common fix.
- **Calibrate every head.** A *calibrated* probability is honest: of the videos predicted at 0.10, about 10% really get liked. A weighted sum only means what the weights say if each probability is honest. If `p(like)` predicts 0.10 for videos liked 5% of the time, the like term silently counts double. Monitor predicted vs observed rates per head.

**Model families**, one sentence each. Gradient-boosted trees are strong on tabular features and simple to serve. Deep models add learned embeddings for ids and explicit feature crosses (Google's Wide & Deep and Meta's DLRM are published examples). Sequence models read the user's recent history directly. In an interview the family matters far less than the inputs, outputs and latency.

**The light ranker** is a smaller model, often distilled from the heavy one, with a few dozen cheap features. Its job is to cut 2,000 to 400 without dropping items the heavy ranker would have put on top.

**Serving it.** The heavy ranker scores all 400 candidates of a request as **one batch** on a GPU or a vectorised CPU path. Per-item calls would never fit 60 ms.

<Trace title="Score three videos" steps={scoring} takeaway="Predict several outcomes, combine them with product weights, and keep every head calibrated so the weights mean what they say." />

<Reveal q="Why not just optimise watch time? It's the closest thing to “the user enjoyed it”.">

Any single proxy gets gamed, by the model and by creators. Watch time rewards long videos and cliffhanger bait. Clicks reward misleading thumbnails. Likes miss passive enjoyment. Combining several signals, including **negative** ones (hides, fast skips, reports), makes it much harder to win one metric while losing the user. And the true goal, people coming back next week, is measured online, where the weights are tuned.

</Reveal>

**Interview takeaway.** "The ranker is multi-task: heads for watch time, like, share, hide. A product-owned weighted value model combines them, with negative weight on negative signals. Each head is calibrated, and the weights are tuned by A/B test."

**Common slip:** "The model ranks by engagement." Which engagement? Say the heads, the formula, who sets the weights, and that negative signals carry negative weight.

<h2 id="rerank">Chapter 5 · Re-ranking: a page is not a sorted list</h2>

The new ranker is better. It is also very sure that you love one creator. You open the feed and get five videos in a row from the same baker. Each one alone is a great recommendation. Together they are a channel you did not subscribe to.

The ranker scores each candidate **independently**. But "is this a good page?" is a property of the **set**: variety, freshness, rules. Re-ranking (also called blending, or the policy layer) turns the scored list into the page.

| Rule | Example | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Diversity** | No two adjacent videos from one creator; at most 2 per creator per page; mix topics and formats | Independent scores cluster; sameness kills sessions |
| **Freshness** | Boost videos under 24 hours old | The ranker's features favour items with history |
| **Dedupe** | Drop already-seen ids; drop near-duplicate re-uploads (perceptual hashes of frames) | Re-uploads of one viral clip look like ten different videos |
| **Exploration and fairness** | Reserve slots for new uploads and new creators (Chapters 11-12) | Otherwise new items never get data |
| **Business** | Ads every N slots, promoted or live content | Product commitments |
| **Policy** | Demote borderline content; enforce age and region rules (Chapter 16) | Hard limits that no score overrides |

**How it is applied.** Usually a **greedy walk**: for each slot, take the highest-scored remaining candidate that breaks no rule. Softer diversity is often done with **MMR** (maximal marginal relevance). It picks the next item maximising `λ × score − (1 − λ) × (its highest similarity to anything already on the page)`, so a near-copy of what is already there loses points. Hard rules (max 2 per creator) are filters; MMR is a soft penalty.

**Worked example.** Ranked order a1, a2, b1, a3, c1, b2 (letters are creators). Rules: no adjacent same creator, at most 2 per creator, page of 5.

- Slot 1: **a1**. Slot 2: a2 is blocked (adjacent a), so **b1**. Slot 3: **a2** (a's second). Slot 4: a3 is blocked twice (adjacent a, and a already has 2), so **c1**. Slot 5: **b2**.
- Page: a1, b1, a2, c1, b2.

<Trace title="Walk the rules" steps={rerank} takeaway="Re-ranking is a greedy walk down the scored list, taking the best candidate that breaks no page rule." />

**Paging an infinite feed.** A ranked feed for followed accounts needs a per-session snapshot so pages don't repeat or skip (<a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-fanout/#ranking`}>Fan-out, ranked feeds</a>). A For You feed is different: there is no fixed list to page through. Each request ranks afresh, **excluding everything already shown this session**, so it can use the session's newest signals. To keep the next page instant, the server can keep a short backlog of the next ranked items in a session cache with a TTL of minutes, and the client fetches the next page while the user is still watching.

**Interview takeaway.** "After scoring, a re-ranker walks the list greedily applying page rules: diversity, freshness, dedupe, exploration slots, policy. Rules live in config behind flags so product can tune them by experiment."

**Common slip:** rules scattered through the code. Keep re-ranking rules as **configuration** behind flags, so "max 2 per creator" can be tuned through an experiment instead of a deploy (<a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/feature-flags/`}>Feature flags & experimentation</a>).

<h2 id="latency">Chapter 6 · The latency budget: two hundred milliseconds</h2>

Imagine the feed's p99 latency jumps from 180 ms to 900 ms at 9:04 p.m. The ranker is fine and the feature store is fine. One candidate source, "friends liked", waits on a graph service that is having a bad night, and the whole feed waits with it. The question to ask: how many of the six sources does a request actually need to show a good page?

**Spend the budget out loud, stage by stage.** Server-side p99 budget: **200 ms** for the first page.

| Stage | Budget | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fetch user context and session features | 10 ms | One read from the online feature store |
| Candidate sources, **in parallel** | 25 ms | Each source has its own 25 ms timeout |
| Merge, dedupe, filter | 5 ms | Bloom filter, blocklists in memory |
| Feature hydration for ~2,000 candidates | 20 ms | Batched multi-gets; hot item features cached in-process |
| Light ranker 2,000 → 400 | 15 ms | |
| Heavy ranker, 400 in one batch | 60 ms | The biggest line, and the biggest cost |
| Re-rank | 10 ms | |
| Hydrate metadata, build response | 15 ms | Titles, creator names, playback URLs |
| **Subtotal** | **160 ms** | |
| Headroom | 40 ms | Network jitter, GC pauses, a retry |

**The tail of a fan-out.** Six sources run in parallel, each independently slow (over 25 ms) 1% of the time. The chance at least one is slow is 1 − 0.99⁶ ≈ **5.9%**, about one request in seventeen. If the feed waits for all six, the slowest source sets everyone's latency. So **every source has a timeout, and the feed ranks whatever arrived in time.** A missing source makes the page slightly less personal. A missing page loses the session. (More on tail latency: <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/core-concepts/#tail-latency`}>Core concepts · tail latency</a>.)

  <Mermaid code={`sequenceDiagram

    F->>FS: user and session features
    F->>S: fan out to six sources, 25 ms timeout
    S-->>F: five answered, one timed out

    F->>FS: batch features for 2,000 candidates

    F-->>C: 10 videos plus impression ids`} />

**Every stage needs a way to be skipped:**

| If this fails… | Serve… |
|---|---|
| One candidate source | The others (timeout) |
| Feature store, partially | Default values the model was trained with for missing features, and an alert on the fill rate |
| Heavy ranker | Light-ranker order |
| All ranking | A cached popular list for the user's region and language |
| Everything personal | That same cached list: a worse feed is better than an error screen |

**The latency the user feels is mostly the first page.** After that, the client asks for the next page when about three videos remain, while the current one plays, so the server's 200 ms is hidden behind playback. That is why the first-page p99 is the number to defend.

<Trace title="Spend the budget" steps={latency} takeaway="Allocate the budget per stage, run sources in parallel with timeouts, and give every stage a fallback that skips it." />

**Interview takeaway.** "200 ms p99 split per stage with 40 ms headroom. Sources run in parallel with their own timeouts, and every stage has a fallback so a slow dependency degrades personalisation instead of availability."

**Common slip:** adding up averages. Stages that each hit their *average* can still blow the *p99*, because the slowest of several parallel calls is what you wait for. Budget p99 per stage, keep headroom, and cut stragglers with timeouts.

<h2 id="features">Chapter 7 · Features and the feature store</h2>

Imagine a spreadsheet of four hundred rows, one per feature the ranker uses, with the job that computes each. Eleven rows have no owner. Nobody knows who computes them, and the model loves them. That is how feature systems rot.

**Four families of features**, and you should be able to name two of each:

| Family | Examples | Changes |
|---|---|---|
| **User** | Language, country, account age, topic affinities over 7 and 30 days, average watch-through rate | Hours to days (plus session features, Chapter 9) |
| **Item** | Duration, topic, creator, upload age, like rate in the last hour, completion rate, content embedding | Minutes (counters) to never (duration) |
| **Context** | Hour of day, day of week, device, network type, position on the page | Per request |
| **Cross** (user × item) | Times this user finished this creator's videos in 30 days; similarity of the item to the user's last 10 watches; the two-tower score | Per pair, often the strongest signals |

**Where each feature is computed** decides its freshness and cost:

| Path | What | Staleness | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Batch** (daily/hourly jobs over the warehouse) | Heavy aggregates such as 30-day topic affinities | Hours | Cheap per value |
| **Streaming** (a stream processor over the event log) | Windowed counters such as "likes in the last 10 minutes" (<a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-timeseries/`}>time-series pattern</a>) | Seconds | Moderate; always-on jobs |
| **Request time** | Context, and cross features computed from values already fetched | None | Latency on every request |

**The feature store** is the infrastructure piece interviewers probe. It holds:

- a **registry**: one definition per feature (name, entity, computation, owner, version);
- an **offline store**: the full history of each feature's values with timestamps, in the warehouse, used to build training sets;
- an **online store**: only the latest value per key, in a low-latency KV store (Redis, DynamoDB, Cassandra...), used at serving;
- **materialisation**: batch and streaming jobs that compute each feature once and write both stores.

| | Offline store | Online store |
|---|---|---|
| Holds | Every value, with timestamps | Latest value only |
| Used by | Training set builds | Serving, per request |
| Read pattern | Large scans, point-in-time joins | Millions of key lookups per second |
| Backed by | Warehouse / lake | Low-latency KV |

<Figure caption="The feature store: one definition, two consumers. Batch and streaming jobs write both the online store (latest value, for serving) and the offline store (history, for training).">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    EV[["Event log"]] --> SP["Stream jobs\nwindowed counters"]
    WH[("Warehouse")] --> BJ["Batch jobs\ndaily aggregates"]
    REG["Feature registry\none definition each"] -.-> SP
    REG -.-> BJ
    SP --> ON[("Online store\nlatest value, KV")]
    BJ --> ON
    SP --> OFF[("Offline store\nhistory with timestamps")]
    BJ --> OFF
    ON --> RK["Ranker at serving"]
    OFF --> TR["Training sets\npoint-in-time joins"]`} />
</Figure>

**The online store is a cache, so give it the four promises** from <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/caching/`}>Caching</a>:

1. **Key**: `f:item:v3:{videoId}` maps to a packed blob of that item's features; `f:user:v3:{userId}` likewise. The version is in the key, so a schema change is a new key space.
2. **TTL**: batch features about 48 hours, long enough that one failed daily job doesn't empty the store. Streaming counters are overwritten continuously.
3. **Invalidation**: by overwrite. The pipeline is the only writer.
4. **Failure plan**: a missing feature gets the **same default value training used**, the request continues, and a fill-rate alert fires.

**Worked numbers: why feature traffic is a design problem.** The heavy ranker sees 400 candidates × 200 features × 4 bytes = **320 KB per request**. At 18,000 requests/s that is **5.76 GB/s** of feature data moving into the rankers. Two consequences: fetch the user's features **once** per request, not once per candidate, and cache **item** features in-process on the ranker hosts, because the same popular items appear in thousands of requests per second.

**Common slip:** "the ranker queries the database for features." For 2,000 candidates per request at 18,000 requests per second, that is 36M lookups per second against a system of record. Features are **precomputed** into an online store built for this.

<h2 id="skew">Chapter 8 · Training/serving skew</h2>

Suppose a new model's offline AUC beats the old one by a margin nobody has seen in a year. The A/B test runs for two weeks and nothing moves. Someone opens two files side by side: the SQL that computes "user 7-day clicks" for training, and the service code that computes it for serving. One counts clicks on ads. The other doesn't. Same name, different feature. The model learned one and is being fed the other.

**Training/serving skew**: the model sees one distribution of inputs in training and a different one in production, and silently underperforms. No error, no crash, only a gap between offline and online results. It comes in four flavours:

| Flavour | Example |
|---|---|
| **Two implementations** | Training computes a feature in SQL, serving in service code; they disagree on edge cases |
| **Different freshness** | Training uses the nightly value; serving uses a value updated a minute ago |
| **Different defaults** | Missing values are 0 in training and −1 in serving |
| **Leakage** (the worst) | Training joins feature values from *after* the impression |

**Point-in-time correctness.** A training example is "this impression, at this moment, with these features, and this outcome". The features must be the values **as of the moment the impression was served**. Suppose an impression from Monday at 10:00 is joined on Friday to the video's *current* like count. That count includes likes that came *because of* impressions like this one. The model learns to lean on information it will never have at serving time. Offline metrics glow; online, nothing.

<Figure caption="Two ways to get point-in-time training data. Log-and-wait makes skew impossible by construction; point-in-time joins let you backfill new features.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart TB
    subgraph A["Log and wait"]
      S1["Serve impression"] --> L1["Log the exact feature\nvector used, with impression id"]
      L1 --> J1["Join to labels\nthat arrive later"]
    end
    subgraph B["Point-in-time join"]
      I2["Impression at time t"] --> J2["For each feature, the latest\nvalue with timestamp before t"]
      O2[("Offline store\nhistory")] --> J2
    end`} />
</Figure>

| | Log and wait | Point-in-time join |
|---|---|---|
| How | Log the feature vector the ranker used, keyed by impression id; join labels later | Offline store keeps timestamped values; for each impression take the latest value before its time |
| Skew risk | None for logged features, by construction | Only as safe as the backfill code matches serving |
| New features | No history until you have logged for a while | Can be backfilled over old impressions |
| Cost | Storage of one vector per impression | Storage of full feature history |

Most mature systems use both: logged features for what is in production, point-in-time joins to experiment with new features. Either way, **one definition in the registry** feeds both paths, which is the feature store's reason to exist. Then **monitor**: compare each feature's distribution at serving with its distribution in training, and alert on drift.

<Trace title="Find the leak" steps={skew} takeaway="Features as of the impression time, from one definition. Log what you served; join labels later." />

Check served-vs-training feature distributions first: it is the cheapest to rule out.

</Reveal>

<h2 id="realtime">Chapter 9 · Real-time signals: the last five minutes</h2>

Open the feed, skip three dog videos in under two seconds each, and swipe again. A dog. The feed is like talking to someone who isn't listening. When will the model learn that you don't want dogs right now? "Tonight, at the retrain" is the wrong layer. The right question: what has to change before the next swipe?

In a short-video feed, the **session** is the strongest signal there is. Someone who skipped three dog videos in ten seconds is telling you something no 30-day aggregate can.

**The model doesn't need to change for the feed to react. Its inputs do.**

- **Session features**: the last N watched video ids (with watch-through and skips), topics engaged and skipped this session, time since session start. Written to the online store **per event**, read at the next request.
- **The user vector reads them.** The user tower takes the recent watch sequence as input and runs per request, so retrieval shifts after a single swipe. The ranker gets cross features such as "similarity of this candidate to the videos skipped this session".
- **Streaming item counters**: likes, completions and hides per video in sliding windows, so a video that is suddenly working gets noticed in minutes, and one that suddenly gets reported gets demoted.

**The freshness ladder.** Match each component to its natural rate. Matching them *is* the design:

| Component | Refresh | Why that rate |
|---|---|---|
| Ranking model weights | Daily-ish (faster with care) | Training is heavy; behaviour patterns shift slowly |
| Item embeddings and ANN index | Hours, plus incremental inserts for new items | 100M items; new ones must appear in minutes |
| Batch features | Daily or hourly | Long-window aggregates |
| Streaming features | Seconds to minutes | Windows over the event log |
| Session features | Per event | The last swipe matters |
| User vector | **Per request** | Must reflect the last few minutes |

**Online learning** means updating model weights continuously from the event stream instead of in daily batches. It adapts to new trends and new items faster. It also learns from bad data faster: a bot wave or a logging bug reaches production in minutes. It needs validation gates, automatic rollback, and monitoring per model update. ByteDance has published a paper on real-time training for recommendations (Monolith, in Further study). Treat it as one company's published approach, not how every feed works.

<Trace title="React to the session" steps={realtime} takeaway="The model can stay the same; its inputs change per event. The user vector is computed per request so retrieval follows the session." />

**Interview takeaway.** "The feed reacts in-session because inputs change per event: session features in the online store and a user vector computed per request. Weights retrain daily. Online learning is an optional extra with its own risks."

**Common slip:** answering "how does the feed react in-session?" with "we retrain in real time". The cheap, standard answer is **session features plus a per-request user vector**.

<h2 id="events">Chapter 10 · Events and labels: every swipe is a sentence</h2>

Imagine the first training job fails a sanity check: 100% of the examples are positive. The client only logged taps. Nothing was logged about videos that scrolled past. As far as the model knows, everyone loves everything they were shown.

**The event pipeline is the recommender's memory.** Everything the model will ever learn comes through it.

| Event | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Impression** | The video was actually on screen (for example, at least half visible for a moment), with **position** and request id | The denominator; the source of negatives |
| Play start | Playback began | Engagement |
| Watch progress / complete | Seconds watched, completion | Watch-time labels |
| Like, share, comment, follow | Explicit positive | Labels and features |
| **Skip** | Swiped away within, say, 2 seconds | Implicit negative |
| **Hide / not interested / report** | Explicit negative | Strong negative labels; safety signals |

**Impressions are the non-negotiable event.** Without them you can't tell "seen and ignored" from "never shown", so you have no negatives. Log what was *on screen*, not what the server sent: ten videos sent are not ten videos seen.

<Figure caption="The event pipeline. One log feeds three consumers: streaming features, the label joiner, and the warehouse.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    APP["Client\nbatches events"] --> COL["Collector\ndedupe by event id"]
    COL --> LOG[["Event log\nkeyed by user id"]]
    LOG --> SF["Stream jobs\nsession + item counters"]
    SF --> ON[("Online feature store")]
    LOG --> LJ["Label joiner\nimpression + outcomes"]
    LOG --> LAKE[("Warehouse / lake")]
    LJ --> TD[("Training data\nfeatures as served + labels")]
    TD --> TRAIN["Daily training"]`} />
</Figure>

**Plumbing details that earn credit** (all from <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/queues-streams/`}>Queues & streams</a>):

- The client batches events and retries. Every event carries an **event id**, and the collector dedupes, so at-least-once delivery doesn't double-count a like.
- The log is **keyed by user id**, so one user's events stay in order in one partition. Session features depend on order.
- Every event carries the **impression id** issued by the feed response, which is how outcomes join back to the exact features that were served.

**Defining the label is a product decision.** "Positive" might be *watched at least 50%, or at least 10 seconds, or any explicit positive action*. "Negative" might be *shown and skipped within 2 seconds*. Each head of the ranker gets its own label. Labels also need a **window**: watch outcomes are known within the session; a follow might be credited if it happens within 24 hours. A window that is too short misses outcomes. One that is too long delays training.

**Position bias.** The video in slot 1 gets more engagement than slot 5 regardless of content. If training ignores this, the model credits content for what the position did and keeps reinforcing whatever it already put first. A common fix: **log position, use it as a feature in training, and set it to one fixed value at serving**, so every candidate is scored as if it were in the same slot. Exploration traffic (Chapter 12) also helps measure it.

**Worked numbers.** 6B impressions/day × about 3 events per impression = **18B events/day**. At about 200 bytes each: **3.6 TB/day**. Per second: 18B ÷ 10⁵ = **180,000 events/s** average, **540,000/s** at 3× peak, about **108 MB/s** into the log at peak. Ninety days of raw events for training is about **324 TB** before compression. Bots and test accounts are filtered before any of it becomes a label.

<Trace title="Label the swipe" steps={events} takeaway="Log impressions with position, or you have no negatives. Define labels on purpose, and correct for position bias." />

**Interview takeaway.** "I log impressions with position and request id, plus outcomes and negatives, with idempotent event ids on a user-keyed log. Labels and windows are defined per head, and position is logged and neutralised at serving."

**Common slip:** "we log clicks." Say impressions (with position and request id), positive and negative outcomes, idempotent event ids, and how a label is defined.

<h2 id="cold-start">Chapter 11 · Cold start: nobody knows you yet</h2>

Two complaints arrive on the same day. A new user writes: "Every video is the same three pop songs." A creator writes: "I've uploaded twelve videos and none has more than four views." It is the same problem twice. The model has never met either of them. **Cold start** is what the system does with a user or item it has no history for.

**A new user** has no history, so the personal sources return nothing useful. The first session has to learn fast:

- **Onboarding**: ask for a few interests (topics, creators). They seed content-based retrieval immediately.
- **Context priors**: popular *for their region, language, device and time of day*, which is far better than global popular.
- **Learn in the session**: session features (Chapter 9) make the second page already different from the first.
- **A bandit framing**: treat the first pages as choosing among broad categories. A **contextual bandit** picks the category it expects to do best for users like this one, sometimes tries another, and updates after each response. It learns what lands in a handful of swipes (the next chapter's machinery, applied to a stranger).

**A new video** has no engagement, so the collaborative sources can't see it:

- **Content embeddings**: the item tower uses content features (frames, audio, caption, creator), so a new video gets a vector as soon as its content is processed and is inserted into the index within minutes. A tower built only on item id embeddings would leave new items invisible.
- **An exploration budget**: reserve a share of impressions for under-exposed videos. At this scale, 6B impressions/day × 5% = **300M exploration impressions**, ÷ 2M uploads/day = **about 150 impressions per upload**. That is enough for a first read on whether it lands.
- **Staged exposure** (a common approach): show the new video to a small, well-matched audience. If completion and likes beat a threshold, widen the audience. Each stage costs little, and good videos climb quickly.

<Figure caption="Staged exposure for a new upload. Each stage spends a few impressions and either widens the audience or stops.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    U["New upload\ncontent embedding"] --> S1["Stage 1\n~150 matched viewers"]
    S1 -->|"completion and likes\nabove threshold"| S2["Stage 2\n~5,000 viewers"]
    S1 -->|"below threshold"| D["Stop: normal\nretrieval only"]
    S2 -->|"above threshold"| S3["Stage 3\nwide audience"]
    S2 -->|"below"| D`} />
</Figure>

**A new creator** gets the same treatment, plus, if product decides, a small boost, because creators who get zero views stop uploading. That is a product choice about the health of the catalogue. Say so.

| Cold entity | Missing | Cover with |
|---|---|---|
| New user | History | Onboarding interests, context priors, session features, bandit |
| New video | Engagement | Content features in the item tower, fresh pool, exploration budget, staged exposure |
| New creator | Track record | Same as new video, plus an optional product-chosen boost |

<h2 id="explore">Chapter 12 · Exploration: the slot machine that learns</h2>

Two videos. Video X: 3 likes in 10 views. Video Y: 300 likes in 1,000 views. The ranker shows Y, always. But X has a higher like rate. Is X better? Nobody knows: ten views is nothing. How do you find out without wasting a thousand views on every unknown?

**Exploitation** shows what the model believes is best. **Exploration** shows something less certain to learn whether it is better. A system that only exploits never learns about anything it has not already shown.

**ε-greedy** is the simplest. With probability ε, fill a slot from an exploration pool. Otherwise exploit. With ε = 0.05 and pages of 10, that is 0.5 exploration slots per page on average, the same 5% budget as Chapter 11 seen from the other side. It is simple and easy to reason about, and it explores blindly: a known-bad item is as likely to be picked as a promising one.

**Thompson sampling** explores where it is *uncertain*. Keep a belief about each item's like rate as a Beta distribution, `Beta(likes + 1, non-likes + 1)`. Each time, draw a random sample from each candidate's distribution and show the highest draw.

| Video | Evidence | Belief | Mean | Spread |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| X | 3 likes / 10 views | Beta(4, 8) | 0.333 | Wide (std ≈ 0.13) |
| Y | 300 likes / 1,000 views | Beta(301, 701) | 0.300 | Narrow (std ≈ 0.014) |

Head to head, X's draw beats Y's about **57%** of the time (a quick simulation confirms it). So X gets shown, its evidence grows, and its distribution narrows. If its true rate is lower than Y's, it soon stops winning. **Exploration fades on its own as evidence accumulates.**

**UCB** (upper confidence bound) is the deterministic cousin: score = estimated rate + a bonus that shrinks with more views. Same intuition, no randomness.

| | ε-greedy | Thompson sampling | UCB |
|---|---|---|---|
| Explores | Blindly, at a fixed rate | Where uncertain | Where uncertain |
| Randomness | Random slots | Random draws from beliefs | None (deterministic) |
| Fades with data? | No, unless you decay ε | Yes, automatically | Yes, the bonus shrinks |
| Effort | Tiny | A belief per item or arm | A bonus term per item or arm |

<Figure caption="Filling one slot. Most slots exploit; a few go to an exploration pool or to the item with the highest sampled belief.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    SL["Fill a slot"] --> Q{"Explore?\nprobability ε"}
    Q -->|"No"| EX["Best legal item\nby score"]
    Q -->|"Yes"| PO["Pick from exploration pool\nor sample beliefs (Thompson)"]
    EX --> LG["Log the item and the\nprobability it was chosen"]
    PO --> LG`} />
</Figure>

In real feeds, exploration is rarely a textbook bandit over every item. It is usually a mix: an exploration budget for new items, uncertainty bonuses in ranking, and randomised slots, each tuned and measured.

**Log the propensity.** For every shown item, log the probability that the policy would show it (1 for a pure exploit slot, small for a random explore slot). Later, training and offline evaluation can **reweight** examples by the inverse of that probability (inverse propensity scoring, IPS), which corrects for the fact that the logged data came from your own choices.

<Trace title="Explore on purpose" steps={explore} takeaway="ε-greedy explores at a fixed rate; Thompson sampling explores where it is uncertain, and fades as evidence grows. Log the propensity of every impression." />

<Reveal q="Exploration costs engagement because some explore slots will be worse. How do you justify it to product?">

Measure it. Run the exploration budget as an experiment: users with and without it, over weeks. Typically the short-term cost is small and the long-term gain is a catalogue that stays fresh, creators who keep uploading, and a model with data about more than the head. Without exploration the feed slowly converges to fewer and fewer items: the same fifty videos, at scale.

</Reveal>

**Interview takeaway.** "Exploration is a budget: new and uncertain items get slots, via ε-greedy or Thompson-style uncertainty. I log the propensity of every impression, and I justify the cost with an experiment."

**Common slip:** treating exploration as randomness for its own sake. Say what is explored (new items, uncertain items), how much (a budget with a number), and how it is measured.

<h2 id="feedback-loops">Chapter 13 · Feedback loops: the rich get recommended</h2>

A year later, the dashboard shows a familiar shape. The top 1% of creators get 70% of Discover impressions. Sessions are fine and engagement per impression is fine. But new creators' uploads reach a median of 40 views. The first fix was for "the same fifty videos". It did not fix why that happened.

**The feedback loop:** the model chooses what to show, users can only engage with what they were shown, that engagement becomes the next training data, and the model learns that what it showed is what people like.

<Figure caption="The loop. Every arrow is reasonable. Together they concentrate impressions on whatever the model already favoured.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    M["Model favours item set S"] --> SH["Shows mostly S"]
    SH --> EN["Engagement only\non what was shown"]
    EN --> TD["Training data\nall about S"]
    TD --> M
    X["Never shown items\nno labels, ever"] -.->|"invisible"| TD`} />
</Figure>

Its symptoms have names:

- **Popularity bias**: popular items get more impressions, so more engagement, so more impressions (the Matthew effect).
- **Filter bubbles**: a user's feed narrows to what they engaged with first.
- **Position bias**: top slots get engagement that the model attributes to content (Chapter 10).
- **Degenerate feedback**: in the extreme, the model's "knowledge" is only about its own past choices.

**Mitigations change the data, not just the model:**

| Mitigation | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Exploration** (Chapters 11-12) | Produces labels for items the model would not have chosen |
| **Propensity logging and inverse propensity weighting** | Corrects for which items were likely to be shown |
| **Popularity correction** in retrieval training | Stops popular items dominating as positives and negatives |
| **Diversity rules** in re-ranking | Caps concentration on any page |
| **A small random holdout** (say 1% of traffic, or a fraction of slots) | Unbiased data for evaluating models and measuring the loop |
| **Long-term metrics** | Retention and catalogue health over weeks, not clicks today |

**Watch the vital signs:** the share of impressions going to the top 1% of creators, and the fraction of new uploads that reach some minimum number of views (say 100). Both should be on the dashboard, with their trends.

<Trace title="Break the loop" steps={loops} takeaway="A model trained only on its own choices reinforces them. Explore, log propensities, hold out random traffic, and watch concentration." />

**Interview takeaway.** "The model only learns from what it shows, so I reserve an exploration budget, log propensities, keep a small random holdout, add diversity rules, and track impression concentration and new-upload reach."

**Common slip:** never mentioning the loop. It is one of the two silent skips interviewers wait for on recommendation questions (cold start is the other).

<h2 id="evaluation">Chapter 14 · Evaluation: offline metrics, A/B tests and the metric that lied</h2>

Suppose a new ranker beats the old one offline on every metric the team tracks. Online, watch time is flat and next-day retention is down a hair. Offline tells you what is worth testing. Online tells you what is true.

### Offline metrics

Offline metrics run on logged data before anything ships. Each measures a different stage.

**AUC** (ranking heads) is the probability that a random positive scores above a random negative. Positives scoring 0.9 and 0.4; negatives 0.7, 0.3, 0.2. Of the 2 × 3 = 6 pairs, 0.9 beats all three negatives and 0.4 beats two: **AUC = 5/6 ≈ 0.83**. It ignores calibration, so pair it with **log loss** and a calibration check per head.

**Recall@k** (retrieval): of the items the user actually engaged with next (held out), what fraction made it into the top k candidates? A user's next 5 watches; 4 of them are in the 2,000 retrieved: **recall@2000 = 0.8**. Retrieval's job is recall, so this is its metric.

**NDCG@k** (the order of a list) rewards putting the most relevant items at the top. `DCG = Σ rel(i) / log₂(i + 1)` over positions i = 1..k; NDCG divides by the DCG of the ideal order. Relevance 2, 0, 3, 1 in ranked order:

- DCG = 2/1 + 0/1.585 + 3/2 + 1/2.322 = 2 + 0 + 1.5 + 0.431 = **3.931**
- Ideal order 3, 2, 1, 0: 3/1 + 2/1.585 + 1/2 + 0 = 3 + 1.262 + 0.5 = **4.762**
- **NDCG@4 = 3.931 / 4.762 ≈ 0.83**

(Another common form uses `2^rel − 1` as the gain. Say which one you mean.)

**Why offline can lie.** Logged data comes from the *old* policy: it only contains outcomes for items the old model chose to show. A new model that would surface different items gets no credit for them. Offline metrics also can't see novelty, diversity, or whether people come back. Propensity-weighted offline evaluation narrows the gap but doesn't close it.

### Online: the A/B test decides

Randomise users into control and treatment (deterministic bucketing by user id, see <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/feature-flags/`}>Feature flags & experimentation</a>) and compare.

| Kind | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Primary** | Watch time per daily user, sessions per user, next-day and 7-day retention |
| **Guardrails** (must not get worse) | p99 latency, error rate, hides and reports per impression, share of impressions to the top 1% of creators, new-upload reach, creator retention |
| **Diagnostics** | Per-head calibration, per-source contribution, coverage of the catalogue |

**How big must the test be?** A rough rule for a two-arm test (80% power, 5% significance): users per arm ≈ 16 × σ² ÷ δ², where σ is the standard deviation of the metric per user and δ is the smallest change you want to detect. Assumption for this example: mean watch time is 40 min/day with σ = 50 min, and you want to detect a 0.5% change, δ = 0.2 min. Then n ≈ 16 × 2,500 ÷ 0.04 = **1,000,000 users per arm**. Halving δ needs four times the users. This is why small ranking gains need huge traffic, and why variance reduction (comparing each user to their own pre-test behaviour) is common. Treat it as an estimate and check your experimentation platform.

**Check the split itself.** In a 50/50 test you expect equal arms. If you see 1,000,000 vs 1,010,000, that is a **sample ratio mismatch**: expected 1,005,000 each, χ² = 2 × 5,000² ÷ 1,005,000 ≈ 50, which is far beyond chance. Something is wrong with assignment or logging (a crash in one arm, bots, a filter applied to one arm). Throw out the result and debug before reading any metric.

**Things that fool A/B tests on feeds:**

- **Novelty effects**: a new feed feels interesting for a few days. Run at least one or two full weeks, covering weekday and weekend.
- **Interference**: users affect each other (a creator's audience is shared across arms, and a heavy-ranker change shifts which creators get views). Marketplace-style effects may need cluster or creator-level randomisation.
- **Long-term effects**: keep a **long-term holdout**, a small group on an older model for months.
- **Peeking**: stopping the moment a metric turns green inflates false positives. Decide the duration up front, or use a method built for sequential testing.

| Method | Question it answers | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Offline metrics** | Is it worth testing? | Fast, free, no users at risk | Biased by the old policy |
| **Interleaving** | Which ranker do users prefer? | Very sensitive: mixes two rankers' results in one list, so each user sees both | Compares rankers only; not for retention or system effects |
| **A/B test** | Does it move the real goal? | The ground truth, with guardrails | Slow, needs a lot of traffic |

<h2 id="serving">Chapter 15 · Serving: precompute or compute</h2>

The infrastructure bill arrives and one line stands out: GPU servers for the ranker. A natural question: why not compute everyone's recommendations overnight and serve them from a cache? Start with the numbers. Say there are 80 million monthly users, and 50 million come back on a given day.

**Batch (precompute) vs request time** is a question about every piece, not the whole system:

| Piece | Precompute? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Item embeddings, ANN index | **Yes**, per model version | 100M items, slow-changing |
| Item-to-item neighbour lists | **Yes**, daily | Expensive joins; the lookup is per request |
| Collaborative-filtering candidates per user | Sometimes, for likely-active users | Expensive; but a day stale |
| User vector | **No**, per request | Must follow the session |
| Ranking | **No**, per request | Needs fresh features and the session |
| Safety filtering | **Never only in advance** | Removals happen any minute (Chapter 16) |

**Worked numbers.** Precomputing 500 candidate ids (8 bytes each) for all 80M monthly users is 80M × 500 × 8 B = **320 GB** every night, and 80M − 50M = **30M lists go unread** on a typical day. For the 50M daily users alone it is 200 GB. The storage is affordable. The problem is that the lists are a day stale and ignore the session. **Precompute what is expensive and slow-changing; compute what depends on the session.**

**Batch scoring still has a home**: recommendations that are *not* read in an interactive session, such as the "videos you might like" email, a push notification, or a weekly digest. They are scored nightly in bulk, where throughput matters and latency doesn't.

<Figure caption="Serving infrastructure. The offline side builds models, indexes and lists; the online side serves requests within the budget. Model versions tie them together.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart TB
    subgraph OFF["Offline"]
      TD[("Training data")] --> TR["Training jobs"] --> REG[("Model registry\nversions")]
      REG --> EMB["Embed all items"] --> BLD["Build ANN index\nper version"]
      WH[("Warehouse")] --> I2I["Item-to-item jobs"]
    end
    subgraph ONL["Online"]
      FD["Feed service"] --> RET["Retrieval services\nANN replicas, i2i KV,\ntrending, follows"]
      FD --> FS[("Online feature store")]
      FD --> MS["Model servers\nlight + heavy ranker"]
      FD --> RR["Re-rank + safety filter"]
    end
    BLD --> RET
    I2I --> RET
    REG --> MS`} />
</Figure>

**Model servers.** The heavy ranker runs on a dedicated fleet (GPU, or optimised CPU) behind the feed service. It batches all candidates of a request, and often batches across requests too. At peak, 7.2M scores/s at an assumed 50,000 scores/s per server is about **144 servers**. It is the largest cost line, which is why the candidate count and the light ranker are the cost levers. New model versions roll out like any risky deploy: **shadow** (score live traffic, discard the result, compare), then **canary** (1% of users), then ramp, all behind flags.

**ANN index refresh.** New items: incremental inserts within minutes. New model version: build the full index offline, load it beside the old one on fresh replicas, warm it, then switch traffic **by model version** together with the matching user tower. Keep the old index for rollback.

**What to cache, with the four promises:**

| Cache | Key | TTL | Invalidation | If it fails |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Item features (in-process on rankers) | `f:item:v3:{videoId}` | ~1 minute | TTL; streaming counters arrive fresh in the online store | Read the online store |
| Item-to-item lists | `i2i:v3:{videoId}` | ~48 hours | Overwritten daily | Skip the source |
| Popular per region | `pop:{region}:{lang}` | ~5 minutes | Recomputed by the stream job | Last good copy |
| Session backlog | `sess:{sessionId}:next` | ~10 minutes | Replaced on each request | Rank afresh |

<Trace title="Choose what to precompute" steps={serving} takeaway="Precompute the item side and expensive lists; compute the user vector and ranking per request; swap indexes by model version." />

**Interview takeaway.** "Precompute the slow, expensive item side. Compute the user vector and ranking per request. Batch-score only emails and pushes. New models go shadow, canary, then ramp, with the index swapped by model version."

**Common slip:** "We precompute everyone's feed nightly and cache it." Say what that costs (staleness, wasted lists, no session) and what you would precompute instead.

<h2 id="safety">Chapter 16 · Safety and privacy: things we never show</h2>

At 2:00 p.m. trust and safety removes a video. At 2:40 p.m. a user reports seeing it in the feed. Tracing shows it was in a precomputed candidate list from 1:00 p.m., and the only removal check ran when the list was built. Caches are allowed to be wrong about what a user would like. They are never allowed to be wrong about this.

<Figure caption="Where filters run. Hard filters run early to save compute and again at the very end, after anything read from a cache.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    R["Retrieval\n(early hard filter)"] --> RK["Ranking\n(soft demotions as features)"] --> RR["Re-rank"] --> F["Final hard filter\nlive blocklist"] --> P["Page"]
    C[("Caches")] -.-> R
    C -.-> RR`} />
</Figure>

**Safety filters come in two strengths:**

| | Hard filters | Soft demotions |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Removed videos, blocked or muted creators, age-restricted content for younger users, content not licensed in the user's region | Borderline content scored by integrity classifiers |
| Mechanism | A filter against a fast, fresh blocklist | A lower score or fewer slots; excluded from exploration |
| Where | Early (to save compute) **and** at the very end, after any cache | Features and re-ranking rules |
| Guarantee | Removal is certain | A tuned, measured trade-off |

A score can't be trusted to remove something. A filter can.

**Privacy belongs in the design, not in a footnote:**

- **Consent and controls.** Users can reset or turn off personalisation. Some jurisdictions require large platforms to offer a feed option not based on profiling (the EU's Digital Services Act is the usual example; check current rules before citing specifics).
- **Sensitive attributes.** Don't infer or target on health, religion, sexuality and similar categories, and don't let models learn them as proxies where policy forbids it.
- **Minimisation and retention.** Keep raw events only as long as needed; aggregate older data.
- **Deletion has to reach every derived copy**: the online feature store, cached candidate lists, the event lake, training snapshots and, over time, models retrained without that data. A deleted user's events must not keep training tomorrow's model.
- **Access control** on event logs, because they reveal what people watch, which is personal.

<Trace title="Never serve it" steps={safety} takeaway="Hard rules are filters, not features, and the last one runs after every cache. Deletion must reach every derived store." />

**Interview takeaway.** "Hard rules are filters applied early and again at the end against a live blocklist, after every cache. Soft integrity signals are features and re-ranking rules. Deletion and retention reach every derived store."

**Common slip:** a design where removals only take effect when lists are rebuilt. The final filter always runs at serving time.

**Five follow-ups you will get:**

<Reveal q="1 · How does a video uploaded a minute ago ever get recommended?">

The item tower uses content features (frames, audio, caption, creator), so the video gets an embedding as soon as its content is processed and is inserted into the ANN index incrementally. A fresh-upload source and an exploration budget guarantee it some impressions (5% of 6B is 300M a day, about 150 per upload), and staged exposure widens its audience if it performs.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="2 · How do you know the new model is better?">

Offline first, as a filter: recall@k for retrieval, AUC and calibration per head, NDCG for list order. Then an A/B test on randomised users for at least a week or two: primary metrics are watch time per daily user and retention; guardrails are latency, hides and reports, creator concentration and new-upload reach. Check for sample ratio mismatch before reading results. Offline gains that don't show up online don't ship; check skew and leakage first.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="3 · What is training/serving skew, and how do you prevent it?">

The model sees different feature values in production than in training: two implementations, different freshness, different defaults, or leakage from joining features as of today instead of as of the impression. Prevent it with one definition per feature in a feature store that writes both online and offline stores, point-in-time joins, and ideally by logging the exact served feature vector with each impression and training on that. Monitor served vs training distributions.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="4 · A user skips three videos about dogs. How fast does the feed adapt?">

On the next request. Each event updates session features in the online store within seconds. The user tower runs per request on the recent watch sequence, so retrieval shifts, and the ranker gets cross features like similarity to skipped videos. The model weights don't change (retraining is daily), but its inputs do.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="5 · How do you stop the feed collapsing into the same popular videos?">

Name it as a feedback loop: the model only learns from what it shows. Reserve an exploration budget (ε-greedy slots or Thompson-style uncertainty), log the propensity of every impression for reweighting, keep a small random holdout, apply diversity rules in re-ranking, correct for popularity in retrieval training, and track impression concentration and new-upload reach as guardrails.

</Reveal>

<h2 id="cheat-sheet">Cheat sheet</h2>

| When you see… | Say… |
|---|---|
| "Recommend / personalise / For You" | The funnel: retrieval → light rank → heavy rank → re-rank, with counts and milliseconds |
| "Why not score everything?" | QPS × corpus, out loud: 18,000 × 100M = 1.8 × 10¹² |
| Where candidates come from | Two-tower ANN, item-to-item, content, trending, follows, fresh pool: union, dedupe, quota, filter |
| Two-tower serving | Item vectors offline into ANN; user vector per request; same model version for both |
| "Rank by engagement" | Multi-task heads + product-weighted value model; negative weight on hides; calibrate each head |
| Five videos from one creator | Re-ranking: greedy walk with diversity rules, MMR |
| A slow dependency blows p99 | Per-stage budget, per-source timeouts, a fallback for every stage |
| Feature freshness | Batch for long windows, streaming for counters, request time for context |
| Feature store | One definition, online + offline stores, and the four cache promises for the online one |
| Offline great, online flat | Skew or leakage: point-in-time joins, log served features |
| "React within the session" | Session features + per-request user vector; online learning only as an addition |
| Events and labels | Impressions with position, positives and negatives, event ids, a defined label and window |
| New user / new video | Onboarding, context priors, bandits / content features in item tower, exploration budget with a number |
| Explore vs exploit | ε-greedy for a fixed budget; Thompson sampling explores where uncertain; log propensities |
| Same items everywhere | Feedback loop: exploration, IPS, random holdout, diversity, concentration metrics |
| "How do you measure it?" | Recall@k, AUC, NDCG offline → A/B test on retention and watch time, with guardrails and an SRM check |
| "Precompute it nightly?" | Precompute the item side and expensive lists; rank per request; batch-score only emails and pushes |
| Removed content | Hard filters early and again after every cache; deletion reaches every derived store |

## Five mistakes to leave behind

1. **"A model recommends videos."** Without the funnel, the counts and the milliseconds, it isn't a design.
2. **Optimising one metric.** Watch time alone breeds bait; CTR alone breeds clickbait. Several heads, negative weights, and retention online.
3. **Two definitions of a feature, or features joined as of today.** One definition, features as of the impression, or log what you served.
4. **Logging clicks instead of impressions.** No impressions, no negatives, no position, and no honest training data.
5. **No cold start, no exploration.** New videos stay invisible and the feed collapses into its own favourites. Say both, with numbers.

**Look these up yourself**

- **Matrix factorisation and ALS** (Koren, Bell & Volinsky, 2009): the collaborative-filtering method behind the Netflix Prize era, and the ancestor of two-tower.
- **Item-to-item collaborative filtering** (Linden, Smith & York, 2003): the classic "people who bought this also bought" paper.
- **Sequence models for recommendation** (SASRec, BERT4Rec, and transformer-based user models): how the recent watch sequence becomes a user vector.

- **Multi-stakeholder and fairness-aware ranking**: balancing users, creators and the platform in the re-ranking stage.
