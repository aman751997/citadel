<h2 id="prologue">Prologue · Why typeahead is not a search query</h2>

Here is how a first version usually fails. A team ships a search box and, for every keystroke, sends the typed text to the search cluster as a prefix query. At the evening peak the cluster's CPU sits at 100% and typeahead p99 is 380 ms. Worse, the real searches, the ones people press Enter on, queue behind millions of half-typed words.

The second failure is quieter. A user types "kes" and the box suggests a stranger's name followed by a phone number. One person had searched for it forty times, and the first version ranked by raw count.

**Typeahead** (autocomplete, query suggestion) shows a short list of likely completions under a search box as the user types. The usual one-line answer is *"a trie, top-K per prefix, under 100 ms"*. Those three phrases are right. The rest of this lesson is why each is there, with numbers, in the order the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/delivery-framework/`}>delivery framework</a> asks for, and then the follow-ups: freshness, privacy, hot prefixes, personalisation, languages and failure.

**The one idea under the whole design: two clocks.** Ranking is expensive and may be hours old. Answering must take microseconds, on every keystroke. So you split the system in two. An **offline builder** turns search logs into a precomputed index. An **online reader** only ever looks things up. Every hard question in this round is a question about which clock a piece of work belongs on.

**Where you have seen the parts.** The <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/search/#typeahead`}>Search lesson, chapters 7–9</a> introduced the latency budget, the trie with top-K per node and the three prefix stores. The <a href={`${BASE}dsa/lessons/tries/`}>Tries lesson</a> builds the data structure by hand. This lesson stands alone, and goes further: every interview step, a tested build, and the parts those chapters only sketched.

**Common slips this lesson is built around.**

- **The cache hand-wave.** "Cache the hot prefixes" is as empty as "cache it in Redis" unless it comes with the four promises: key, TTL and why, invalidation, failure plan. Chapter 8 gives all four.
- **Pacing.** The trie is the fun part. Candidates draw it at minute four and never reach freshness or privacy. Reach it at 0:25, as the clock says.
- **Silent skips.** Say out loud what you are not designing (the results page, spelling correction, ads in suggestions) so nobody wonders.

<h2 id="requirements">Chapter 1 · What to build</h2>

A stakeholder usually hands you a list: suggest what people search for, show trending stuff fast, make it personal, support eleven languages, fix typos, and make it instant. That is six features and one adjective. Before drawing, turn it into answers: how many suggestions, ranked by what, how fresh is "fast", and what number is "instant"?

**Functional requirements**, in the order you would build them:

1. **As the user types, return the top K completions of the current prefix** (K = 10 on web, 5 on mobile). Completions are **whole past queries**, not documents: typing "red ru" suggests "red running shoes".
2. **Rank by popularity with recency**: how many distinct people searched it, weighted so this week counts more than last year.
3. **Fresh**: a query that suddenly trends (a storm, a launch) should appear **within minutes**, not after tomorrow's batch.
4. **Safe**: never suggest a private or harmful string, such as names with phone numbers, slurs, or anything a court asked you to remove.

**Stretch, said out loud:** personalisation (the user's own recent searches, location) and typo tolerance ("iphnoe" gives "iphone"). **Out of scope:** the results page, spelling correction as a product, ads in suggestions, denylist admin tooling. Ask the interviewer if they want any of those.

**Languages, in one sentence:** one index per locale, keyed on a normalised form of the text (case-folded, Unicode-normalised); input-method languages such as Chinese and Japanese are a follow-up. Chapter 11 has the details.

**Non-functional requirements, each with a number or a choice:**

| Quality | The requirement | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| **Latency** | **p99 under 100 ms** keystroke to painted suggestions; origin p99 under 10 ms | Precomputed, in memory, in region, hot prefixes at the edge |
| **Availability** | Keep working when anything behind it fails; **availability over consistency** | Every replica serves a possibly stale index rather than an error |
| **Freshness** | Batch ranking daily; trending within about 5 minutes | Two paths: a batch build and a small streaming layer |
| **Scale** | 100M daily users, peaks around 175K keystrokes/s (Chapter 2) | Many stateless replicas; edge caching |
| **Privacy** | No suggestion unless at least *k* distinct users searched it | A filter in the pipeline, not a patch in the UI |

**Why availability over consistency is easy here.** Nobody can tell whether a suggestion list is one minute or one day old, and two replicas on different builds for a few minutes during a rollout harm no one. Typeahead is an aid: if it fails, the user just types the whole query. Say that, because it licenses every degradation in Chapter 12.

**The bridge**, at 0:09: *"Top 10 completions per keystroke, ranked by decayed popularity, trending within minutes, never private or harmful; p99 under 100 ms, stale over unavailable; personalisation and typos as stretch. Does that match?"*

**A common slip:** "It should be fast and accurate." Fast is a number (100 ms p99, keystroke to paint). Accurate is a ranking rule (decayed distinct-user popularity). An adjective in a requirement is a blank you have not filled.

<h2 id="estimation">Chapter 2 · Requests per second</h2>

An old prototype's graph shows the shape of the problem: every search was ten requests. Someone typing "running shoes" asked for "r", "ru", "run", "runn" and so on, and three of those responses arrived after the user had typed further. The system painted them anyway. Three blanks decide the architecture: **keystrokes per second**, **requests per second**, **bytes of index**.

**Assumptions, stated out loud** (each is a number you would check against real logs):

- **100M daily active users**, **5 searches** each per day, so **500M searches/day**.
- **10 keystrokes** per search before the user picks a suggestion or presses Enter.
- **Peak = 3 × average**; a day is about **86,400 s**.

**Keystrokes.** 500M × 10 = **5 billion a day**, about 57,870/s on average, so **about 175,000/s at peak** (57,870 × 3 is about 173,600).

**The client's effect.** Not every keystroke becomes a request. A short **debounce** collapses bursts (fast typing, paste). **Cancelling** superseded requests stops wasted work. A **client cache** answers backspaces and can narrow a short list locally (Chapter 7). Assume these cut **10 keystrokes to about 6 requests** per search. That number is one to measure, not to trust.

- 500M × 6 = 3 billion requests/day, about 34,700/s average, so **about 104,000/s at peak**.
- The **edge** (CDN) answers short, popular prefixes. Assume **40%** of requests hit there (Chapter 8). The origin sees 104,200 × 0.6 = **about 62,500/s at peak**.

**Servers.** An in-memory lookup costs microseconds; the real per-node limit is request handling. Plan on a conservative **10,000 requests/s per node, run at 50%**, so 5,000/s each. 62,500 ÷ 5,000 = 12.5, so **about 13 nodes of capacity worldwide**. If the busiest of three regions carries 40% of the origin peak (25,000/s), it needs 5 nodes plus one spare for failures and deploys: **6**. The conclusion to say aloud: *the fleet is small; the hard parts are latency, freshness and safety, not throughput.*

**Bandwidth.** A response is about 10 suggestions × 25 B plus JSON, so **500 B**. 104,200/s × 500 B is about **52 MB/s, or 420 Mbit/s** worldwide at peak. Say "not a constraint" and move on.

**The index.** It holds only queries that pass the filters (Chapter 6). Assume **10M distinct suggestible queries** per large locale, **about 20 characters** each.

- Query table: 10M × about 32 B (text, a score and an id) is **320 MB**.
- Index structure: Chapter 5 computes about **1.2 GB** as a compressed trie with top-10 lists, so **about 1.5 GB** all in.

**Logs, the pipeline's input.** 500M searches × about 100 B per record (normalised query, hashed user id, time, locale, whether a suggestion was clicked) = **50 GB/day**. A 30-day window is **1.5 TB**: an ordinary batch job.

<h2 id="entities-api">Chapter 3 · The contract</h2>

A POST to `/search/suggest` with a JSON body cannot be helped by any browser cache, CDN or proxy. Every "a" anyone types goes all the way to your servers. The contract should be one the internet can cache.

**Core entities, two minutes, names and relationships:**

- **Query**: a normalised search string in a locale, with a decayed popularity **score**. Built from logs; read-only to the serving path.
- **Prefix**: not stored as its own row in the trie design; it is a path in the tree. (In the key-value design it is the key.)
- **Suggestion list**: the top-K queries for one (locale, prefix). The thing every request reads.
- **Index snapshot**: an immutable, versioned build of all lists for a locale. Serving nodes hold one live and one previous.
- **Trending set**: a few thousand queries per locale whose recent rate spikes above baseline; refreshed every minute.
- **User history**: one user's recent searches; lives on the device (and optionally a per-user store). Never enters the global index.

**The API.** One read endpoint, a `GET`, so every cache on the path can help:

```http
GET /v1/suggest?q=red%20ru&locale=en-US&limit=10

200 OK
Content-Type: application/json
Cache-Control: public, max-age=300, stale-while-revalidate=60, stale-if-error=86400

{
  "q": "red ru",
  "build": "en-US/2026-10-08T03",
  "suggestions": [
    { "text": "red running shoes", "kind": "popular" },
    { "text": "red rug",           "kind": "popular" },
    { "text": "red rum cocktail",  "kind": "trending" }
  ]
}
```

Each piece earns its place:

- **`GET` with the prefix in the URL.** The URL is the cache key at the browser, the CDN and any proxy.
- **`locale` as a parameter, not a header.** `Vary: Accept-Language` splits the cache on every variant of that header ("en-US,en;q=0.9", "en-US,en;q=0.8"). One explicit, normalised parameter keeps the key space small.
- **The client normalises `q` before building the URL**: lowercase, trim, collapse spaces, Unicode NFKC. "Red Ru", "red ru " and "red  ru" then share one cache entry. The server normalises again (never trust the client) and returns an empty list for `q` longer than about 50 characters.
- **`max-age=300`.** Five minutes bounds how stale a trending suggestion can be at the edge; batch lists change only daily. **`stale-while-revalidate`** lets the edge refresh in the background. **`stale-if-error`** keeps serving the last list for a day if the origin is unreachable (both from RFC 5861).
- **`build`** in the body answers "why did I see that?", and lets a client discard a response from an older build.
- **`kind`** lets the UI mark trending or personal items.
- **Personal results are not in this response.** The global list is the same for everyone and cacheable. The user's history is merged on the device, or fetched from a separate `GET /v1/suggest/personal` marked `Cache-Control: private, no-store`. Mixing them would put one person's searches in a shared cache.

**Logging, the other half of the contract.** Ranking needs what people *searched*, not what they typed on the way. The search endpoint (not the suggest endpoint) logs the final query, a hashed user id, the locale, and whether it came from a clicked suggestion. Logging each keystroke would multiply log volume by ten and record mostly half-words.

<Trace title="Write the contract" steps={contract} takeaway="A cacheable GET with a normalised prefix and locale in the URL, a five-minute TTL with stale-if-error, and personal results kept out of shared caches." />

**A common slip:** an API that cannot be cached: a `POST`, a session token in the URL, the user's id in the global response. Each silently deletes your cheapest layer.

<h2 id="high-level">Chapter 4 · Two clocks</h2>

A tempting first sketch is one box, SUGGEST SERVICE, with an arrow to the search logs: it reads the logs, counts, and returns the top ten. Ask how many log records it reads for one keystroke. The answer erases the arrow. Draw a line down the middle instead, with **every keystroke** on the left and **every night** on the right.

**The design, one requirement at a time.** First the reader: a request arrives, and these are the fewest boxes that answer it within 100 ms.

<Figure caption="The high-level design. Left: the online path, on the keystroke clock. Right: the offline path, on the nightly clock (plus a one-minute trending loop). The only thing that crosses the line is an immutable snapshot.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    subgraph ON["Online · every keystroke"]

      E -->|"2 · miss"| LB["Regional LB"]
      LB --> S["Suggest service × N\nwhole index in memory\n+ trending set"]

> **Say it:** "A keystroke: the client waits for a short pause, cancels anything in flight, and checks its local cache. If it needs the server, it sends a GET with the normalised prefix and locale. Short popular prefixes are answered at the CDN edge. Misses go to the nearest region, through a load balancer, to any suggest-service node. Each node holds the **whole index in memory**, so it walks the trie to the prefix and returns the stored top-K list, merged with any trending items: microseconds of work. The client paints it only if the prefix still matches the box."
>
> "Every night a batch job reads the search logs, computes decayed counts per query, drops anything searched by too few distinct users or matching safety filters, and builds the top-K index. It writes a versioned snapshot; each node loads it beside the old one and swaps a pointer. A small streaming job runs every minute for trending queries."

**Every box earns one sentence:**

- **Client logic**: the cheapest requests are the ones never sent, and only the client knows which response is still relevant.
- **CDN edge**: a small set of short prefixes carries a large share of requests, and their answers are the same for everyone.
- **Suggest service, whole index per node**: 1.5 GB fits in memory, so any node answers any prefix with no network hop behind it.
- **Batch pipeline**: ranking is too expensive for the keystroke path and is allowed to be a day old.
- **Snapshot store**: the hand-off between clocks is an immutable file, easy to validate, canary and roll back.
- **Trending job**: the one requirement the nightly clock cannot meet (minutes, not a day).

**What is deliberately missing:** no database on the read path, no search cluster on the read path, and no cache in front of the suggest service (the service is itself an in-memory copy of the snapshot). Say why each is missing.

<Reveal q="Why not just keep the search cluster and add a cache in front of it?">

The cache would miss on most long prefixes, and every miss is a scatter-gather search query across shards with a long tail, which is the 380 ms from the prologue. You would be caching your way around the wrong machine. The search index answers "which documents match these words"; typeahead asks "which popular past queries start with these characters". A precomputed index answers the second in microseconds, and the search cluster goes back to serving the searches people press Enter on.

</Reveal>

**A common slip:** drawing one box, "suggest service", with an arrow to "the database". The interviewer will ask what query it runs and how long it takes, and there is no good answer. The offline/online split is the design.

<h2 id="data-model">Chapter 5 · Tries and prefix indexes</h2>

Say a trie holds a few hundred thousand queries below the letter "c", and somebody types "c" about a thousand times a second. Either the server visits all of them every time, or the "c" node already knows the answer. Precomputing that answer is the whole idea.

Three machines can answer "top K completions of this prefix". Know all three, commit to one, and say when you would switch.

**Option 1 · Trie with top-K stored at every node.** A **trie** (prefix tree) stores strings so that every path from the root spells a prefix. Walking the typed characters lands on the prefix's node, and every query below it is a completion. On its own that is not enough, since the subtree under "c" is enormous. So **every node stores its own top-K list, precomputed**. A lookup walks one hop per typed character and returns the stored list: **O(prefix length + K)**, independent of how many queries lie below.

<Figure caption="k = 3. Each node stores the best three of its whole subtree, computed bottom-up. suggest('ca') reads [car, cat, catalog] from the 'ca' node without visiting anything below it. 'cab' and 'cars' have no node, so they return an empty list; the empty prefix returns the root’s list.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart TD

**Building the lists bottom-up.** A node's top K is the best K of **its own query (if one ends there) plus its children's top-K lists**. Why is that enough? Take any query outside a child's top K. K other queries in that same child beat it, and all K are candidates at the parent, so it can never make the parent's top K either. One post-order pass builds every list.

**Break ties deterministically**: score descending, then alphabetical. Otherwise two replicas built from the same data can order equal scores differently, and the list flickers between keystrokes as requests land on different nodes.

```java id=typeahead group=typeahead
/** An immutable prefix tree. Every node stores the best k completions of its whole subtree. */
static final class TopKTrie {
    private static final class Node {
        final Map<Character, Node> children = new HashMap<>();   // any alphabet: Latin, accents, CJK
        boolean isQuery;                                          // a whole query ends here
        String[] top;                                             // best k in this subtree, best first
    }

    private final Node root = new Node();
    private final int k;

    /** scores: normalised query -> decayed popularity, already filtered by the offline pipeline. */
    TopKTrie(Map<String, Long> scores, int k) {
        if (k < 1) throw new IllegalArgumentException("k must be positive");
        this.k = k;
        for (String q : scores.keySet()) {
            if (q.isEmpty()) continue;                            // an empty query is never a suggestion
            Node node = root;
            for (char c : q.toCharArray()) node = node.children.computeIfAbsent(c, x -> new Node());
            node.isQuery = true;
        }
        // Higher score first; ties broken a..z, so every replica returns exactly the same list.
        Comparator<String> better = Comparator.<String>comparingLong(scores::get).reversed()
                                              .thenComparing(Comparator.naturalOrder());
        build(root, "", better);
    }

    /** Bottom-up: a node's top k is the best k of (its own query + its children's top-k lists). */
    private void build(Node node, String path, Comparator<String> better) {
        List<String> candidates = new ArrayList<>();
        if (node.isQuery) candidates.add(path);
        for (Map.Entry<Character, Node> child : node.children.entrySet()) {
            build(child.getValue(), path + child.getKey(), better);   // children first (post-order)
            candidates.addAll(Arrays.asList(child.getValue().top));
        }
        candidates.sort(better);
        node.top = candidates.subList(0, Math.min(k, candidates.size())).toArray(new String[0]);
    }

    /** One hop per typed character, then read the stored list: O(prefix length + k). */
    List<String> suggest(String prefix) {
        Node node = root;
        for (char c : prefix.toCharArray()) {
            node = node.children.get(c);
            if (node == null) return List.of();                   // nobody searched anything starting so
        }
        return List.of(node.top);                                 // immutable: callers can't disturb it
    }
}
```

**What the checker proves.** `tests/java/typeahead/Check.java` compares `suggest` with a brute-force oracle (filter every query by the prefix, sort by score descending then alphabetically, take K) on 3,000 random query sets over small alphabets, so ties and shared prefixes are everywhere. It covers every prefix up to length 4, the **empty prefix** (the root's list), **missing prefixes** (an empty list, including one that runs past a leaf), accented and CJK characters, and the exact lists in the figure above.

**Cost.** Build: every node hands at most K candidates to its parent, so about **nodes × K** items are sorted in total, in small batches. Query: **O(P + K)** for a prefix of length P. Memory: **K entries per node**, the price of never searching at read time.

**A bug that looks right and suggests nothing:**

}

On the figure's data every list comes out empty, even the root's. The checker builds exactly this version and shows it. A milder cousin forgets the node's own query only at nodes with children, and "car" (900) silently disappears from every list while "card" and "care" remain.

**Worked numbers: does a trie fit?** 10M queries × about 20 characters:

- **Plain trie:** at most one node per character, so **≤ 200M nodes**. Storing each top-10 list as 4-byte query ids (40 B) plus about 20 B of node structure in a compact array layout is about 60 B per node: **up to 12 GB**.
- **Compressed (radix) trie:** merge chains of single-child nodes into one node with a multi-character edge. Every remaining node either ends a query (at most 10M) or branches (fewer than the number of queries), so **≤ 20M nodes**, and 20M × 60 B = **1.2 GB**. A prefix that stops mid-edge ("cata") uses the list of the node at the end of that edge: below a single-child chain the subtree is the same, so the list is the same. Add the 320 MB query table for **about 1.5 GB**. It fits on every node.
- The Java above is the readable version. A `HashMap` per node and a `String[]` per list cost many times more memory. The offline builder writes the compact format and the serving node memory-maps it.

**Option 2 · Prefix → top-K table in a key-value store.** Store one row per prefix: key `sugg:en-US:red ru`, value the top-K list. A lookup is one read, sharding is trivial (hash the key), and you can run it on Redis or a Dynamo-style store instead of custom servers. In Redis a sorted set per prefix is a natural fit:

```text
ZADD sugg:en-US:ca 900 car 700 cat 500 catalog   # written by the build, one set per prefix
ZREVRANGE sugg:en-US:ca 0 9 WITHSCORES            # the read: top 10 for "ca"
```

The cost is storage, because every prefix repeats its list:

- Distinct prefixes: up to 10M × 20 = **200M rows**. With the list stored as strings (10 × about 20 B) plus a key of about 20 B, that is about 220 B per row, **up to 44 GB**.
- Cap prefixes at **10 characters** (a longer prefix falls back to its 10-character list filtered on the server, which may give fewer than K results, or to search): at most **100M rows × 220 B = 22 GB**, plus the store's per-key overhead (often tens of bytes per key; measure it).
- A network hop on every miss, about 0.5 to 1 ms in region. Fine within the budget, but a hop the trie does not have.

**Option 3 · Edge n-grams in the search index.** Index "lamp" as `l, la, lam, lamp` so a prefix becomes an ordinary term (the n-gram analyzer runs at index time only; Search, Chapter 9). This is the right machine for **completion over documents with filters**, such as "my listings, in this category". It is the wrong one for global popular queries: a search query, with search latency and fan-out, on every keystroke.

| | Trie + top-K per node | Prefix → top-K KV table | Edge n-grams in search |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lookup | O(prefix) in local memory, microseconds | One read, about 1 ms in region | A search query, 10+ ms with tail |
| Memory (10M queries) | About 1.5 GB compressed | Up to 22 GB (10-char cap) | Index grows several × for that field |
| Sharding | Usually unnecessary; by locale or range | Hash the prefix | The search cluster's own |
| Freshness | Rebuild and swap | Rewrite changed rows | Near-real-time indexing |
| Best for | **Global popular-query suggestions** | The same data, if you would rather run a KV store | **Documents with filters** |

**Trie versus "prefix index" in plain words.** A plain database index (`LIKE 'ca%'` on a B-tree) can find every match quickly, but "which ten are best" still needs a read and sort of every match. A trie with stored lists, or a KV row per prefix, answers *top K* directly. The difference is not how you find the prefix. It is whether the ranking was already done.

**Commit:** *"A compressed trie with top-10 per node, built offline, held whole in memory on every serving node: about 1.5 GB. If we did not want to own custom servers, the same lists go into a KV table at about 22 GB. For 'search my own listings' I would use edge n-grams in the search index instead."*

<Trace title="Pick the index" steps={indexChoice} takeaway="Popular queries: precomputed top-K, in a trie (about 1.5 GB) or a prefix table (about 22 GB). Documents with filters: edge n-grams. Bottom-up merge works because a child’s non-top-K query is beaten by K others in that child." />

<Reveal q="Why store K for every node instead of only for short prefixes?">

You could: long prefixes have small subtrees, so scanning them at query time is cheap, and some designs store lists only down to a depth. But in a compressed trie the deep nodes are few (at most about two per query) and their lists are already cheap, and a uniform "every node has a list" keeps the read path branch-free and its latency flat. Mention the depth cap as the memory lever if the index grows. Do not start with it.

</Reveal>

**A common slip:** "Walk the subtree and sort by count." The output is right and the cost is wrong: for "c" or "s" it visits hundreds of thousands of nodes per keystroke. The precomputed list is the whole point.

<h2 id="pipeline">Chapter 6 · The nightly build</h2>

A first ranking query is usually `GROUP BY query ORDER BY count DESC`. Count of what? Forty searches by one person for a neighbour's phone number is forty. A song that was number one in 2019 is four million, forever. The index is only as good as the job that builds it.

The offline pipeline has five stages, run on a schedule:

<Figure caption="The nightly build. Each stage can stop a bad build before it reaches a single user; the last stage is a pointer swap, so rolling back is a pointer swap too.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    L[["Search logs\n50 GB/day"]] --> A["1 · Aggregate\nper locale, query:\ndistinct users, decayed score"]
    A --> F["2 · Filter\nk distinct users, PII patterns,\nblocklist, bot traffic"]
    F --> B["3 · Build\ntop-K bottom-up,\ncompact snapshot"]
    B --> V["4 · Validate\nsize diff, golden prefixes,\ncanary node"]
    V --> SW["5 · Swap\nload beside old,\nflip pointer"]`} />
</Figure>

**1 · Aggregate with time decay.** Group yesterday's logs by (locale, normalised query): count searches and **distinct users** (hashed ids; HyperLogLog if exact distinct counts get expensive). Then fold the day into a running score with exponential decay, so recent interest counts more:

```text
decay  = 2^(−1 / halfLifeDays)                 half-life 7 days → 0.906 per day
score  = score_yesterday × decay + count_today  one day of logs per run, not 28
```

```python
HALF_LIFE_DAYS = 7
DECAY = 2 ** (-1 / HALF_LIFE_DAYS)          # 0.906

def fold_day(scores: dict[str, float], today: dict[str, int]) -> dict[str, float]:
    out = {q: s * DECAY for q, s in scores.items()}
    for q, n in today.items():
        out[q] = out.get(q, 0.0) + n
    return {q: s for q, s in out.items() if s >= 1.0}   # drop dead queries so the table stays bounded
```

**Worked numbers** (half-life 7 days). Query A was searched 1,000 times every day for 28 days; query B 7,000 times, all today. A = 1,000 × (1 + 0.906 + 0.906² + … 28 terms) ≈ **9,944**. B = **7,000**. A still ranks higher tonight: a steady favourite beats one day's spike. Tomorrow, if B gets no more searches, it decays to 7,000 × 0.906 ≈ 6,340. If you want B in the box *today*, that is the trending layer's job (Chapter 10), not the batch's. The half-life is a product knob: shorter for news-like domains, longer for a catalogue.

**2 · Filter, the stage the first version skipped.**

- **k distinct users.** A query is suggestible only if at least *k* distinct users searched it in the window (say k = 20; a policy choice). One person's private search can never reach someone else's box, and one account or bot cannot push a phrase up by repetition. This is the core privacy rule: count users, not searches.
- **PII patterns.** Drop anything that looks like an email, phone number, card number, street address or long id, even above the threshold.
- **Safety blocklist.** Slurs, sexual and violent terms, and "sensitive completions": a person's name followed by an accusation is not a slur and still must not be suggested. Usually a list plus a classifier.
- **Bot traffic.** Exclude sessions the abuse systems flagged before counting, or coordinated campaigns can buy a suggestion.
- **Legal removals.** A maintained denylist, applied here *and* at serve time (Chapter 12).

**3 · Build.** Sort the surviving queries, build the compressed trie and its top-K lists bottom-up (Chapter 5), and serialise to a compact, read-only file per locale. Store a few more than you show (say 15 for a K of 10) so serve-time filters can remove an item and still return a full list. Write it to the snapshot store under a version such as `en-US/2026-10-08T03`, with a checksum.

**4 · Validate.** A build is code you did not review. Before it serves anyone:

- **Size diff**: node count and file size within a few percent of yesterday's.
- **Golden prefixes**: a few hundred fixed prefixes must return mostly the same lists as yesterday's build. A big diff on "k" stops the rollout for a human.
- **Canary**: one node loads it and serves a small slice of traffic; watch errors and suggestion click-through rate.

**5 · Swap.** Each serving node downloads the snapshot, loads (or memory-maps) it **beside the current one**, runs a smoke test and flips one reference. Readers already in flight finish on the old snapshot, new requests see the new one, nothing is locked, and no request sees half of each.

```java id=swap group=typeahead
/** Serving nodes read one whole snapshot at a time; a new build goes live with one reference swap. */
static final class SuggestIndex {
    private final AtomicReference<TopKTrie> live;

    SuggestIndex(TopKTrie first) { live = new AtomicReference<>(first); }

    List<String> suggest(String prefix) { return live.get().suggest(prefix); }

    /** Load and validate the next snapshot beside the old one, then flip. Keep the old one: rollback is another swap. */
    TopKTrie swap(TopKTrie next) { return live.getAndSet(next); }
}
```

The checker races four reader threads against 20,000 swaps between two different builds and asserts every answer equals one whole build's answer, never a mix. It works because each `TopKTrie` is **immutable after construction** and is published through the atomic reference. **Memory note:** at the moment of the swap a node holds two snapshots, so budget **2 × 1.5 GB = 3 GB** for the index.

**How often?** **Daily** is the default; **hourly** if the domain moves fast (news, a marketplace with drops). The run is cheap (one day of logs folded into running scores, then a build), so the limit is usually validation and rollout time, not compute. Faster than hourly is the wrong tool: that is what the trending layer is for.

<h2 id="latency">Chapter 7 · The latency budget</h2>

A common proposal is "servers are expensive, so wait until people stop typing: 200 ms of quiet, then ask." Write **100** on the board, and under it **200**. The debounce alone is twice the budget.

**The budget, keystroke to paint, at p99.** Every line is an assumption you would say out loud:

| Step | p99 budget | Why that number |
|---|---|---|
| Client debounce | 30 ms | Short: it spends the user's time |
| Network round trip, phone to nearest edge | 20 ms | A good connection to a nearby point of presence |
| Edge to regional origin (misses only) | 20 ms | Same continent |
| Load balancer + suggest service | 5 ms | Microsecond lookup; the rest is request handling |
| Render | 10 ms | Paint a short list |
| Headroom for jitter | 15 ms | It is p99, not the average |
| **Total** | **100 ms** | An edge hit skips the origin hop: about **60 ms** |

**Read the table backwards and the design falls out:**

- A cross-ocean round trip is often 100 to 200 ms by itself, so **serve from every region** and put short prefixes **at the edge**.
- A database query or a search scatter-gather has a multi-millisecond median and a long tail, so **precompute and keep it in memory**: one lookup, no fan-out.
- The origin gets 5 ms of 100. **Most of the latency belongs to the network and the client**, so most of the engineering goes there.

**Be honest about mobile.** On a congested mobile network the radio alone can take 100 ms at p99, and you cannot fix physics. Measure two things: **origin p99 under 10 ms** (yours to keep) and **perceived p99 per network class** (yours to improve). Make the client hide the rest.

**The client half of the system:**

- **Debounce, briefly.** Wait for a short pause (here 30 ms) before sending, so bursts of fast typing and pastes collapse into one request. In typeahead, debounce is a latency knob first and a load knob second.
- **Cancel and ignore stale responses.** Abort the previous in-flight request when a new one starts, and paint a response only if its `q` still matches the box. Without this, a slow response for "la" can arrive after "lamp" and overwrite the right list.
- **Show something instantly from the local cache.** Keep recent prefix → list in memory. Backspacing from "lamp" to "lam" costs nothing. Typing "cat" after "ca": filter "ca"'s list to entries starting with "cat" and paint that at once, then replace it when the real answer arrives. If "ca"'s list was shorter than K, it already held *every* completion, so the filtered list is exact and no request is needed.
- **Prefetch carefully.** Some clients request the empty-prefix list (global top K, or the user's history) when the box gets focus, so the first paint is instant.

```js
let timer, inflight;
const cache = new Map();                       // normalised prefix -> list

function onInput(box) {
  const q = box.value.trim().toLowerCase().normalize('NFKC');
  clearTimeout(timer);                         // debounce: restart the wait on every keystroke
  timer = setTimeout(() => fetchSuggestions(q, box), 30);
}

async function fetchSuggestions(q, box) {
  if (cache.has(q)) return paint(cache.get(q));
  inflight?.abort();                           // cancel the superseded request
  inflight = new AbortController();
  const res = await fetch(`/v1/suggest?q=${encodeURIComponent(q)}&locale=en-US`, { signal: inflight.signal });
  const body = await res.json();
  cache.set(q, body.suggestions);
  if (box.value.trim().toLowerCase().normalize('NFKC') === q) paint(body.suggestions);  // ignore stale answers
}
```

**Debounce versus throttle**, two ideas that look alike:

| | Debounce | Throttle |
|---|---|---|
| Rule | Wait for N ms of quiet, then fire once | Fire at most once every N ms while input continues |
| During fast typing | Fires only when typing pauses | Fires steadily every N ms |
| Typeahead fit | The default: short wait, then one request for the latest text | Useful when a user types continuously and you still want periodic updates |

<h2 id="edge">Chapter 8 · Caching hot prefixes</h2>

Sort a day of suggest-service logs by prefix and the top of the list is short: "s", "a", "c", "m", "the". Each was asked hundreds of millions of times and got almost the same answer every time. Recomputing "a" four hundred million times a day, when it changed twice, is waste.

**Count the short prefixes.** With a 26-letter alphabet there are 26 one-character, 676 two-character and 17,576 three-character prefixes: **18,278** in all, per locale. Every search passes through them as it is typed, and their answers are the same for everyone. Caching just those at the CDN edge costs about 18,278 × 500 B = **9 MB per locale per edge location**, and under this chapter's assumption takes about **40%** of requests off the origin. That share depends on how long a debounced request's prefix usually is, so measure it from your own logs.

**The four promises, for this cache** (from the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/caching/`}>Caching lesson</a>):

1. **Key.** The URL: `/v1/suggest?q=<normalised prefix>&locale=<locale>&limit=10`. Client normalisation (lowercase, trim, NFKC) and a fixed parameter order keep "Ca", "ca " and `locale=en-US&q=ca` from becoming separate entries. Longer prefixes can pass through uncached, or be cached with a lower hit ratio; either is fine.
2. **TTL and why.** **300 seconds**: batch lists change daily, but trending items change by the minute, and five minutes is the staleness accepted for them at the edge. `stale-while-revalidate=60` lets the edge refresh in the background so no user waits on an expiry.
3. **Invalidation.** None for normal builds: the TTL rolls the new build out within five minutes. For an **urgent removal**, purge the affected prefixes (every prefix of the removed query, up to the cached length) through the CDN's purge API.
4. **Failure plan.** An edge miss falls through to the origin, which holds everything in memory, so nothing behind it can stampede. If the origin is unreachable, `stale-if-error=86400` serves the last list for up to a day. If the edge itself fails, the origin absorbs 100% of requests by scaling out stateless replicas (62,500/s becomes about 104,000/s at peak: about 21 nodes of capacity instead of 13).

**Never at the edge:** anything personal. The edge key has no user in it, by design.

**Three layers, different jobs.** Each one saves a different thing:

| Layer | Saves | Hit when | Cannot hold |
|---|---|---|---|
| Client cache | A whole network round trip | Backspace, or narrowing a short list | Anything the user has not typed yet |
| CDN edge | The trip to the origin region | Another user asked the same short prefix recently | Personal results |
| In-process index at the service | The remote database or Redis read | Always (it is the data) | More than fits in node memory |

A Redis cache between the service and its own in-memory index has no row in that table because it would be slower than the lookup it protects.

<Reveal q="Why not cache every prefix at the edge, not just short ones?">

You can, and the hit ratio rises a little. But long prefixes are a long tail: millions of distinct keys, each asked rarely, so most are evicted before a second request arrives. Short prefixes are the opposite: few keys, each asked constantly. Caching is worth it when the same key repeats, and prefix length is a cheap proxy for that. A CDN with enough space will simply cache whatever it sees with the TTL you set. The point is not to *count* on the tail.

</Reveal>

**A common slip:** "Cache popular prefixes in Redis." In front of what? The service already holds the whole index in memory, and a Redis hop would be *slower* than the lookup it protects. The caches that pay are the ones that save a network round trip: the edge and the client.

<h2 id="sharding">Chapter 9 · Sharding the trie</h2>

A common first answer is to shard by first letter: twenty-six shards, very tidy. Then draw the traffic on top: a skyscraper over "s", "c" and "m", a few sheds over "q", "x" and "z". Every search starts by asking one of those shards, and the popular letters carry far more load.

**First question: does it need sharding at all?** The index is about **1.5 GB**. A serving node has tens of gigabytes of memory. So **replicate the whole index on every node**:

- **No routing**: any node answers any prefix; the load balancer can pick any of them.
- **No hot shard**: "s" traffic spreads across every node like any other.
- **No data loss on failure**: a dead node is one fewer copy, not a missing range.
- **The cluster exists for throughput and availability, not capacity.** Thirteen nodes at peak because of requests per second, not bytes.

**When it stops fitting.** Imagine years later: 40 locales, longer histories, top-30 lists for personalisation (Chapter 11), and 300 GB. Then shard, in this order:

1. **By locale.** A natural partition: a query never crosses locales, and each locale is a separate snapshot anyway. Small locales share nodes; a big one gets its own.
2. **By prefix range, cut by traffic, not by alphabet.** If one locale outgrows a node, split its key space into contiguous ranges (`[a, ca)`, `[ca, cl)`, `[cl, e)`, …) chosen so each carries a similar share of **requests**, not letters. "s" might need three ranges, and "q–z" might share one. A range router (a small, replicated map from range to node group) sends each prefix to its range. This is range partitioning from the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-partitioning/#ranges`}>Partitioning lesson</a>, with its usual cost: ranges must be rebalanced as traffic shifts.
3. **Replicate hot ranges more.** Throughput skew is handled with replicas, not more splitting: the "s" ranges get more copies than "x".

**The subtle part: short prefixes that span ranges.** If "c" is split into `[ca, cl)` and `[cl, d)`, no single shard holds the answer for "c". Two exact options:

- **Scatter to the shards that overlap the prefix and merge their top-K lists.** Exact for the same reason the bottom-up build is: any query outside a shard's top K is beaten by K queries on that shard.
- **Or precompute the short-prefix lists globally.** There are only 18,278 prefixes of up to three characters, so replicate that small table on every node. If range boundaries are cut within the first three characters, short prefixes never fan out and longer ones always hit exactly one range.

**Why not hash?** Hashing whole queries scatters a prefix's completions across every shard, so each lookup becomes a scatter-gather. Hashing *prefixes* works only if each prefix is its own key with its own list. That is the KV-table design (Option 2), where it is the natural choice. Its hot keys ("s", "a") are then handled by the edge and an in-process cache, exactly the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/caching/#hot-keys`}>celebrity-key fix</a>.

| Question | Replicate everything | Shard by range |
|---|---|---|
| Fits in one node's memory? | Yes: choose this | No: choose this |
| Routing needed? | No | Yes, a small range map |
| Hot prefix like "s"? | Spread over all nodes | More replicas for that range |
| Short prefixes | Local | Precomputed everywhere, or scatter and merge |
| Node loss | One fewer copy | One fewer replica of a range |

<h2 id="trending">Chapter 10 · Batch versus streaming</h2>

A storm named Odile makes landfall at 14:05. By 14:10 a hundred thousand people have typed "od" and been offered "odor eliminator" and "odd socks". The nightly build ran at 03:00 and the next is thirteen hours away. How do new counts reach the index?

**Two ways to update counts, and what differs:**

| | Batch rebuild | Streaming in-place patch |
|---|---|---|
| How | Recompute all scores, rebuild every list, swap | Bump the changed query's score, patch lists on its own prefixes |
| Latency to appear | Hours (the build interval) | Seconds to minutes |
| Score goes up | Exact | Exact: the new list is the top K of the old list plus that query |
| Score goes down (decay, removal) | Exact | **Can be wrong**: the rightful replacement may never have been in the list |
| Filters and validation | Run on every build | Easy to skip; abuse goes in first |
| Write load | None on the read path | Writes to every prefix list on every replica, under read load |
| Rollback | Swap back | Hard: state was mutated in place |

The widget in Chapter 5 shows this. Scroll back to it, pick "car", choose **Streaming**, and press -500. The list for "c" now holds "car" at 100 while "card" (400) was never in it, so the patched list is wrong and only a rebuild fixes it. Decay makes *every* count go down continuously, so a live-patched index drifts from the truth.

**So the design is both, with a small streaming layer beside the big batch index.** The trending layer:

1. **Stream** the same search events (from the search endpoint's log) into a stream processor.
2. **Count recent queries** per locale in short windows, say one-minute buckets over the last hour. To keep memory small for millions of distinct strings, use a **count-min sketch** for counts and a **heap of current leaders** (heavy hitters), as in <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-timeseries/#top-k`}>Time-series · top-K</a> and its <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-timeseries/#sketch-trending`}>trending sketch</a>.
3. **Score "trending" as surprise, not volume**: recent rate divided by the query's expected rate (its batch score, scaled to an hour). "weather" is always popular; "odile" going from nothing to thousands per minute is trending.
4. **Apply the same filters** (distinct users, PII patterns, blocklist) with a *higher* distinct-user bar, because fast paths are where abuse goes first.
5. **Publish** the top few thousand trending queries per locale to the serving nodes every minute. It is small (a few hundred KB), so every node holds it whole.

**Merging at query time.** For prefix "od", the node reads the batch list from the trie and the trending candidates that start with "od" (a tiny trie over the trending set, rebuilt every minute). Then:

```python
def merge(batch: list[str], trending: list[str], k: int = 10, max_fresh: int = 2) -> list[dict]:
    fresh = [t for t in trending if t not in batch][:max_fresh]      # dedupe, cap the slots
    base = batch[: k - len(fresh)]
    return [{"text": q, "kind": "popular"} for q in base] + [{"text": q, "kind": "trending"} for q in fresh]
```

- **De-duplicate** (a trending query may already be in the batch list).
- **Reserve at most 1–2 of the K slots** for trending items above a threshold, so freshness always gets a place and a burst of noise cannot take the whole list.
- **Mark them** (`"kind": "trending"`) so the UI can show it.

The edge's 300-second TTL means a new trend reaches short prefixes within about five minutes, inside the requirement.

    ST --> FL["Filters\nstricter for fast path"]
    FL -->|"every minute\nfew thousand queries"| TS["Trending set\non every node"]
    SNAP[("Nightly snapshot")] --> TRIE["Top-K trie"]

<Reveal q="Why not update the trie's counts live, on every search?">

Three reasons. **Cost and contention**: every search would write to the lists of every prefix of its query, on every replica, including the hottest nodes ("s", "a") under full read load. **Correctness**: a top-K list can absorb a count going *up*, but not one going *down*, because the rightful replacement might be a query that was never in the list and the node cannot know which without scanning its subtree. **Safety**: live updates skip the filters and validation, so a coordinated campaign would be in the box within seconds. The big index is rebuilt and swapped, and only a tiny, filtered set moves fast.

</Reveal>

**A common slip:** "Rebuild the index every minute." A full build, validation and rollout each minute turns your safest process into your riskiest. Keep the big thing slow and the fast thing small.

<h2 id="personal">Chapter 11 · Personalisation</h2>

A user types "ru" and gets "running shoes", "rugby", "russian dictionary", though they have searched "rubber plant care" every week for a month. Suggestions should know that, without telling anyone else.

**Personalisation, in increasing cost:**

1. **On-device history (default).** The client keeps the user's recent searches locally. When the user types, it filters its own history by the prefix and shows matches first (often with a clock icon), then the global list from the server, de-duplicated. **Zero server cost, private by construction, works offline, and the global response stays cacheable at the edge.** For signed-in users across devices, sync the history from a per-user store, fetched once per session, not per keystroke.
2. **Context without identity.** Location and language change rankings for everyone in a region ("football" means different things in Leeds and Dallas). Use **regional variants of the index**, built per (locale, country) where the difference is worth it. Still global per region, still cacheable.
3. **A thin server-side re-ranker (stretch).** Store a longer candidate list per node (say top 30), fetch it, and re-rank with a small model using the user's features (recent categories, past clicks) from a per-user feature store. It must fit the roughly 5 ms server slice, so it is a light scoring function, not a heavy model. Its response is **private** (`Cache-Control: private, no-store`), so it bypasses the edge and personalised requests cost origin capacity. Memory: 30 ids per node instead of 10, so 20M nodes × (120 + 20) B is about **2.8 GB**; it still fits and is still replicated.

| | On-device merge | Regional variant | Server re-ranker |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where personal data lives | Device only | None (not personal) | Per-user store |
| Edge-cacheable? | Yes (global part) | Yes, per region | No |
| Extra origin cost | None | Index per region | Every request |
| Use when | Default | Location changes meaning | Click data proves the lift |

**Commit:** *"History on the device, merged on the client; regional index variants for location; a server re-ranker only if click-through data shows it is worth losing edge caching for signed-in users."*

**Many languages:**

- **One index per locale**, built from that locale's logs; the locale comes from the app setting, not the IP address.
- **Normalise the match key, keep the display string.** Case-fold, NFKC-normalise and, per locale, fold accents so "cafe" finds "café" in French (but not where accents change meaning, a per-language decision). Store the original for display.
- **Characters, not bytes.** The trie's alphabet is whatever characters appear (the Java uses a map per node, not a 26-slot array). A 26-letter array is a DSA convenience, not a production choice.
- **Input methods (Chinese, Japanese, Korean).** Users compose characters through an IME, and the text box holds romanised input (pinyin, romaji) until a character is committed. Do not query on every composition event: wait for committed text, or **index transliterations as extra keys** pointing to the same suggestion, so "beij" can suggest 北京. Languages without spaces also need word segmentation in aggregation.
- **Right-to-left scripts** affect rendering, not the index: the prefix is still the logical start of the string.

<Reveal q="A user deletes a search from their history. What has to happen?">

On the device, remove it from local history immediately. In the per-user store, delete it and let other devices sync. In the **global** index, nothing: one user's search never entered it unless at least *k* distinct users searched the same thing, and then it is not that user's search any more. That separation is why the privacy rule lives in the pipeline: deleting personal data never requires rebuilding the global index.

</Reveal>

**A common slip:** personalising on the server for everyone, then caching the response at the edge. Either the edge serves one person's history to strangers, or you turn caching off and origin load jumps by the 40% the edge was taking.

<h2 id="failure">Chapter 12 · When things break</h2>

Imagine the nightly build fails three nights in a row because of a schema change upstream in the log format. Nobody notices for three days. Click-through and latency dashboards are flat. A three-day-old index was served to a hundred million people, and the only problem is that nobody knew.

**Degrade in steps, and make the worst case silent.** Typeahead is an aid. An empty dropdown is acceptable and an error in the search box is not.

| What fails | What the user sees | What the system does |
|---|---|---|
| **Nightly pipeline** | Nothing: yesterday's suggestions | Keep serving the last good snapshot. **Alert on index age** (over 36 hours) |
| **Bad build** (garbage, spam, offensive) | Nothing, if caught | Validation gates and a canary stop it; if it got out, **roll back by swapping to the previous snapshot**, kept loaded for this |
| **Trending job** | Slightly less fresh lists | Serve batch-only; alert |
| **One serving node** | Nothing | Health checks remove it; every other node has the whole index |
| **A whole region** | Slower suggestions | Edge keeps serving cached short prefixes (`stale-if-error`); DNS or anycast fails over at higher latency, maybe over 100 ms, still useful |
| **Overload** | Fewer or no suggestions | Shed load: return an empty list fast rather than queueing; the client stops asking for a few seconds after errors |
| **Urgent removal** | The item disappears in minutes | Serve-time denylist + CDN purge (Chapters 6 and 8); the next build drops it |
| **Coordinated manipulation** | Nothing, if caught | Distinct-user threshold, bot filtering, stricter trending thresholds, golden-prefix diffs |

**Client side:** a request timeout of about 200 ms (after that the answer is too late to matter) and no retries on timeout, since the next keystroke is the retry. On errors, back off for a few seconds and keep showing history-only suggestions.

**Serve the stale index on purpose.** Because nobody can tell a one-day-old list from a fresh one, the system's most common failure (the pipeline) is invisible to users. That is the design working. The only thing to add is the **index-age alert**, so "invisible to users" does not become "invisible to you".

<h2 id="wrap-up">Chapter 13 · Wrap-up</h2>

An SRE reading a design reads it from the bottom up: what pages me first, and what would you build next. The last three minutes should answer both.

1. **Recap against the requirements.** "Top 10 completions per keystroke from a precomputed top-K trie, about 1.5 GB, replicated on every node in every region; short prefixes cached at the edge for five minutes; a nightly pipeline with decay and a k-distinct-users privacy filter; a one-minute trending layer with capped slots; history merged on the device. About 60 ms on an edge hit, under 100 ms at p99 otherwise."
2. **Bottleneck at 10× (about 1M requests/s at the edge).** Origin throughput first: stateless replicas scale linearly, so it is a cost question. Then index size if locales and personalisation grow: shard by locale, then traffic-balanced ranges. Then pipeline run time: incremental decay keeps it to one day of logs per run.
3. **Failure modes.** Pipeline down gives a stale index and an index-age alert. A bad build gives gates, canary and one-swap rollback. A region down gives edge `stale-if-error` and failover.
4. **Monitor.** p99 keystroke-to-paint per region and network class; origin p99; edge hit ratio; **index age**; suggestion **click-through rate** (the product metric; falling CTR is the first sign of a bad build); empty-list rate by prefix length; denylist hits; trending false positives reported.
5. **What I would build next.** Typo-tolerant suggestions (a fuzzy match over the trie for the last word, or spelling correction feeding the prefix); learning-to-rank on clicks instead of raw popularity; a server re-ranker for signed-in users if CTR justifies it; per-country index variants.

**The one-breath version:** *"At 10× it is replica count and then index size: shard by locale, then traffic-balanced ranges. I would page on index age, origin p99 and a drop in suggestion click-through."*

### Five follow-ups you will get

<Reveal q="1 · Why not just run a prefix query on the database or the search cluster?">

`LIKE 'ca%'` with an index finds matching rows, but ranking them means reading every match (for "c", a huge set) on every keystroke. A search cluster does a scatter-gather with a long tail, which the first version measured at 380 ms p99, and it steals capacity from real searches. Both answer "which records match" at query time. Typeahead needs "which K are best", and that is cheapest to compute once, offline, and store at every prefix.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="2 · How do suggestions stay fresh?">

Two paths. The batch index rebuilds daily (hourly if needed) with decayed scores, `score × 0.906 + today` for a 7-day half-life, so recent interest outweighs old. For minutes-level freshness, a streaming job counts the last hour in one-minute windows with a count-min sketch and heavy hitters, scores queries by surprise against their baseline, filters them strictly, and pushes a small trending set to every node each minute, merged into at most two of the ten slots. The big index is never updated live.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="3 · How do you stop private or offensive suggestions?">

In the pipeline, before the build: a suggestion needs at least k distinct users (not k searches), so one person's search cannot become public and one account cannot buy a suggestion. PII patterns are dropped regardless, a blocklist and classifier remove harmful completions, and bot sessions are excluded before counting. Builds are diffed on golden prefixes and canaried. For urgent removals, a serve-time denylist plus a CDN purge takes effect in minutes.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="4 · Do you shard the index? What about hot prefixes like 's'?">

Not while it fits: 1.5 GB, so every node holds the whole index and "s" traffic spreads across all nodes. The cluster is sized for requests per second and availability. If it outgrows a node I shard by locale, then by prefix ranges cut by measured traffic, give hot ranges more replicas, and precompute the 18,000-odd short prefixes on every node so they never fan out. The edge cache absorbs the hottest prefixes too.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="5 · How do you personalise without breaking caching?">

Keep personal data out of the shared response. The device holds recent searches and merges matching ones above the global list: private, free, and the global list stays edge-cacheable. Location is handled with regional index variants. A server re-ranker over a longer candidate list (top 30) fits the 5 ms slice if it is a light model, but its responses are private and skip the edge, so add it only if click-through data justifies the cost.

</Reveal>

## Five mistakes to leave behind

1. **"I will use a trie."** A data structure is not a design. Requirements, numbers, then the offline/online split.
2. **Ranking at query time.** Walking and sorting the subtree for "s" on every keystroke. Precompute top-K per node.
3. **Counting searches instead of people.** One user's 40 searches caused the incident in the prologue. Require k distinct users.
4. **Sharding by first letter by reflex.** Ask whether 1.5 GB fits first; if it does not, cut by traffic.
5. **Quoting server latency as the 100 ms.** The user feels keystroke to paint: debounce, network and render are most of it.

- [Unicode Normalization Forms (UAX #15)](https://unicode.org/reports/tr15/) — NFC/NFKC, the reason "café" can be two different strings.
- [MDN — AbortController](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/AbortController) — cancelling superseded requests in the browser.

**Look these up yourself**

- **Weighted finite state transducers for completion** — how Lucene stores suggestions with weights so top-K can be found without per-node lists.
- **Succinct tries (LOUDS)** — tree encodings at about two bits per node, for when even the compressed trie is too big.
- **Fuzzy prefix matching (Levenshtein automata)** — the usual route to typo-tolerant typeahead without exploding the index.
- **Learning to rank for query auto-completion** — using clicks and context instead of raw popularity; a common "what next" in the wrap-up.
- **k-anonymity and differential privacy for query logs** — the formal versions of the "k distinct users" rule, and their limits.
- **The space-saving algorithm** — a deterministic heavy-hitters algorithm, an alternative to count-min plus a heap for trending.
- **Input method editors and composition events** — how browsers report pinyin or kana input before characters are committed, and why typeahead must wait for them.
- **Zero-prefix suggestions** — what to show when the box is focused but empty, and why it is usually personal history.
