<h2 id="prologue">Prologue · What a shortener is</h2>

A **URL shortener** maps a short code to a long URL. The link `https://sho.rt/aZ3kQ9x` answers with a redirect to `https://shop.example.com/listings/88213/vintage-brass-lamp?ref=share&utm_source=chat`. (`sho.rt` is an illustrative domain used throughout.) That is the whole product: make a short link, follow one.

It is a favourite interview question because it is small enough to finish in 45 minutes, and every part of it opens onto a classic idea:

- **ID generation:** how do many servers mint unique, short, hard-to-guess codes?
- **Read-heavy design:** about a hundred clicks for every link created, so the cache carries the system.
- **HTTP semantics:** 301 versus 302 decides whether you can count clicks at all.
- **Partitioning:** which key to shard on, and why the obvious one (the user) breaks the hottest query.
- **Async pipelines:** counting clicks without slowing them.

You met this prompt in the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/delivery-framework/`}>delivery framework</a> and its numbers in <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/estimation/#shortener`}>estimation</a>. This lesson is the full answer. Four slips to avoid:

- **Pacing.** Code generation is fun, and candidates spend twenty minutes on base62 before drawing a box. Be at the deep dive by 0:25, not 0:10.
- **The cache hand-wave.** "Cache the redirects in Redis" invites four follow-up questions. Chapter 13 answers them in advance.
- **Silent skips.** Say out loud what you are not designing: the analytics dashboard UI, billing, link previews.
- **Unchecked numbers.** Some practice prompts give numbers that contradict each other. Check yours (Chapter 2).

**How to use it.** Attempt each step yourself, say the answer aloud, then read on. Every chapter ends with a quiz that tests decisions, not definitions.

<h2 id="requirements">Chapter 1 · Requirements</h2>

A product owner will happily hand you five features: shorten, redirect, custom names like `sho.rt/spring-sale`, links that expire when a promotion ends, and click counts per day and per country. You have 45 minutes. Which features define the product, what happens to the rest, and what does "never break a link" mean as a number?

**Functional requirements.** Keep the verbs without which it is not this product, then the ones that create hard engineering.

1. **Shorten:** a user submits a long URL and gets a short link.
2. **Redirect:** anyone following the short link is sent to the long URL.
3. **Custom alias (optional):** a user may choose the code, if it is free.
4. **Expiry (optional):** a link may carry an expiry time, after which it stops redirecting.

**Stretch, said out loud:** click analytics (counts per link per day, by country and referrer). It does not change the core design, but it constrains the redirect path. Say: "I'll make sure the redirect can emit click events cheaply, and design the pipeline if we have time."

**Out of scope, said out loud:** the analytics dashboard UI, billing and plans, link-in-bio pages, QR codes, editing a link's destination after creation. "Tell me if you want any of those."

Aliases and expiry stay because they are cheap and they **touch the core**: aliases share the code namespace (Chapter 11), and expiry changes cache TTLs (Chapter 14). A feature that touches the core deserves two sentences. One that does not gets a line in the out-of-scope list.

**Non-functional requirements, each with a number or a choice.** Assumption for this example: a large consumer product.

| Quality | Requirement | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| **Redirect latency** | p99 under **50 ms** at the server, plus network time | The read path is one memory lookup, no sequential database calls |
| **Availability** | Redirects **99.99%** (about 4.3 minutes of downtime a month); creates 99.9% | Redirects degrade last: caches, replicas, multi-region reads |
| **Durability** | **An acknowledged link is never lost** and never changes meaning | Replicated write before `201`; codes are never reused (Chapter 14) |
| **Consistency** | A new link may take **a second or two** to resolve in other regions, but never resolves to the wrong URL | Async replication is fine; a stale *miss* must not become a 404 (Chapter 17) |
| **Scale** | **100M new links a day**, **100 redirects per link**, links kept **5–10 years** | Chapter 2's arithmetic: partitioning, cache cluster, 7-character codes |
| **Code shape** | **Short** (7 characters), URL-safe, **not sequential** | Base62, length from keyspace maths, a non-guessable order (Chapters 6–10) |

"Never break a link" became two rows: **durability** (we never lose a mapping) and **availability** (we always answer). A third failure, the shortening company shutting down, is only defended by owning the domain and the data. Mention it in one line.

**The bridge**, in the delivery framework's shape: "So: shorten and redirect, custom aliases and expiry as small extras, analytics as a stretch. Redirects under 50 ms at p99 and four nines; links never lost; a second of cross-region lag is fine. Does that match what you had in mind?"

<h2 id="estimation">Chapter 2 · The arithmetic of a short link</h2>

It is tempting to write "100M DAU" because practice prompts say so. What you need is how many links are made per day, how many redirects follow, and which numbers change a box.

The <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/estimation/#shortener`}>estimation lesson</a> sized this prompt at two scales: **Scale A**, 1M new links a day, and **Scale B**, 100 times that. This lesson designs for Scale B, and every number below matches estimation's table. (Scale A is a single Postgres primary and one cache node. It is the follow-up "what if it is a hundred times smaller?" and needs almost none of Parts Four and Five.)

**Assumptions, stated:** 100M new links a day · 100 redirects per link over its life, so reads to writes is about 100:1 · a day rounded to 10⁵ seconds · peak is 3× average · 500 B per stored link including indexes · 5-year planning horizon, 10-year sanity check.

```text
Writes     100M/day ÷ 10⁵ s            = 1,000/s average   → 3,000/s peak
Reads      100M × 100 = 10B/day ÷ 10⁵  = 100,000/s average → 300,000/s peak
Storage    100M × 500 B                = 50 GB/day
           × 365                       ≈ 18.25 TB/year
           × 5 years                   ≈ 91 TB logical       (× 3 replicas ≈ 274 TB raw)
           × 10 years                  ≈ 182.5 TB logical
Links      100M × 365 × 5              = 182.5 billion in five years
                          × 10         = 365 billion in ten
Bandwidth  300,000/s × ~500 B          ≈ 150 MB/s peak       → non-issue, skip
```

The 10⁵ rounding overstates the per-second rate by about 16% (a real day is 86,400 seconds). That errs on the safe side for capacity, and estimation uses the same convention.

**The conclusions are the deliverable, not the numbers:**

- **Reads dominate 100:1 and peak at 300,000 a second.** The redirect path is the system. It needs a cache that answers about 99% of reads (Chapter 13).
- **91 TB in five years** is past one comfortable database node. Partition from day one, by the key the redirect uses (Chapter 12). At about 2 TB per partition that is roughly 46 partitions, before replicas.
- **182.5 billion links in five years.** The code must address at least that many, with room to stay sparse. Chapter 6 turns this into "seven characters".
- **Writes are only 3,000 a second at peak.** Creating links is not the hard part. Do not spend the deep dive there unless asked.
- **Bandwidth is tiny.** Say "non-issue" and move on.

<h2 id="entities-api">Chapter 3 · Entities and the API</h2>

Start with a real failure. A user on a train taps "shorten" on a link. The request succeeds on the server, but the response is lost in a tunnel. The app retries, and the retry creates a second link. After a few taps there are nine codes for one URL and a plan quota that counts nine links. The network misbehaved, so what should the API have done?

**Core entities, two minutes, names and relationships:**

- **Link:** the short code, the long URL, the owner, created-at, an optional expires-at, a status (active, disabled, expired), and whether the code is a custom alias.
- **User:** the creator. One user has many links (**one-to-many**). Anonymous links have no owner.
- **Click:** an event, not a row we update: code, time, country, referrer, a hashed client id. It flows to analytics and never touches the Link.

Say the one non-obvious thing: **the Link is immutable after creation** (apart from status). There is no "edit destination", and immutability is what makes the caching in Chapter 13 almost free.

**The API.**

```http
POST /urls HTTP/1.1
Host: api.sho.rt
Authorization: Bearer <token>
Idempotency-Key: 5b0e7c9e-4c1a-4d6f-9d1e-0c2f6a7b3e10
Content-Type: application/json

{ "longUrl": "https://shop.example.com/listings/88213?ref=share",
  "alias": "spring-sale",
  "expiresAt": "2026-12-31T23:59:59Z" }
```

`alias` and `expiresAt` are optional. The owner comes from the token, never the body.

```http
HTTP/1.1 201 Created
Content-Type: application/json

{ "code": "spring-sale", "shortUrl": "https://sho.rt/spring-sale", "expiresAt": "2026-12-31T23:59:59Z" }
```

Other outcomes: `400` invalid URL or alias, `409` alias taken, `429` creating too fast.

```text
GET /{code}                       follow a short link (on the short domain, no /api prefix)
  → 302 Location: <longUrl>       (Chapter 4 explains 302)
  → 404 no such code · 410 expired or disabled
DELETE /urls/{code}               owner only → 204
GET /users/me/urls?cursor=...     the owner's links, newest first, cursor-paginated
GET /urls/{code}/stats?from=...&to=...   stretch: daily click counts
```

**The idempotency key, precisely.** The client generates one unique key per *intent* ("shorten this link, this tap") and sends it with every retry of that intent. The server keeps `(owner, key) → (request fingerprint, response)` for 24 hours:

- **First time:** claim the key with an insert-if-absent so two concurrent retries cannot both run, create the link, store the response.
- **Same key, same request:** return the stored `201` with the same code. No second link.
- **Same key, different body:** a client bug. Reject it (409 or 422; pick one and document it).
- **Same key, first attempt still running:** answer 409 "in progress, retry shortly" rather than running it twice.

```sql
CREATE TABLE idempotency (
  owner_id      BIGINT NOT NULL,
  idem_key      CHAR(36) CHARACTER SET ascii NOT NULL,
  request_hash  BINARY(32) NOT NULL,          -- SHA-256 of the request body
  state         ENUM('running','done') NOT NULL,
  response_json JSON NULL,
  created_at    DATETIME NOT NULL DEFAULT CURRENT_TIMESTAMP,
  PRIMARY KEY (owner_id, idem_key)
);
-- Claim: a duplicate key raises error 1062, which tells you "someone else has it".
INSERT INTO idempotency (owner_id, idem_key, request_hash, state) VALUES (42, '5b0e...', UNHEX('9f2c...'), 'running');
```

Cost: 100M creates a day × about 200 B per record, kept 24 hours, is about **20 GB** live. Small.

The delivery framework called idempotency keys optional for a shortener, because a duplicate link is harmless to routing. That holds until a duplicate costs the user something. Here a retry mints a second link against a plan quota and splits click counts, so it flips. **The rule is not "always add idempotency keys"; it is "ask what a duplicate costs".**

<Figure caption="Why the key matters. The first attempt succeeds but its response is lost. The retry carries the same key, so the server replays the stored response instead of minting a second link.">

    C->>S: POST /urls with Idempotency-Key K

Two more habits worth a sentence each: the list endpoint is **cursor-paginated** (a user with 200,000 campaign links exists), and `GET /{code}` sits on the short domain with **no `/api` prefix**. The path is the product.

<h2 id="status-codes">Chapter 4 · 301 or 302</h2>

The redirect status looks like a two-character decision, and interviewers like it because it hides a caching question. Picture a shortener that returned 301 Moved Permanently because the links are permanent. Its traffic fell by a third in the first month, and the team celebrated. Then marketing noticed the campaign links showed a third fewer clicks than the ad platform did. Then a link was reported as malware and disabled, but people kept landing on it for weeks. The traffic had not gone away. It had stopped asking the server.

| Status | Meaning | Cached by browsers by default? | Method on redirect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **301** Moved Permanently | This resource has moved for good | **Yes**: "heuristically cacheable" (RFC 9110); browsers may keep it a long time | May change POST to GET (historical) |
| **302** Found | Temporarily elsewhere | **No**, unless you send explicit freshness (`max-age`, `Expires`) | May change POST to GET (historical) |
| **307** Temporary Redirect | Like 302 | No | **Preserved** |
| **308** Permanent Redirect | Like 301 | Yes | **Preserved** |

For a shortener every request is a `GET`, so the method column does not matter. **Cacheability does.**

- **301 and the browser remembers.** The second time this browser follows the link, it may not ask you at all. That means less load and a faster click. It also means **you do not see the click**, and **you cannot change your mind**: disable, expire or take down the link, and every browser that cached the 301 still goes to the old destination. You cannot purge a browser.
- **302 and the browser asks every time.** Every click reaches you (or your CDN). You can count it, and you can stop redirecting the moment a link is disabled or expires.

**The status code sets the default. `Cache-Control` sets the truth.** A 301 with `Cache-Control: max-age=3600` bounds how long browsers keep it. A 302 with `max-age` lets them cache it. So say both halves:

```http
HTTP/1.1 302 Found
Location: https://shop.example.com/listings/88213/vintage-brass-lamp?ref=share
Cache-Control: private, max-age=0
```

> "302 Found, with `Cache-Control: private, max-age=0`. We sell click analytics and we need to disable links instantly, so every click must come back to us. If analytics did not matter, a 301 with a bounded max-age would save load, but expiry and takedown would then lag by up to that max-age."

**Interview takeaway:** 301 hands control of every future click to each user's browser. 302 keeps control with you at the cost of more requests.

A common first draft has a code-generation service, a ZooKeeper cluster, a Bloom filter and a Kafka topic. Ask of each box: which requirement is it for? Draw shorten first, then redirect, with the fewest boxes. The rest must be earned in the deep dives.

<h2 id="keyspace">Chapter 6 · How long is short?</h2>

Product teams often ask for six characters, because the previous service used six and shorter is nicer in a chat bubble. Three questions settle it: how many links will exist in five years, how many codes do six characters give, and what happens to the rest of the design as the code space fills?

**Base62** is the 62 characters that are safe in a URL path without escaping and easy to type: `0–9`, `A–Z`, `a–z`. (Base64 adds `+` and `/`, which need escaping in URLs. base64url swaps them for `-` and `_`, which work but look odd and are easy to mistype. Base62 is the convention.) A code of length *n* can be one of 62ⁿ values.

| Length | Codes (62ⁿ) | Five years at 100M/day (182.5B) | Ten years (365B) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | 56.8 billion | **Too few**: runs out in about a year and a half | Too few |
| **7** | **3.52 trillion** | **5.2% full**, 19× headroom | 10.4% full, 9.6× headroom |
| 8 | 218 trillion | 0.08% full | 0.17% full |

62⁷ = 3,521,614,606,208. At Scale A (1M links a day) six characters *would* last: 1.8 billion links fill 3% of 62⁶. That is why the scale question comes before the length question.

<Figure caption="Choosing the length: count links to the horizon, compare with 62ⁿ, and say the fill fraction.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    N["182.5B links in 5 years"] --> S6["62^6 = 56.8B\nfewer codes than links"]
    N --> S7["62^7 = 3.52T\n5.2% full, 19x headroom"]
    S6 --> X["Rejected"]
    S7 --> OK["Chosen: 7 characters"]
    OK --> F["Flip to 8 if growth is 10x\n(52% full)"]`} />
</Figure>

**Why the fill fraction matters more than the raw count.** It decides three things in the rest of the design:

- **Collision rate for random codes.** A uniformly random code is already taken with probability equal to the fill. At 5.2%, about one draw in 19 collides. Expected draws per link are 1 ÷ (1 − 0.052) ≈ **1.05**, and needing more than three draws is about **1 in 7,000**.
- **Guessability.** A scraper trying random codes finds a real link **one try in 19** at five years. Short codes are not secrets (Chapter 9).
- **Headroom.** At 10× growth the five-year fill would be 52%, which means eight characters. Say the flip condition before the interviewer asks.

**Commit:** "Seven characters of base62: 3.5 trillion codes, about 5% full after five years and 10% after ten. Six is too few at our scale. Eight is only needed at ten times the traffic."

**A common slip:** "62⁷ is 3.5 trillion, so collisions are impossible." The space is large, not empty. What matters is how full it is, and the next chapter shows why.

<h2 id="hash">Chapter 7 · Hash and truncate</h2>

The first idea most people have is elegant: hash the long URL with SHA-256 and take seven base62 characters. The same URL always gives the same code, so deduplication is free and no state is needed. The question is how many links can exist before two *different* URLs get the same seven characters.

**Hash-and-truncate:** `code = 7 base62 characters of SHA-256(longUrl)`. SHA-256 is collision-resistant across all 256 bits, but seven base62 characters keep only about 42 bits. Truncated, a good hash behaves like a random code, so it collides like one.

```python
import hashlib

ALPHABET = "0123456789ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZabcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz"

def hash_code(long_url: str, attempt: int = 0) -> str:
    data = long_url if attempt == 0 else f"{long_url}#{attempt}"
    n = int.from_bytes(hashlib.sha256(data.encode()).digest()[:8], "big")
    code = ""
    for _ in range(7):                 # keep 7 base62 digits (the low ones) of the first 64 bits
        n, d = divmod(n, 62)
        code = ALPHABET[d] + code
    return code
```

**The birthday bound.** Put *n* random codes into a space of *N* values. The chance that *at least one pair* collides is about

```text
P(some collision) ≈ 1 − e^(−n² / 2N)        so a 50% chance arrives at n ≈ √(2N · ln 2) ≈ 1.18 √N
```

**Worked numbers** for N = 62⁷ ≈ 3.52 × 10¹²:

- √N ≈ 1.88 million.
- A **1%** chance of some collision after about **266,000** links.
- A **50%** chance after about **2.2 million** links, which at 100M links a day is about **32 minutes** after launch.
- After five years (182.5 billion links) the expected number of colliding pairs is n² / 2N ≈ **4.7 billion**. Collisions are not an event but a flow: at the five-year fill, about **5.2 million a day** (100M × 5.2%).

Two different questions hide here, and interviewers ask both:

| Question | Answer | Number |
|---|---|---|
| Has **any** pair collided yet? (birthday bound) | Yes, within the first hour | 50% after about 2.2M links |
| Is **this new** code already taken? (fill fraction) | A small but constant chance | 5.2% at five years |

So the design must detect and resolve collisions. Resolution is where hash-and-truncate loses its charm. On a collision, rehash with a suffix and look again:

```python
def create_by_hash(cur, long_url, owner_id):
    for attempt in range(10):
        code = hash_code(long_url, attempt)
        if insert_link(cur, code, long_url, owner_id):    # insert-if-absent, defined in Chapter 10
            return code
        cur.execute("SELECT long_url FROM links WHERE code = %s", (code,))
        if cur.fetchone()[0] == long_url:                 # the same URL was shortened before
            return code
    raise RuntimeError("probe chain too long")
```

Now finding the code for a URL means walking that chain, so the "free deduplication" costs a lookup per step. And the deduplication is often *wrong* anyway:

- **Two users shortening the same URL share a code**, and its click counts, its expiry and its "owner". One user deletes "their" link and breaks the other's.
- **Anyone can compute the code for a URL they can guess**, and check whether it has been shortened. That leaks which private documents were shared.
- **Salting per user** fixes both and removes the only advantage hashing had.

**Where hashing is right:** content-addressed storage, where identical bytes *should* share one key (the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/blob-cdn/#dedup`}>blob storage lesson's</a> dedup), or as an optional *per-user* dedup index ("you already shortened this; here is your link") kept beside the main table, not as the code itself.

<h2 id="counter">Chapter 8 · Counter and ranges</h2>

Next idea: avoid collisions entirely with a counter. Every link gets the next number, base62-encoded. A reasonable start is one Redis `INCR`: very fast, very simple. Then the primary dies and the replica is promoted, but the replica was eleven seconds behind. Eleven seconds of numbers are handed out a second time, and every one of those codes now means two different things.

**Counter plus base62.** A counter hands out 0, 1, 2, ...; base62 turns each number into a code. Distinct numbers give distinct codes, so there is **nothing to check** on insert. Seven characters hold every number below 62⁷.

The encoder (Java, tested). The alphabet is in ASCII order (`0–9` < `A–Z` < `a–z`), which buys a small gift: **fixed-width codes sort in the same order as their numbers.**

```java id=base62 group=codes
/** Base62: digits, then upper case, then lower case — ASCII order, so fixed-width codes sort like numbers. */
static final class Base62 {
    static final String ALPHABET = "0123456789ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZabcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz";
    private static final int[] DIGIT = new int[128];               // char → value, -1 if not base62
    static {
        Arrays.fill(DIGIT, -1);
        for (int i = 0; i < 62; i++) DIGIT[ALPHABET.charAt(i)] = i;
    }

    /** Shortest form of a non-negative id: 0 → "0", 61 → "z", 62 → "10". */
    static String encode(long n) {
        if (n < 0) throw new IllegalArgumentException("ids are non-negative: " + n);
        char[] buf = new char[11];                                  // 62^11 > Long.MAX_VALUE: 11 always suffice
        int pos = buf.length;
        do {
            buf[--pos] = ALPHABET.charAt((int) (n % 62));
            n /= 62;
        } while (n > 0);                                            // do-while, so 0 still yields "0"
        return new String(buf, pos, buf.length - pos);
    }

    /** Fixed width, left-padded with '0': encode(1000, 7) → "00000G8". */
    static String encode(long n, int width) {
        String s = encode(n);
        if (s.length() > width) throw new IllegalArgumentException(n + " needs more than " + width + " characters");
        return "0".repeat(width - s.length()) + s;
    }

    static long decode(String code) {
        if (code.isEmpty() || code.length() > 11) throw new IllegalArgumentException("bad length: " + code.length());
        long n = 0;
        for (int i = 0; i < code.length(); i++) {
            char c = code.charAt(i);
            int d = c < 128 ? DIGIT[c] : -1;
            if (d < 0) throw new IllegalArgumentException("not base62: '" + c + "'");
            n = Math.addExact(Math.multiplyExact(n, 62), d);        // ArithmeticException past Long.MAX_VALUE
        }
        return n;
    }
}
```

The checker for this page round-trips 0, `Long.MAX_VALUE` (which encodes to `AzL8n0Y58m7`, eleven characters) and 200,000 random longs against an independent `BigInteger` implementation, checks that fixed-width codes sort numerically, and confirms that `decode` refuses, rather than silently wraps, anything above `Long.MAX_VALUE`. That last one matters: a decoder that overflows quietly turns a garbage code from a scraper into a valid-looking id.

**The single counter's three problems.** A database sequence or one Redis `INCR` can easily keep up with 3,000 creates a second, so throughput is not the problem.

1. **A single point of failure** on the create path.
2. **Re-issued ids after failover.** With asynchronous replication, a promoted replica can be behind the old primary and hand out numbers that already belong to links. The fix is a *durable, strongly consistent* counter (a synchronously replicated database row, or a consensus store such as etcd or ZooKeeper), and those are slower per call.
3. **Sequential codes leak** your volume and let anyone walk your links (Chapter 9).

**Range allocation fixes the first two.** Each link-service instance reserves a **block** of ids, say 10,000, from the durable counter, then hands them out from memory. The counter is touched once per block, so it can afford to be slow and strongly consistent.

<Figure caption="Each server owns a block and hands out ids from memory. A crashed server leaves a gap, and nobody ever receives its unused ids, because the counter only moves forward.">
  <Mermaid code={`sequenceDiagram
    participant A as Link service A
    participant B as Link service B
    participant C as Counter row
    A->>C: reserve 10,000
    C-->>A: block 1,240,000 to 1,249,999
    B->>C: reserve 10,000
    C-->>B: block 1,250,000 to 1,259,999
    Note over A: hands out 1,240,000 onward from memory
    Note over A: crashes at 1,244,999
    A->>C: restarted A reserves 10,000
    C-->>A: block 1,260,000 to 1,269,999
    Note over C: 1,245,000 to 1,249,999 are a gap, never reused`} />

The one shared, durable thing is a row in a synchronously replicated MySQL database. Reserving a block is a row-locked read followed by an update:

```sql
CREATE TABLE id_blocks (name VARCHAR(32) PRIMARY KEY, next_id BIGINT UNSIGNED NOT NULL);
INSERT INTO id_blocks VALUES ('links', 0);
```

```python
def reserve_block(conn, size: int = 10_000, name: str = "links") -> int:
    """Return the first id of a fresh block [start, start + size). The row lock makes blocks disjoint."""
    conn.begin()
    with conn.cursor() as cur:
        cur.execute("SELECT next_id FROM id_blocks WHERE name = %s FOR UPDATE", (name,))
        (start,) = cur.fetchone()
        cur.execute("UPDATE id_blocks SET next_id = next_id + %s WHERE name = %s", (size, name))
    conn.commit()
    return start
```

A single statement also works: `UPDATE id_blocks SET next_id = LAST_INSERT_ID(next_id + 10000) WHERE name = 'links'` makes the new value readable on the same connection, so the block starts at that value minus 10,000. Either way the lock and the transaction are what stop two servers getting the same block. The in-memory generator is the Java below.

```java id=ranges group=codes
/** The one shared, durable thing: a counter. Each call reserves a whole block of ids. */
interface RangeSource {
    long reserve(long size);    // returns the first id of a fresh block [start, start + size)
}

/** Stand-in for one row in a strongly consistent store:
 *  UPDATE id_blocks SET next_id = next_id + :size WHERE name = 'links' RETURNING next_id - :size */
static final class CounterRow implements RangeSource {
    private final AtomicLong next;
    CounterRow(long start) { next = new AtomicLong(start); }
    public long reserve(long size) { return next.getAndAdd(size); }    // atomic: blocks never overlap
}

/** One per link-service instance. Hands out ids from memory; touches the counter once per block. */
static final class RangeIdGenerator {
    private final RangeSource source;
    private final long blockSize;
    private long next, end;                     // the current block is [next, end); it starts empty

    RangeIdGenerator(RangeSource source, long blockSize) {
        if (blockSize < 1) throw new IllegalArgumentException("block size must be positive");
        this.source = source;
        this.blockSize = blockSize;
    }

    synchronized long nextId() {
        if (next == end) {                      // block used up (or never fetched): reserve another
            next = source.reserve(blockSize);
            end = next + blockSize;
        }
        return next++;
    }
}
```

**What stays true:** the counter only moves forward, and each reservation is atomic, so **no two blocks overlap**. Within a block, `synchronized` makes each `next++` belong to exactly one caller. The checker runs eight "servers", each shared by four threads, drawing 800,000 ids concurrently from one counter in blocks of 1,000: every id from 0 to 799,999 appears exactly once. It also crashes a server halfway through a block and checks that the unused half is never handed out.

**Worked numbers.** At 3,000 creates a second and blocks of 10,000, the whole fleet makes **0.3 counter calls a second**, so the counter can live in an ordinary synchronously replicated database row. Gaps are harmless: even a hundred servers each losing a whole block every day for ten years wastes about **0.1%** of 62⁷. A production refinement is to fetch the next block in the background when the current one is 80% used, so no request ever waits on the counter. Flickr's "ticket servers" are a well-known version of the idea (see Beyond this page): two databases issuing odd and even numbers.

**And Snowflake?** The <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/databases/#ids`}>databases lesson</a> builds Snowflake ids: 41 bits of milliseconds, 10 of machine, 12 of sequence, with no coordination per id.

```python
EPOCH_MS = 1_700_000_000_000          # a fixed starting point you choose

def snowflake(now_ms: int, machine_id: int, seq: int) -> int:
    return ((now_ms - EPOCH_MS) << 22) | (machine_id << 12) | seq
```

They are excellent *primary keys* but poor *short codes*:

- **Length.** The timestamp is shifted left 22 bits, so an id one year past its epoch is already about 1.3 × 10¹⁷, which is **10 base62 characters**. It passes 62¹⁰ about 6.3 years after the epoch and becomes **11**. Not short.
- **Guessable.** The high bits are the clock. Anyone can predict the codes being minted right now.

**Counter versus Snowflake versus a key pool.**

| | Single counter | Counter with ranges | Snowflake | Pre-generated key pool |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coordination per id | Every id | Once per block | Machine id at startup | Once per batch of keys |
| Failover risk | Re-issue if the replica lags | None if the row is durable | Clock going backwards, duplicate machine ids | The pool table is the durable thing |
| Code length | 7 | 7 | 10–11 | 7 |
| Order visible | Yes | Yes, unless scrambled | Yes (clock) | No (keys are pre-randomised) |

A **key pool** (sometimes called a key generation service) is a variant worth naming. A background job fills a table with random, already-unique 7-character keys, marked unused. Each link-service instance claims a batch (`UPDATE ... SET state='taken' ... LIMIT 1000`, one transaction), then hands them out. You get random-looking codes with no collision handling on the hot path. The cost is another table and job to run, and keys lost when an instance crashes (harmless, as with gaps above).

<Reveal q="Why does the crashed server’s half-block become a gap rather than being handed back?">

Because handing it back requires knowing, reliably, which ids the dead server did *not* use, and it is dead. Maybe it handed out 1,244,999 and wrote the link a millisecond before crashing. Maybe the write is still in flight on the network. Reclaiming is a coordination problem with a correctness risk, while the gap costs a few thousand numbers out of trillions. **Waste the numbers, keep the promise.**

</Reveal>

**A common slip:** "Redis INCR, it's atomic." Each `INCR` is atomic. A failover that loses recent increments is not, and a counter that goes backwards breaks the one promise a shortener makes.

<h2 id="enumeration">Chapter 9 · Hiding the order</h2>

Suppose the counter prototype is on a test server. One IP address requests `/0000001`, `/0000002`, `/0000003` in order, twelve a second, for six hours. It is not attacking. It is reading: every link anyone made, in the order they were made, including the ones people thought were private because nobody else had the link. And a competitor who shortens one link today and one tomorrow can subtract the two codes to learn your daily volume.

**Sequential codes leak two things:**

1. **Every link, walkable.** Count upwards and you visit them all: shared documents, unlisted videos, password-reset pages someone shortened. Researchers have shown this against real shorteners: short codes for cloud-storage sharing links were enumerated, exposing private files (see "Look these up yourself").
2. **Your business volume**: the difference between two codes made a day apart.

**Fix one: scramble the counter with a keyed permutation.** Keep the counter (no collision checks), but pass each id through a one-to-one shuffle of `[0, 62⁷)` before encoding. Distinct ids still give distinct codes, and consecutive ids land far apart. A standard construction is a **Feistel network**: split the number into two halves, and for a few rounds swap them while mixing one half into the other with a keyed function. Feistel networks are invertible whatever the mixing function is. That is their whole trick. Because 62⁷ is not a power of two, run the network on 42 bits (2⁴² ≈ 4.4 × 10¹² ≥ 62⁷) and **cycle-walk**: if the output lands at or above 62⁷, apply the network again until it lands inside. The network is a permutation of the 42-bit space, so this gives a permutation of the smaller domain, and since 2⁴² is only about 1.25 × 62⁷, it takes about 1.25 rounds of walking on average.

```java id=scramble group=codes
/** A keyed permutation of [0, domain): distinct inputs give distinct outputs, and the order is hidden.
 *  A Feistel network on 2 × halfBits bits, plus cycle walking to stay inside the domain. */
static final class CodeScrambler {
    static final long SEVEN_CHARS = 3_521_614_606_208L;    // 62^7, which fits in 42 bits = 2 × 21

    static CodeScrambler sevenChars(long... roundKeys) { return new CodeScrambler(SEVEN_CHARS, 21, roundKeys); }

    private final long domain;
    private final int half;
    private final long mask;
    private final long[] keys;

    CodeScrambler(long domain, int halfBits, long... roundKeys) {
        if (halfBits < 1 || halfBits > 31 || domain < 1 || domain > (1L << (2 * halfBits)))
            throw new IllegalArgumentException("domain must fit in 2 × halfBits bits");
        if (roundKeys.length < 3) throw new IllegalArgumentException("use at least 3 rounds");
        this.domain = domain;
        this.half = halfBits;
        this.mask = (1L << halfBits) - 1;
        this.keys = roundKeys.clone();
    }

    long scramble(long x) {
        check(x);
        do x = forward(x); while (x >= domain);       // cycle walking: land inside the domain
        return x;
    }

    long unscramble(long y) {
        check(y);
        do y = backward(y); while (y >= domain);
        return y;
    }

    private long forward(long x) {
        long l = x >>> half, r = x & mask;
        for (long k : keys) { long t = l ^ round(r, k); l = r; r = t; }
        return (l << half) | r;
    }

    private long backward(long y) {                   // the same rounds, in reverse
        long l = y >>> half, r = y & mask;
        for (int i = keys.length - 1; i >= 0; i--) { long t = r ^ round(l, keys[i]); r = l; l = t; }
        return (l << half) | r;
    }

    // Mixes one half with a round key. A fast mixer, NOT a cipher: see below.
    private long round(long v, long key) {
        long h = (v ^ key) * 0x9E3779B97F4A7C15L;
        h ^= h >>> 29;
        return h & mask;
    }

    private void check(long v) {
        if (v < 0 || v >= domain) throw new IllegalArgumentException(v + " is outside [0, " + domain + ")");
    }
}
```

A code is then `Base62.encode(scrambler.scramble(generator.nextId()), 7)`. The checker proves the permutation property **exhaustively** on small domains (every input maps to a distinct output inside the domain, and `unscramble` inverts it), round-trips 100,000 random ids on the real 62⁷ domain, and checks that 100,000 consecutive ids produce 100,000 distinct, non-consecutive codes.

**Be honest about what it is.** This round function is a fast mixer, not a cipher. It hides the order from a casual observer, not from a determined cryptanalyst. If hiding your volume must survive a motivated adversary, use a vetted **format-preserving encryption** mode (NIST's FF1) with a key from your secrets manager, or skip the counter and draw random codes (next chapter).

**Fix two, needed whatever you mint: codes are not secrets.** Even with perfectly random codes, at five years the space is 5.2% full, so **one random guess in 19 finds a real link**. A short code is a *name*, not a *password*. So:

- **Rate-limit lookups per IP**, and alert on high 404 rates. A scraper's signature is a wall of misses.
- **Private links get a long code** (22 base62 characters is about 131 bits, unguessable in practice) or sit behind authentication. Say it as a product rule: "short links are public; for private content, the long-token mode or sign-in."

<h2 id="random">Chapter 10 · Random code, unique key</h2>

Three approaches so far: hash, counter with ranges, Snowflake. The fourth is to roll dice. Draw seven random characters and let the database refuse if the code is taken.

**Random codes with a conditional insert.** Draw seven characters from a cryptographically secure random generator. Write the link with an **insert-if-absent**: a unique key on `code` in a relational database, or a conditional put ("only if no item with this key exists") in a key-value store. If the store says the code is taken, draw again.

**Why "insert if absent" and not "check, then insert".** Two servers can draw the same code in the same millisecond. Both read "absent", both insert, and the second silently overwrites the first link, which now redirects somewhere its creator never chose. A **single conditional write** lets the store decide atomically: exactly one wins, and the other gets a conflict and redraws. This is the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-contention/`}>contention lesson's</a> rule, pushing the check into the atomic write, in its smallest form.

<Figure caption="Two servers draw the same code at the same instant. With check-then-insert both see 'absent' and the second overwrites. With a conditional insert the database lets one through.">

**The table.** One detail trips people up: MySQL's default text collations are **case-insensitive**, so `aZ3` and `az3` would count as the same key and halve your keyspace. Use a binary collation on the code column.

```sql
CREATE TABLE links (
  code       VARCHAR(32) CHARACTER SET ascii COLLATE ascii_bin NOT NULL,
  long_url   VARCHAR(2048) NOT NULL,
  owner_id   BIGINT NULL,
  created_at DATETIME NOT NULL DEFAULT CURRENT_TIMESTAMP,
  expires_at DATETIME NULL,
  status     ENUM('active','disabled','expired') NOT NULL DEFAULT 'active',
  is_alias   BOOLEAN NOT NULL DEFAULT FALSE,
  PRIMARY KEY (code)
) ENGINE=InnoDB;
```

```python
import secrets
import pymysql

DUP_KEY = 1062                           # MySQL error number for a duplicate unique key

def random_code(length: int = 7) -> str:
    return "".join(secrets.choice(ALPHABET) for _ in range(length))     # uniform, no modulo bias

def insert_link(cur, code, long_url, owner_id, expires_at=None, is_alias=False) -> bool:
    try:
        cur.execute(
            "INSERT INTO links (code, long_url, owner_id, expires_at, is_alias) VALUES (%s,%s,%s,%s,%s)",
            (code, long_url, owner_id, expires_at, is_alias))
        return True
    except pymysql.err.IntegrityError as e:
        if e.args[0] == DUP_KEY:
            return False                 # taken: the store decided, atomically
        raise

def create_link(cur, long_url, owner_id, expires_at=None, max_attempts=5) -> str:
    for _ in range(max_attempts):
        code = random_code()
        if insert_link(cur, code, long_url, owner_id, expires_at):
            return code
    raise RuntimeError("no free code after 5 draws: the space is too full, alert")
```

Run this in autocommit mode, or commit right after the insert, so the row (and its unique-key lock) is not held open.

**Five ways to write "insert" in MySQL, and what each does on a taken code:**

| Statement | On a duplicate code | Use here? |
|---|---|---|
| `INSERT` (plain) | Raises error 1062 | **Yes.** The signal you want |
| `INSERT IGNORE` | Silently does nothing, and also swallows other errors | No: you cannot tell success from conflict |
| `INSERT ... ON DUPLICATE KEY UPDATE` | Updates the existing row | No: overwrites the other link |
| `REPLACE` | Deletes the old row, inserts yours | No: destroys the other link |
| `SELECT`, then `INSERT` | Race between the two statements | No: the bug of this chapter |

The same idea in Java, tested. The `ConcurrentHashMap.putIfAbsent` call plays the role of the unique key.

```java id=mint group=codes
/** Stand-in for the links table: a unique key on code, written with insert-if-absent. */
static final class LinkStore {
    private final ConcurrentHashMap<String, String> byCode = new ConcurrentHashMap<>();
    boolean insertIfAbsent(String code, String longUrl) { return byCode.putIfAbsent(code, longUrl) == null; }
    String get(String code) { return byCode.get(code); }
    int size() { return byCode.size(); }
}

/** The committed design: random codes, claimed atomically; on a collision, draw again. */
static final class CodeMinter {
    static final Set<String> RESERVED = Set.of("api", "admin", "login", "help", "static", "health");
    private final LinkStore store;
    private final Random random;            // a SecureRandom in production
    private final int length;
    private final int maxAttempts;
    final AtomicLong collisions = new AtomicLong();

    CodeMinter(LinkStore store, Random random, int length, int maxAttempts) {
        this.store = store;
        this.random = random;
        this.length = length;
        this.maxAttempts = maxAttempts;
    }

    String create(String longUrl) {
        for (int attempt = 0; attempt < maxAttempts; attempt++) {
            char[] c = new char[length];
            for (int i = 0; i < length; i++) c[i] = Base62.ALPHABET.charAt(random.nextInt(62));
            String code = new String(c);
            if (store.insertIfAbsent(code, longUrl)) return code;     // the store decides, atomically
            collisions.incrementAndGet();
        }
        throw new IllegalStateException("no free code after " + maxAttempts + " draws: the space is too full");
    }

    /** Custom alias: same table, same conditional insert. false means taken → 409 Conflict. */
    boolean claimAlias(String alias, String longUrl) {
        if (!alias.matches("[A-Za-z0-9_-]{4,32}") || RESERVED.contains(alias.toLowerCase(Locale.ROOT)))
            throw new IllegalArgumentException("invalid alias: " + alias);   // → 400
        return store.insertIfAbsent(alias, longUrl);
    }
}
```

**What the checker proves.** On a deliberately tiny space (three characters, 238,328 codes) filled to 25–33%, the number of collisions matches the fill-fraction prediction (expected redraws at fill *f* are *f* ÷ (1 − *f*) per link) within 5%. That is Chapter 6's "one in 19" claim, tested at a scale where it happens often. Eight threads creating 40,000 links concurrently never hand one code to two links and never overwrite one. And a completely full space fails loudly after `maxAttempts` draws instead of looping forever, which in production is the alarm that says "time for eight characters". (With `maxAttempts` of 5 at our fill, the chance of five straight collisions at 5.2% is about 4 in 10 million.)

**The four strategies, side by side:**

| | Hash + truncate | Counter + ranges (+ permutation) | Snowflake | **Random + conditional insert** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coordination | None | One counter call per block | Machine-id assignment | **None** |
| Check on insert | Yes, plus a probe chain | **No** (but see aliases) | No | **Yes**, one conditional write |
| Length | 7 | 7 | 10–11 | **7** |
| Order guessable? | Computable from the URL | Raw: yes. Permuted: hidden | Yes, from the clock | **No** |
| What can go wrong | Shared codes across users | Counter durability, key management | Long codes | Retries grow as the space fills |

**Commit:**

> "Random 7-character base62 codes from a secure random generator, written with insert-if-absent. On conflict, redraw, up to five times. At 5% fill that is 1.05 draws per link. It needs no coordinator, reveals no order, and uses the same conditional write as custom aliases, so there is one uniqueness mechanism in the system. I would flip to counter ranges through a keyed permutation if our store made conditional writes expensive (some wide-column stores run a consensus round per conditional write) or if the space got crowded enough that retries showed up in latency."

<Reveal q="With counter ranges, generated codes never collide. So can the insert be a plain write?">

Only if custom aliases can never land in the generated space. A user could claim the alias `0004C92` today, and the counter reaches id 1,000,000 next month and mints exactly `0004C92`. A plain write would **overwrite the user's alias**. Two fixes: keep the insert conditional everywhere (and treat a conflict on a generated code as "skip this id"), or make the namespaces disjoint by rule, for example aliases must contain a `-` or have a length the generator never produces. Say which.

</Reveal>

<h2 id="aliases">Chapter 11 · Custom aliases</h2>

Marketing will bring a list: `sho.rt/sale`, `sho.rt/login`, `sho.rt/help`, `sho.rt/careers`. If someone else claims `sho.rt/login` first, they own the most convincing phishing link the service will ever host. Aliases need rules.

**Custom aliases** are short codes a person picks. They live in **the same table and the same namespace** as generated codes, because the redirect path does not know or care which kind a code is: `GET /{code}` looks up one key.

- **Uniqueness:** the same insert-if-absent as generated codes. Taken means **409 Conflict**, with a suggestion if you are feeling generous.
- **Validation (400):** a character set (`A–Z a–z 0–9 - _`), a length range (4–32 here), and a **reserved-words list**: `api`, `admin`, `login`, `help`, `static`, `health`, your own product names. Anything that could pass for an official page or collide with a route you will need later. Test that reserved words are refused in any letter case.
- **Case:** codes are case-sensitive (`aZ3` and `az3` differ, which is where base62's extra capacity comes from). Keep aliases case-sensitive too, so there is one rule. The UI can warn when `Spring-Sale` and `spring-sale` would both exist. This is also why the column needs the binary collation from Chapter 10.
- **Squatting:** aliases are scarce, so limit how many a user can hold, and require an account (no anonymous aliases).
- **Profanity in generated codes:** random base62 will eventually spell something rude. Some shorteners drop vowels from the alphabet to make that much rarer. It costs keyspace (an alphabet of 52 for seven characters is about 1.03 trillion codes, still plenty here). Mention it, do not design it.

```python
import re

RESERVED = {"api", "admin", "login", "help", "static", "health"}
ALIAS_RE = re.compile(r"[A-Za-z0-9_-]{4,32}")

def claim_alias(cur, alias, long_url, owner_id) -> int:
    if not ALIAS_RE.fullmatch(alias) or alias.lower() in RESERVED:
        return 400
    return 201 if insert_link(cur, alias, long_url, owner_id, is_alias=True) else 409
```

<Reveal q="Should a user be able to change where their alias points after a campaign ends?">

That is "edit destination", which was put out of scope. This is the moment to explain that it was a cut with consequences. <h2 id="storage">Chapter 12 · Storage and partitioning</h2>

Ask the same question of any proposed table: what query hits it the most, and what does that query know? Here the answer is the redirect, and it knows the code and nothing else. That one answer is the whole schema decision. Everything else is a side table.

**The access patterns, ranked:**

1. **Get link by code:** 300,000 a second at peak, before caching. Single key, no joins, no ranges.
2. **Insert link if code absent:** 3,000 a second at peak.
3. **List a user's links, newest first:** a dashboard page view, rare by comparison.
4. **Find links expiring today:** a batch job (Chapter 14).

**Schema:**

```text
links        PRIMARY KEY (code)
             code · long_url · owner_id · created_at · expires_at · status · is_alias
user_links   PRIMARY KEY (owner_id, created_at DESC, code)       -- "my links", newest first
idempotency  PRIMARY KEY (owner_id, idem_key)  TTL 24 h          -- request fingerprint + response
```

The `links` DDL is in Chapter 10. The user index is a second table written beside the link, and paging it uses a **cursor** (the last row seen), not `OFFSET`, which gets slower the deeper you page:

```sql
CREATE TABLE user_links (
  owner_id   BIGINT NOT NULL,
  created_at DATETIME NOT NULL,
  code       VARCHAR(32) CHARACTER SET ascii COLLATE ascii_bin NOT NULL,
  PRIMARY KEY (owner_id, created_at, code)
);

-- next page, given the last row of the previous page
```

**Which store?** Both answers below are defensible. The senior move is to say why for *this* access pattern.

| | Key-value / wide-column (DynamoDB, Cassandra-style) | Relational (MySQL or Postgres, sharded) |
|---|---|---|
| Fit for "get by key" | Exactly what it is built for | Fine: a primary-key lookup |
| 91 TB, growing | Partitioning and rebalancing are built in | Needs a sharding layer (application code, Vitess, Citus) |
| Conditional insert | Conditional put; cost varies by store, so check yours | Unique key: cheap |
| Joins, ad-hoc queries | Few or none | Rich, useful for admin and support tools |
| Operational shape | Managed services remove most of it | You run the shards, or pay someone who does |

**Commit:** "A partitioned key-value store keyed by code. The hot query is a single-key lookup, there are no joins on any request path, and at 91 TB I want partitioning built in. Writes need a cheap conditional put, which I would confirm for the specific store. At Scale A I would just use MySQL or Postgres with a unique key on code. It is 1 TB."

**Partition by the code, hashed.** Every redirect knows the code and nothing else, so each lookup touches exactly one partition. Random codes are already uniformly spread, and hashing the key keeps it uniform even for aliases like `sale` and `sale2`. In a sharded relational setup, route with a hash of the code to a **slot** (for example 4,096 slots mapped to shards) rather than `hash % number_of_shards`, so adding a shard moves a few slots instead of reshuffling nearly every key. The <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-partitioning/`}>partitioning lesson</a> covers how partitions are placed and moved.

| Partition key | Redirect lookup | "List my links" | Writes | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hashed code** | One partition | Needs a side table | Even | **Chosen** |
| User id | Must ask every partition (a redirect has no user id) | One partition | Skewed by heavy users | Breaks the hottest query. The <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/databases/`}>databases lesson</a> asks this exact question |
| Creation date | One partition if date is known, but it is not | Range scan | Every write hits today's partition | Hot spot by design |

**Worked numbers:** 91 TB ÷ about 2 TB per partition ≈ **46 partitions**, and with 3 replicas about **274 TB raw**. At 3,000 writes a second that is about 65 writes a second per partition, which is nothing. Reads that miss the caches, at a 99% hit ratio, are about 3,000 a second across the whole store (Chapter 13), also nothing. **This store is sized by bytes, not by requests.** Say that sentence.

**Durability.** "An acknowledged link is never lost" means returning `201` only after a quorum of replicas (two of three) has the write, or after a synchronous replica has it. A link someone has already pasted into a chat must survive any single node dying a millisecond later.

**The user index may lag; the links table may not.** A dashboard that shows a new link a second late hurts nobody, so `user_links` can be written asynchronously or fed by a change stream from `links`. The links table is the truth.

<h2 id="redirect-path">Chapter 13 · The redirect path</h2>

A load test makes the problem concrete. Imagine one link posted to forty million followers. Traffic climbs to 50,000 redirects a second for one code. The Redis cluster holds and the redirect fleet holds, but one Redis shard sits at 100% CPU while the other eleven idle. The cache was sharded, so why is one box doing all the work?

The <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/caching/`}>caching lesson's</a> rule is that a cache is four promises, spoken, or it does not count. For the redirect cache:

1. **Key shape:** `link:<code>` maps to a compact value: long URL, expires-at, status. A few hundred bytes.
2. **TTL, and why:** **24 hours**, capped at the link's remaining life, `min(24 h, expires_at − now)`, with ±10% jitter. Links never change, so the TTL is not about staleness. It is about **memory**: it lets links nobody clicks any more leave the cache.
3. **Invalidation:** links are **immutable**, so the only events are *disable* and *delete*. Both delete the key in Redis, broadcast a delete to the L1 caches (or just wait for their one-second TTL), and purge the CDN if the redirect was edge-cached. This is the rare cache with almost nothing to invalidate, and that is why the design is easy.
4. **Failure plan:** the cache is **load-bearing**. At 300,000 redirects a second against a store read budget of about 30,000, the store survives a 90% hit ratio but not a cold cache. So: Redis replicas with failover, request coalescing so a cold key is fetched once per server, the L1 and CDN still absorbing hot links while Redis warms, and shedding (fast 503s for new requests) rather than letting the store collapse.

**Who loads data after a miss?** The redirect service does: on a miss it reads the store and writes Redis itself. That is **cache-aside**. In **read-through** the cache layer itself calls the store. The distinction matters when you ask who owns the TTL cap and the negative entries: here, the service does.

The same flow in Python. Note that expiry and status are checked on every read, whichever layer answered:

```python
import json, random, time
from cachetools import TTLCache            # in-process L1

L1 = TTLCache(maxsize=100_000, ttl=1)      # one second bounds staleness after a disable
MISSING = {"missing": True}

def resolve(code: str):
    entry = L1.get(code)
    if entry is None:
        entry = L1[code] = load_entry(code)     # coalesce concurrent loads per code in production
    if entry.get("missing"):
        return 404, None
    if entry["status"] != "active" or (entry["exp"] and entry["exp"] <= time.time()):
        return 410, None
    return 302, entry["url"]

def load_entry(code: str) -> dict:
    raw, not_found = redis.mget(f"link:{code}", f"nf:{code}")
    if raw:
        return json.loads(raw)
    if not_found:
        return MISSING
    row = db_get(code)                          # SELECT long_url, expires_at, status FROM links WHERE code = %s
    if row is None:
        redis.set(f"nf:{code}", 1, ex=60)       # negative cache; DEL it when this code is created
        return MISSING
    entry = {"url": row.long_url, "exp": to_epoch(row.expires_at), "status": row.status}
    ttl = int(86_400 * random.uniform(0.9, 1.1))
    if entry["exp"]:
        ttl = min(ttl, int(entry["exp"] - time.time()))
    if ttl > 0:
        redis.set(f"link:{code}", json.dumps(entry), ex=ttl)
    return entry
```

The Redis side of it:

```redis
SET link:aZ3kQ9x "{\"url\":\"https://shop.example.com/a\",\"exp\":1798761599,\"status\":\"active\"}" EX 86400
SET nf:zzzzzzz 1 EX 60
DEL nf:aZ3kQ9x        # when the code is created
DEL link:aZ3kQ9x      # when the link is disabled or deleted
```

**The hit-ratio arithmetic.** The store sees `(1 − h) × 300,000` reads a second:

| Hit ratio | Store reads at peak | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 90% | 30,000/s | Exactly the read budget, no headroom (estimation's Scale B number) |
| **99%** | **3,000/s** | **The target** |
| 99.9% | 300/s | What a good day looks like |

99% is realistic because clicks are concentrated: a link is clicked mostly in the hours and days after it is shared. **Sizing the cache:** estimation's upper bound puts *every* link from the last 30 days in cache at full row size, 3B links × 500 B = **1.5 TB**. A cache entry is smaller (call it about 250 B with Redis overhead, an assumption to measure), and most clicks land in a link's first week, so a working set of 7 days × 100M × 250 B ≈ **175 GB** is a better plan: about **8 nodes** at about 25 GB usable each, with LRU or LFU evicting the cold tail. Throughput: 300,000 reads a second at very roughly 100,000 simple operations per node is 3 nodes' worth, so the cluster is sized by **memory**, and its replicas by availability.

**What happens when the cache restarts empty at peak?** Every request misses at once and 300,000 reads a second go to a store budgeted for 30,000. This is a stampede. Try it below: with no mitigation the store is overwhelmed, while coalescing and warming protect it.

**The hot link.** One code hashes to one Redis shard, so adding shards does not help (the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/caching/#hot-keys`}>caching lesson's</a> celebrity). The simulation below shows one key taking half the traffic. Fixes, in order:

- **In-process L1 cache, about 1 second TTL**, on every redirect server. With 45 servers, Redis sees at most about 45 reads a second for the hot code instead of 50,000. Staleness costs nothing, since the link cannot change, and the one-second TTL bounds how long a *disabled* link keeps redirecting.
- **Request coalescing:** concurrent misses for the same code on one server share one fetch.
- **The edge:** let the CDN cache the redirect itself.

**Edge redirects, the lever and its price.** Send the 302 with a shared-cache lifetime that only the CDN obeys, and the edge answers repeat clicks without reaching you. A viral link's load then dies at hundreds of edge locations.

| Header | Who obeys it |
|---|---|
| `Cache-Control: max-age=N` | Browsers **and** shared caches (CDNs) |
| `Cache-Control: s-maxage=N` | Shared caches only (CDNs), overriding `max-age` for them |
| `Cache-Control: private` | Browsers only. Shared caches must not store it |
| `CDN-Cache-Control: max-age=N` (RFC 9213) | The CDN only, where your provider supports it |

For example `Cache-Control: private, max-age=0` plus `CDN-Cache-Control: max-age=60` keeps browsers asking while the CDN holds the redirect for 60 seconds (check your CDN's documentation for which headers it honours). The price:

- **Clicks served at the edge never reach your service.** Analytics must come from the CDN's request logs (most large CDNs can stream them, so check your provider) or from code running at the edge.
- **Takedown needs a purge,** and is bounded by the 60 seconds if the purge is slow.

**Commit:** "Redirects go through the CDN for TLS and anycast, but by default the edge does not cache them, so every click reaches our fleet and our analytics. The L1 cache handles hot links inside our data centre. If fleet cost or a DDoS made edge caching worthwhile, I would turn it on for hot codes only, with a 60-second CDN lifetime, and take those clicks from the CDN's log stream."

**Negative caching.** Scrapers and typos request codes that do not exist, and each one misses every layer and reaches the store. Cache "not found" for a short time (say 60 seconds), and delete that entry when a code is created, or a brand-new link reads as missing for a minute (Chapter 17 adds the cross-region wrinkle). A Bloom filter of existing codes in front of the store can also answer "definitely not present" cheaply. It is an optimisation for later, it must be updated on every create, and it can never replace the store, because "maybe present" still needs a lookup.

<h2 id="expiry">Chapter 14 · Expiry</h2>

A sale ends at midnight. At 12:04 a.m. a customer emails a screenshot: the sale link still works, and the checkout page it lands on says the prices are gone. The cleanup job runs at 2 a.m., and the cache entry had a 24-hour TTL. Who was supposed to know it was midnight?

**Expiry is a read-path check, not a cleanup job.** Correctness cannot wait for a batch.

- **Store `expires_at` in the link and in every cached copy.** Every layer checks it on read: expired means **410 Gone**. (404 means "never existed", 410 means "existed, deliberately gone". It is more honest, and it tells crawlers to stop.)
- **Cap every cache TTL** at the time left: `min(24 h, expires_at − now)`. The entry leaves the cache on time, so the check above is a backstop, not the only defence.
- **Edge copies** carry the same cap: `s-maxage` never exceeds the time left.

<Figure caption="Three independent defences. Correctness comes from the first two, which run on every read. The sweeper only reclaims storage.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    R["Read at any layer"] --> C1{"now past expires_at?"}
    C1 -->|"yes"| G["410 Gone"]
    C1 -->|"no"| OK["302"]
    W["Cache write"] --> C2["TTL = min of 24 h and time left"]
    S["Daily sweeper"] --> T["status = expired, keep tombstone"]`} />
</Figure>

**Cleanup is about storage, and it is lazy.** Rows for expired links cost bytes, not correctness. Either let the store's **native TTL** delete them (several key-value stores offer per-item expiry, but deletion is typically delayed, sometimes by days, so check the store's guarantees; that is fine precisely because reads already check `expires_at`), or run a sweeper over an index of links by expiry time:

```sql
CREATE INDEX idx_links_expiry ON links (expires_at);

-- mark, do not delete: the row stays as a tombstone
UPDATE links SET status = 'expired'
WHERE status = 'active' AND expires_at <= NOW()
ORDER BY expires_at
LIMIT 1000;                      -- batch, repeat until zero rows
```

**Never reuse a code.** This one bites random generation specifically. If cleanup *deletes* the row, the code is free again, and the minter will eventually draw it. Then an old copy of the link, in an email from years ago or a printed poster, opens a stranger's destination. So cleanup leaves a **tombstone**: the code with status `expired` and no URL (a few dozen bytes), which keeps the conditional insert failing for that code forever. With 19× headroom there is no reason to recycle and every reason not to. A counter never re-issues on its own, but aliases can be re-claimed, so tombstones matter there too.

<Trace title="Expire on time" steps={expiry} takeaway="Check expires_at on every read, cap every TTL at the time left, clean up lazily, and tombstone the code so it can never mean something else." />

<Reveal q="A user deletes a link by mistake and asks for it back. Can you restore it?">

If you tombstoned it, yes. The code is still reserved and nobody else can have taken it, so restoring is flipping the status back (and the URL, if you kept it in a soft-delete window). That is another argument for tombstones over deletion, and for keeping the long URL for a grace period, say 30 days, before trimming the tombstone to just the code.

</Reveal>

**A common slip:** "A cron job deletes expired links every night." Then a link expires at midnight and redirects until the job runs, and if the job deletes the row, the code is up for grabs.

<h2 id="analytics">Chapter 15 · Analytics without slowing redirects</h2>

The first analytics prototype is two lines in the redirect handler: increment a counter row, then redirect. It works beautifully on a test server. Under the viral-link load test, 50,000 increments a second land on one row. Lock waits climb, and the redirect p99, which was two milliseconds, becomes four hundred. Every click now waits so that it can be counted.

**The rule: the redirect must never wait on analytics.** Counting is a stretch goal and redirecting is the product. So the redirect service:

1. Sends the 302.
2. Appends a click event to an **in-memory buffer** (no I/O on the request).
3. A background thread ships buffered events to a **partitioned log** (a Kafka-style stream) in batches.

Downstream, a **stream aggregator** rolls events into counts per code per minute (and per country and referrer), writing to a store built for counters and time ranges, the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/pattern-timeseries/`}>time-series lesson's</a> territory. The dashboard reads the rollups, never the raw events.

**Three ways to count, compared:**

| Approach | Cost on the redirect | Hot-link behaviour | Accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|
| `UPDATE links SET clicks = clicks + 1` in the handler | A synchronous write per click | One hot row, lock waits | Exact, but the product waits on it |
| One event per click to the log | A memory append | One hot log partition (50,000/s) | At-least-once, minutes late |
| **Pre-aggregate per server per second, then log** | A memory increment | 45 records a second per hot code | Approximate, minutes late |

The pre-aggregating counter, in Python. It touches only memory on the request path:

```python
import threading, time
from collections import Counter

class ClickCounter:
    def __init__(self, ship, interval=1.0):
        self.ship, self.interval = ship, interval      # ship(list of (code, count, ts)) does the network I/O
        self.counts, self.lock = Counter(), threading.Lock()
        threading.Thread(target=self._loop, daemon=True).start()

    def record(self, code: str) -> None:               # called after the 302 is sent
        with self.lock:
            self.counts[code] += 1                     # memory only, no I/O

    def _loop(self):
        while True:
            time.sleep(self.interval)
            with self.lock:
                batch, self.counts = self.counts, Counter()
            if batch:
                now = int(time.time())
                self.ship([(code, n, now) for code, n in batch.items()])   # one record per code per second
```

An event for the raw stream looks like this (country comes from the edge, the client id is hashed so no raw IP is stored):

```json
{"event_id": "01HX...", "code": "aZ3kQ9x", "ts": 1767225600, "country": "DE", "referrer": "chat.example", "client": "c91f..."}
```

**Worked numbers.** 300,000 clicks a second at peak × about 200 B per event ≈ **60 MB/s** into the log, a modest stream cluster. 10B events a day × 200 B ≈ **2 TB a day** raw, which goes to cheap object storage for reprocessing. The rollups are tiny by comparison.

**The hot link, again.** If events are partitioned by code, the viral link's 50,000 events a second land on one partition. Same disease, same cure: **pre-aggregate on the redirect server**, so 45 servers send 45 records a second for that code. Per-click detail, if anyone needs it, goes to raw storage partitioned randomly.

**What the counts promise.** Buffering means a crashed redirect server loses its last second or so of events. The log's at-least-once delivery means a retry can count an event twice. So: **approximately exact, minutes late.** If billing depends on clicks, give each event an id and deduplicate in the aggregator. Filter bots (known crawler user agents, link-preview fetchers in chat apps) before anyone calls the number "clicks", since a chat app's preview fetch is a request, not a human. Treat client identifiers as personal data: hash them, set a retention period, and check the privacy rules that apply to you.

<h2 id="abuse">Chapter 16 · Abuse</h2>

A week after launch, a bank's security team writes in. A text message is going round: "Your parcel is held. Pay the 1.99 fee: sho.rt/pX7r2Qa." The link lands on a perfect copy of a delivery company's payment page. The service's own domain is now the most trustworthy-looking part of a phishing kit.

**A shortener hides the destination by design.** That makes it an abuse tool by default. Four layers:

1. **Validate on create.** `http` and `https` only, never `javascript:` or `data:`. A length cap (a few KB). Refuse URLs on your own short domain, which create redirect loops and launder other short links.
2. **Check reputation on create, and again later.** Look the URL up against a threat-intelligence list (Google Safe Browsing is a well-known public one) before issuing the code. Re-check periodically and on report, because **the destination can change after you check it**: a clean page on Monday can be a phishing kit by Wednesday.
3. **Limit creation.** Per-account and per-IP creation limits at the gateway, tighter for new and anonymous accounts: a token bucket per identity, 429 with Retry-After. The <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/rate-limiter/`}>rate limiter walkthrough</a> is that whole design, and here it is one sentence. Bulk creation through the API needs a verified account.
4. **Kill fast.** A takedown sets status `disabled`, deletes the Redis key, waits out (or broadcasts to) the one-second L1 caches, purges the CDN if needed, and serves a **warning page** (or 410) instead of the redirect. This is where Chapter 4 pays off: **you cannot purge a browser that cached a 301.**

```python
from urllib.parse import urlsplit

OWN_HOSTS = {"sho.rt", "www.sho.rt"}

def validate_long_url(url: str) -> str:
    if len(url) > 2048:
        raise ValueError("too long")
    parts = urlsplit(url)
    if parts.scheme not in ("http", "https") or not parts.hostname:
        raise ValueError("only http and https URLs")
    if parts.hostname.lower() in OWN_HOSTS:
        raise ValueError("cannot shorten our own links")
    return url
```

If the service itself fetches the destination (for reputation checks or link previews), treat that as an outbound request to untrusted input. Block private and internal address ranges, or an attacker can use your fetcher to probe your own network (server-side request forgery).

**An optional interstitial.** For links flagged as suspicious but not confirmed, show a preview page ("You are about to visit ... continue?") instead of redirecting. It costs a click for real users, so keep it for the doubtful ones.

<h2 id="regions">Chapter 17 · Multi-region</h2>

Suppose the service opens regions in Frankfurt and Mumbai. A seller in Mumbai shortens a listing and pastes it into a chat with a buyer in Frankfurt, who taps it within a second. Frankfurt has never heard of the code, because replication from Mumbai takes two seconds. The buyer sees a 404, taps again, and sees the 404 again, because Frankfurt cached the miss. The link exists everywhere. Just not yet.

**Reads go everywhere, writes need a plan.** Links are immutable and read 100× more than written, which is the friendliest possible shape for multi-region: replicate the links table **asynchronously** to every region, and serve every redirect from the nearest one. Geo-routing (anycast or latency-based DNS, the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/load-balancing/#global`}>load-balancing lesson's</a> global tier) sends each click to its closest region.

**Problem 1: two regions minting the same code.** A conditional insert is atomic *within one copy of the data*. If Mumbai and Frankfurt each mint codes and replicate asynchronously, both can claim `aZ3kQ9x` in the same second, each insert succeeds locally, and replication later picks a "winner" while one link silently points somewhere else. Two fixes:

| | One home region for creates | Split the code space by region |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | All creates go to one region's database | Each region mints only codes in its own slice (for example the first character modulo 3) |
| Uniqueness | One conditional write, as before | A local conditional write is globally unique, because slices are disjoint |
| Create latency | A cross-region round trip (call it 100–200 ms, fine for a create) | Local |
| If that region fails | Creates pause or fail over | Other regions keep creating |
| Costs | Home region is a create SPOF | Code reveals the region, and slices fill unevenly if traffic is uneven |

The home-region design is simpler, and is the default here.

**Problem 2: a new link clicked before it arrives.** On a local miss, do not answer 404 yet: **ask the home region** (or the region that owns the code's slice) before deciding. That costs one cross-region read, only for codes that are both missing locally *and* brand new, which is rare. Only negative-cache the answer *after* the home region also says "not found". This turns "a second of replication lag" from a broken product into a slightly slower first click.

```python
def load_entry_multi_region(code: str) -> dict:
    entry = local_lookup(code)                 # cache, then the local replica
    if entry is not None:
        return entry
    entry = home_region_lookup(code)           # one cross-region read, short timeout
    if entry is None:
        redis.set(f"nf:{code}", 1, ex=60)      # negative-cache only after home also said no
        return MISSING
    return entry
```

<h2 id="wrap-up">Chapter 18 · The last three minutes</h2>

Reviewers often read design docs from the bottom up, because the last section tells them whether the author has ever been paged. The wrap-up is where you show that. Five parts:

1. **Recap against the requirements.** "Shorten with an idempotency key; redirect with a 302 in a millisecond or two from cache; aliases and expiry through the same table; 7-character random codes, unique by a conditional write; a partitioned store sized by bytes; clicks counted off the hot path."
2. **Bottleneck at 10×** (1B links a day, 3M redirects a second). The redirect fleet and the cache scale out linearly, and edge caching becomes the default rather than a lever. **The code length flips:** 1B a day for five years fills 52% of 62⁷, so random codes would need about two draws per link. Move to **8 characters** (0.84% full). Storage reaches about 910 TB over five years, and partitions grow from about 46 to about 460.
3. **Failure modes.** Redis loses a shard: L1 and the CDN hold the hot set, the store takes the misses for the cold tail, shed if it cannot. The store loses a node: replicas serve, and creates need a quorum. The home region goes down: redirects continue everywhere (reads are local) and creates pause or fail over. The click pipeline stalls: counts are late, redirects unaffected.
4. **Monitor.** Redirect p99 and error rate; hit ratio at each layer (L1, Redis, CDN); store reads per second (a rising number means the cache is failing quietly); 404 and 410 rates per IP (scraping); **create retry rate** (rising means the space is filling, the early warning for 8 characters); click-pipeline lag; abuse reports and takedown latency.
5. **What I would build next.** Per-user dedup ("you already shortened this"), link previews, the analytics dashboard, private long-token links, and editable destinations with proper invalidation.

**The one-breath version:** "At 10× the cache and redirect fleet scale out, edge caching becomes default, and codes go to eight characters. I would watch redirect p99, per-layer hit ratio and create retries."

Answer it at three depths. Say your own answer aloud before you reveal each one.

**Five follow-ups you will get:**

<Reveal q="1 · 301 or 302, and does it really matter?">

It decides who sees the click. A 301 is cacheable by default, so a browser can skip us on the next click: less load, but we lose the count, and if we disable a malicious link, every browser that cached the 301 keeps going there. We cannot purge a browser. A 302 is not cached unless we say so, so every click reaches us. I would return 302 with `Cache-Control: private, max-age=0`. If analytics did not matter, a 301 with a bounded max-age is reasonable.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="2 · Walk me through how you generate the code. Why that length?">

182.5 billion links in five years. 62⁶ is only 56.8 billion, too few. 62⁷ is 3.5 trillion, about 5% full. I draw seven characters from a secure random generator and write with insert-if-absent. On conflict (about one draw in 19 at five years) I redraw, up to five times, and alert if that limit is ever hit. Alternatives: counter ranges (each server reserves 10,000 ids from a durable counter, encoded in base62 and scrambled with a keyed permutation so the order does not leak), which has no retries but a counter to run. Hashing collides and makes users share codes. Snowflake is 10–11 characters.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="3 · A celebrity posts a link. What breaks?">

The link hashes to one Redis shard, so that shard takes all of it, and adding shards does not help. A one-second in-process cache on each of the 45 redirect servers caps Redis at about 45 reads a second for that code. Links do not change, so staleness is free. Concurrent misses on one server are coalesced. If the fleet itself is the cost, the CDN caches the redirect for 60 seconds and we count those clicks from its logs. Analytics has the same hot-partition problem, solved the same way: pre-count per server per second.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="4 · How do you count clicks without slowing redirects?">

The redirect sends its 302, then appends an event to an in-memory buffer, with no I/O on the request. A background thread ships batches to a partitioned log, a stream job rolls them up per code per minute into a counter store, and raw events go to object storage. About 60 MB/s at peak. Counts are minutes late and approximately exact: a crash loses a second or so of buffered events and retries can double-count, so I would add event ids and dedupe if billing depended on it.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="5 · Run it in three regions. What changes?">

Links are immutable and read-mostly, so they replicate asynchronously everywhere and every redirect is served locally. Uniqueness is the catch: two regions minting independently could both claim a code, since a conditional insert is atomic only within one copy. So creates go to a home region (a create can afford 150 ms) or each region mints from its own slice of the code space. And a local miss checks the home region before returning 404, so a link clicked a second after creation in another region still works.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="6 · Redis is down entirely. What happens, and what do you do?">

Every L1 miss goes to the store. The L1 caches (1 second) and any edge copies absorb the hot links, and coalescing limits duplicate loads, but the cold tail now reads the store directly. The store is budgeted for about 30,000 reads a second against 300,000 redirects, so the design must shed: return fast 503s for requests beyond a store read budget rather than letting the store fall over. Redirects for hot links continue, creates are unaffected. Prevention: replicas with failover, and a warm-up plan before sending full traffic back.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="7 · How would you debug a sudden spike in 404s?">

First look at the source: per-IP and per-user-agent counts. A wall of misses from a few IPs is a scraper, so rate-limit and, if needed, block. Spread across many IPs for codes in a narrow range suggests a broken client, or a dead campaign. Then look for a replication lag or a failed create path: are the missing codes ones that were just created? If so, check the home-region fallback and the negative-cache delete on create. Negative caching keeps the store from feeling it either way.

</Reveal>

|---|---|
| "Design a URL shortener" | Shorten + redirect; aliases, expiry small; analytics stretch; redirects are the product |

| Sequential codes leak | Keyed permutation (Feistel + cycle walking; FF1 for real secrecy) or random codes |
| Random codes | Secure RNG + insert-if-absent; draws = 1 ÷ (1 − fill) |
| Uniqueness | One conditional write, never check-then-insert |
| MySQL | Plain INSERT and catch 1062; not INSERT IGNORE, ON DUPLICATE KEY UPDATE or REPLACE; ascii_bin collation |
| Custom alias | Same table, same conditional insert, 409 if taken, reserved words |
| 301 vs 302 | 301 cached by browsers: lost clicks, no takedown; 302 + explicit Cache-Control |
| Storage | KV partitioned by hashed code; user_links side table; sized by bytes |
| Redirect cache | `link:code`, 24 h capped at expiry, delete on disable, load-bearing |
| Viral link | 1 s in-process L1, coalescing, then edge caching with CDN logs |
| Missing codes | Short negative cache, rate-limit 404s per IP |
| Expiry | Check on read, cap TTLs at time left, lazy cleanup, tombstone, never reuse |

| 10× | Fleet and cache scale out; edge caching default; 8 characters |

## Common mistakes

1. **"301, because links are permanent."** You just gave every browser your analytics and your kill switch.
2. **"Check if the code exists, then insert."** A race. One conditional write.
3. **"Hash the URL, collisions are astronomically unlikely."** After 2.2 million links it is a coin flip.
4. **"Cache the redirects in Redis."** Key, TTL and why, invalidation, failure plan, and the hot link.
5. **Counting clicks inside the redirect.** The product now waits on the stretch goal.
6. **Treating short codes as secrets.** At 5% fill, a random guess finds a link one time in 19.
7. **Deleting expired rows.** The code is free again, and old copies open someone else's page.

- [Flickr: Ticket Servers: Distributed Unique Primary Keys on the Cheap](https://code.flickr.net/2010/02/08/ticket-servers-distributed-unique-primary-keys-on-the-cheap/): the classic range/odd-even counter write-up behind Chapter 8.

- [Google Safe Browsing](https://developers.google.com/safe-browsing): a public threat list for checking URLs at creation and on re-scan.

**Look these up yourself**

- **"Gone in Six Characters: Short URLs Considered Harmful for Cloud Services"** (Georgiev and Shmatikov, 2016): the research that enumerated real short links and found private cloud documents behind them.
- **Format-preserving encryption and cycle walking:** how FF1/FF3-1 encrypt a number into the same domain, and why cycle walking keeps the result a permutation.
- **Bloom filters for "probably taken" pre-checks:** when a local filter of used codes saves store round trips, and why it can never replace the conditional write.
- **Lightweight transactions / conditional writes in wide-column stores:** what a compare-and-set costs in your chosen store, which decides random versus counter.
- **MySQL collations (`ascii_bin` vs `utf8mb4_*_ci`):** how a collation decides what a unique key considers equal.
- **HyperLogLog:** counting *unique* clickers per link in a few kilobytes, the natural next analytics feature.
- **Link-preview fetchers and bot filtering:** how chat apps fetch links to render previews, and why that inflates naive click counts.
- **Open redirects:** why a redirect endpoint that accepts arbitrary destinations is a security finding in other products, and what a shortener does differently (ownership, reputation, takedown).
- **Server-side request forgery (SSRF):** why any service that fetches user-supplied URLs must block internal address ranges.
