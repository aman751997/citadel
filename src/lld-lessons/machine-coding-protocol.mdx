<h2 id="prologue">Prologue · “Show me”</h2>

<Scene chapter="Prologue" title="Show me" mood="dawn">

Wren's Friday mock rounds happen in the small meeting room with the broken blind. She sets a kitchen timer to ninety minutes, slides a card across the table — *Parking lot. Park, unpark, pay.* — and goes back to her own work.

You are good at this. By minute fifty you have eleven classes: an abstract `Vehicle` hierarchy, a `ParkingFloor` with a builder, a `PaymentProcessor` interface with three implementations, and a beautiful enum. By minute eighty you are wiring them together. At minute eighty-nine the compiler finds a generic type mismatch you don't have time to understand.

The timer rings. Wren looks up.

*"Show me,"* she says.

There is nothing to show. Eleven classes, and not one ticket has ever been issued.

</Scene>

Machine-coding rounds fail people for boring reasons: **code that doesn't run**, requirements silently skipped, no demonstrable output, and forty minutes spent on a database nobody asked for. Design talent rarely decides the result. The protocol below is a time-boxed defence against each of those failures. Learn its shape; the numbers flex with the round's length (a 60-minute round shrinks each box by a third, a 2-hour take-home stretches the core flow).

A machine-coding round is not a whiteboard LLD interview. You write **real, running code** for a small system — a parking lot, a lending library, an elevator, a splitwise — in 60 to 120 minutes, usually in your own IDE, sometimes on a shared screen. At the end, someone runs it. Then they ask for one more feature, and watch how much of your code has to move.

<Figure caption="The protocol on one line. The gold box is the one that must never be skipped: one flow, running end to end, before anything broad.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart LR
    A["0–10\nClarify + cut list"] --> B["10–20\nEntities +\nclass diagram"]
    B --> C["20–30\nInterfaces +\nskeleton compiles"]
    C --> D["30–60\nCORE FLOW\none demo path running"]
    D --> E["60–75\nFollow-up +\nedge cases"]
    E --> F["75–85\nTests +\ndemo main"]
    F --> G["85–90\nBuffer +\nwalkthrough"]
    style D stroke:#e3b341,stroke-width:3px`} />
</Figure>

Three ideas carry the whole lesson:

1. **Something runs at every checkpoint.** If the round ends early — or you stall — you still have a demo. Bad days cost features, never the whole submission.
2. **Scope is a decision you say out loud.** Cutting is senior; cutting silently reads as forgetting.
3. **The follow-up is the exam.** Every round is designed around one extra requirement. If it lands as a new class plugged into a seam you built on purpose, the design passed. If it edits five methods, it didn't.

<h2 id="the-clock">Chapter 1 · Ninety minutes on the wall</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 1" title="Ninety minutes on the wall">

The next Friday, Wren doesn't give you a problem. She gives you a sheet of paper with seven boxes on it and a minute range above each.

*"Your problem last week wasn't Java,"* she says. *"It was that you never knew what time it was. Tape this above your monitor."*

"And when I'm behind?"

*"You'll know you're behind at minute thirty, not at minute eighty-nine. That's the whole point."*

</Scene>

Here is the clock, with what to have **done** at the end of each box, and the sentence to **say out loud** while you do it. Graders can only credit reasoning they can see or hear — narration is not decoration, it is how the "communication" line on the sheet gets filled.

| Minutes | Phase | Done when | Say it aloud |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0–10** | Clarify and scope | Must / should / won't list read back and agreed | *"Let me check what must work at the demo… so: borrow and return are musts, the overdue report is a should, payments and holds are out. Sound right?"* |
| **10–20** | Entities and class diagram | Nouns sorted into entities and values; every rule has an owner; a diagram anyone can read in 90 seconds | *"Copy is an entity — it has an id and a status that changes. Loan owns its due date. The fee rule varies, so it goes behind an interface."* |
| **20–30** | Interfaces and skeleton | The whole shape **compiles**, with stub bodies that throw | *"I'm writing the interfaces for what varies and for storage first, so the follow-up has a slot waiting for it."* |
| **30–60** | Core flow | The one demo path runs end to end by ~50; first edge cases by 60 | *"I'll get borrow working end to end before touching return — a narrow slice that runs."* |
| **60–75** | Follow-up and edges | The Interviewer's extension lands; edge cases throw domain exceptions | *"This lands as a new policy class; nothing that already works reopens."* |
| **75–85** | Tests and demo | A self-test and a scripted `main`: happy path plus two edge cases | *"main runs the self-test, then tells the story: borrow, late return, two rejections."* |
| **85–90** | Buffer and walkthrough | The grader has been shown the seams, the cut list, and what's next | *"If I had another hour: holds as an observer on returns, and per-title locks."* |

**Why the boxes are those sizes.** Clarifying past ten minutes has diminishing returns — ask what changes the design, then commit. The diagram is where wrong models die cheaply, so it gets its own box; ten minutes on paper saves thirty in code. Interfaces go *before* the core flow because they make the whole shape compile at minute 30, and from then on you never have a non-compiling file for more than a few minutes. The core flow gets the biggest box because it is the only one whose failure is fatal. The buffer exists because something will go wrong, and because the walkthrough at the end is your last chance to point the grader at your best decisions.

**The 30-minute rule of thumb for any length of round:** about a third of the time on understanding and shape, a third on the core, a third on extension, tests and demo. In a 60-minute round: 5 / 7 / 8 / 20 / 10 / 7 / 3.

<Trace title="What time is it?" steps={clock} takeaway="Each box has a deliverable. Know at minute 30 whether the skeleton compiles, at minute 50 whether the core path runs — and spend the last five minutes guiding the grader, not typing." />

**Catch the trap:** *"I'll make up the time later."* There is no later. The core flow box is where every overrun lands, and an overrun there is the one that leaves you with nothing to demo. When a box runs long, **cut scope from the should list immediately and say so** (Chapter 11).

<h2 id="the-rubric">Chapter 2 · The grader's sheet</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 2" title="The grader's sheet" mood="dusk">

After the mock round, Wren turns over the sheet she has been writing on. It is a grid. Down the side: nine lines. Across the top: *strong*, *ok*, *weak*.

You scored *strong* on one line — "design" — and *weak* on six.

"But the design was the hard part," you say.

*"The design is one line,"* Wren says. *"Count the others."*

</Scene>

Companies word their sheets differently, and nobody publishes theirs, but the same dimensions show up on almost every machine-coding rubric. Read this table as a checklist of what the person across the table is looking for — and notice how many lines have nothing to do with cleverness.

| Dimension | What earns it | What kills it | When they check |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Working code** | Compiles, runs, core flow demonstrable, edge cases printed | Compile errors, *"it would work if…"* | The demo, minute 85+ |
| **Requirement coverage** | Everything promised in the cut list, cut list honoured | Silent skips | Against the card and your list |
| **Extensibility under the follow-up** | The follow-up lands as a new class in a seam you built on purpose | The follow-up edits five methods | Minute 60–75, live |
| **Separation of concerns** | Entities own their invariants; service orchestrates thinly; strategies decide what varies | God class, anemic model, pattern soup | Reading your class list |
| **Naming and readability** | Domain names (`borrow`, `Loan`, `dueOn`), small methods, no dead code | `Manager`/`Helper`/`data2`, 300-line methods, `catch (Exception e) {}` | Reading any file cold |
| **Edge cases and errors** | Domain exceptions with ids in the message; invalid id, full, double operation all handled | `null` returns, booleans that lose the reason, crashes on bad input | Your demo, then their own input |
| **Concurrency awareness** | You can name the race and fix it simply when asked | *"ConcurrentHashMap makes it safe"* | A question, usually late |
| **Tests** | A few fast tests on the core flow and the invariants | None, or twenty tests and no feature | Your `main` or test class |
| **Communication** | Narrated decisions, honest trade-offs, announced cuts | Forty minutes of silent typing | The whole round |

**Working code is a gate, not a line.** A design that doesn't compile is a design nobody can verify, so many graders cap the whole submission when the demo doesn't run. That is why the protocol keeps the code compiling from minute 30 onward: a late mistake should cost a feature, never the build.

**Over-engineering is scored, too.** YAGNI is a grading criterion. More interfaces than implementations, a dependency-injection container in a 90-minute round, six patterns where two would do — these lose the "separation of concerns" line as surely as a God class does, because they make the code harder to read and slower to change. Patterns must be *forced* by a real problem; the entire <a href={`${BASE}lld/war/patterns-why/`}>War Room</a> is a catalogue of what forcing looks like.

<Trace title="Read the sheet" steps={rubric} takeaway="Working code is the gate; the follow-up is the extensibility exam; and an in-memory round grades no database. Optimise for the lines on the sheet, not for the hardest-looking class." />

<Reveal q="Two of the nine lines are checked only at the very end — working code and tests. What does that imply about the order you build in?">

That they must be **protected from the start**, not added at the end. Keep the build green from minute 30 so "working code" can't be lost to a late compile error, and write the tiny test helpers early (they are part of the skeleton in Chapter 7) so that adding a test at minute 75 costs one line each. Anything checked last but built last is the first thing a bad day takes from you.

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** *"The design is what they're really testing."* Design is one line of nine. A modest design that runs, covers its cut list, absorbs the follow-up and handles three edge cases beats a brilliant one that doesn't run — every time, on every sheet.

<h2 id="clarify">Chapter 3 · One cut list</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 3" title="One cut list">

The card says: *Design a library lending system.*

You start typing `class Book`. Wren's hand lands flat on the table beside your keyboard.

*"What does 'lending' mean?"*

"Borrowing and returning."

*"For how long? How many at once? What happens when it's late? Who's a member? What happens when two people want the last copy?"* She lifts her hand. *"You don't know. So you're not typing. You're asking."*

</Scene>

Ask questions until you can state three things: the **actors**, the **core operations**, and what is **out of scope**. Then *say the cut*. Writing the cut list down does two jobs: it shows judgement, and it converts "you missed X" into "X was descoped — do you want it instead of Y?"

**The five questions that fit almost every problem:**

1. **Which operations must demo at the end?** These are your must-haves and get built first.
2. **What varies?** Vehicle types, pricing rules, member tiers, split types — these become your strategy seams.
3. **What must stay consistent?** Money sums, no double-booking, no copy lent twice — your invariants.
4. **Single-threaded or concurrent? In-memory or persistent?** Almost always in-memory; say you're designing so storage can be swapped later. If concurrency is in scope, ask which operations can collide.
5. **Any scale hints?** Only to pick data structures — this is not a high-level design round.

**Then sort everything into must / should / won't** and read it back. A worked version for the library card:

| Must (demo) | Should (if time) | Won't (said out loud) |
|---|---|---|
| Add books and copies; register members | List overdue loans | Payments (fees are computed, not collected) |
| Borrow a title: picks a free copy, sets a due date | Per-tier lending rules (seam now, rules later) | Holds and reservations |
| Return a copy: closes the loan, computes the late fee | | Search, persistence, auth, any UI |
| Reject: no copy free, loan limit reached, unknown ids, returning twice | | Renewals |

**The sentence that closes the phase:** *"So for the demo: borrow, return with a late fee, and four rejections. Overdue list if time allows. Payments, holds, search and persistence are out — the repository is an interface, so storage can be swapped later. Shall I go ahead?"* Wait for the nod. That nod is a contract: from now on, nobody can mark you down for not building holds.

<Figure caption="Scoping as a funnel. Every requirement you hear goes into exactly one bucket — and the won't bucket is said aloud, never left implicit.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart TD
    Q["Everything on the card\n+ answers to your questions"] --> M["MUST\nbuilt first, demoed"]
    Q --> S["SHOULD\nbuilt if time,\nseam ready either way"]
    Q --> W["WON'T\nsaid aloud,\nsketched if asked"]
    S -.->|"behind at minute 45?"| W`} />
</Figure>

<Trace title="Ask the question that changes the design" steps={clarify} takeaway="Ask what must demo, what varies, and what must stay consistent. A hint like “treat them the same for now” is the Interviewer telling you where the follow-up lands." />

<Reveal q="The Interviewer answers a question with “up to you.” What do you do?">

Pick the simpler option, **state it as an assumption**, and write it on the cut list: *"I'll assume one copy can only be on one loan at a time and loans are 14 days. Easy to change — it's in the policy."* "Up to you" is sometimes a test of whether you can make a reasonable call quickly. Never spend more than a minute on it, and never pick the option that needs more code.

</Reveal>

**Catch the trap:** *"Asking lots of questions shows thoroughness."* Ten minutes is the box. Ask the questions whose answers change your classes — what varies, what's consistent, what demos — and assume the rest out loud.

<h2 id="entities">Chapter 4 · Nouns on a napkin</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 4" title="Nouns on a napkin" mood="dusk">

Wren tears a page from her notebook and pushes it across with a pencil. *"No IDE yet. Ten minutes. Boxes and arrows."*

"Isn't it faster to just start coding?"

*"Moving a box on paper takes a second. Moving a class after three others depend on it takes ten minutes. Which do you want to find your mistakes in?"*

</Scene>

**Underline the nouns** in the card and your agreed list, then **mark each one an entity or a value** (the <a href={`${BASE}lld/lessons/object-modeling/`}>object-modeling lesson</a> covers this in depth):

- An **entity** has an identity that survives change: a `Copy` is still the same copy after it is lent. Give it an id; let it own the state that changes and the rules about that state.
- A **value** is defined entirely by its contents and never changes: a `Receipt`, a `Book`'s isbn and title, an amount of money. Make it a `record`.

**Then attach every rule to its owner.** "A copy can't be lent twice" belongs to `Copy`. "A loan can't be closed twice" belongs to `Loan`. "A member may hold at most three loans" spans members and loans, so it belongs to the service that coordinates them. A rule that varies — the late fee — belongs to a **strategy**.

For a parking lot, the same pass looks like this:

| Noun | Entity or value | Owns which rule |
|---|---|---|
| `Vehicle` | value (plate + type) | — |
| `Spot` | entity (id, occupied or not) | "one vehicle per spot", "fits this vehicle type" |
| `Ticket` | entity (id, entry, exit) | "closed once; exit after entry" |
| `Money` | value | arithmetic never goes negative |
| fee rule | strategy | "how much for this stay" — it varies |

**The 90-second class diagram.** Boxes for the entities, a box with `<<interface>>` for each seam, arrows for "uses". Draw it, then *talk through it*: *"ParkingService uses SpotRepository and FeeStrategy; Ticket and Spot own their own guards."* If the Interviewer frowns at an arrow, you just saved twenty minutes.

<Trace title="Who owns the rule?" steps={entities} takeaway="Entities own the state that changes and the invariants about it; values never change; rules that vary go behind a strategy. Put each rule next to the data it guards." />

**Catch the trap:** *"Getters and setters on everything, rules in the service."* Lombok `@Data` on an entity abandons its invariants: anyone can set `exit` before `entry`. The rule ends up re-implemented in three places, and one of them forgets.

<h2 id="interfaces-first">Chapter 5 · Seams before flesh</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 5" title="Seams before flesh">

At minute twenty you finally open the IDE. You type `class LibraryService {` and start writing `borrow`.

Wren clears her throat.

*"Borrow calls what?"*

"The copies… and the member… and the fee thing."

*"Do those exist?"*

They don't. You were about to write a method against three classes you hadn't decided on.

</Scene>

Write the **interfaces** for whatever varies, the **repository** boundary and the **service entry points** first — with bodies that throw. Now the whole shape compiles, every later method has something real to call, and the follow-up feature already has a slot waiting for it.

```java fragment
interface LendingPolicy {                 // the axis that varies
    int maxLoans();
    int loanDays();
    long lateFee(long daysLate);
}

interface Repository<ID, T> {             // the storage boundary
    T save(T entity);
    Optional<T> findById(ID id);
    List<T> findAll();
}

class LibraryService {                    // entry points, empty for now
    Loan borrow(String memberId, String isbn) { throw new UnsupportedOperationException("borrow"); }
    Receipt returnCopy(String copyId)        { throw new UnsupportedOperationException("returnCopy"); }
}
```

**Three kinds of thing get an interface at minute 20, and nothing else:**

| Kind | Why | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **The axis that varies** | The follow-up will add a second implementation | `LendingPolicy`, `FeeStrategy`, `SplitStrategy` |
| **The storage boundary** | Storage is "in-memory now, swappable later" — say it | `Repository<ID, T>` |
| **Anything nondeterministic** | Tests and demos need to control it | `java.time.Clock`, an id generator |

Entities don't get interfaces. The service doesn't get an interface (there is only ever one). If an interface has no second implementation, no test double and no announced axis, it is speculative — inline it.

**Stubs throw, they never return null.** `throw new UnsupportedOperationException("borrow")` compiles, and if you forget to fill it in, the demo fails loudly on the exact method. `return null` fails three calls later with a `NullPointerException` that points somewhere else.

<Trace title="Which seams?" steps={seams} takeaway="Interfaces for variation, storage and nondeterminism — each with a second implementation or a test double. Stubs throw with the method’s name." />

**Catch the trap:** *"I'll design the interfaces as I go."* Then every method you write is written against a guess, and the first wrong guess means editing callers. Ten minutes of signatures at minute 20 is the cheapest refactoring you will ever do.

<h2 id="layout">Chapter 6 · Where everything lives</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 6" title="Where everything lives" mood="night">

Oona opens your mock submission to review it. It is one file: `Solution.java`, 640 lines, classes in the order you thought of them.

She scrolls for a while. *"Where's the fee rule?"*

"Line… four hundred and something."

*"And where would the next person look for it?"*

</Scene>

A layout that **reads as senior** takes sixty seconds to set up, and tells the grader where everything is before they open a file:

```text
com.gearhouse.library
├── model/          // entities + values: Book, Copy, Member, Loan, Receipt, Tier
├── strategy/       // the variation seams: LendingPolicy, FlatFeePolicy, GraceAndCapPolicy
├── repository/     // Repository<ID, T> + InMemoryRepository (Map-backed) behind it
├── service/        // LibraryService — orchestration, thin
├── exception/      // DomainException + NotFound, NoCopyAvailable, LoanLimit, NotOnLoan
└── Main.java       // composition root + the demo script — YOUR closing argument
```

**Dependencies point one way:** `Main → service → (repository, strategy) → model ← exception`. The model depends on nothing but its exceptions. The service depends on *interfaces* for storage and policy. `Main` is the composition root: the only place allowed to name every concrete class and wire them together.

<Figure caption="The layers. Arrows are source dependencies; nothing points up. Main is the only class that knows every concrete type.">
  <Mermaid code={`flowchart TD
    MAIN["Main\ncomposition root + demo"] --> SVC["service/\nLibraryService"]
    MAIN --> IMPL["InMemoryRepository\nFlatFeePolicy"]
    SVC --> REPO[["repository/\nRepository interface"]]
    SVC --> STRAT[["strategy/\nLendingPolicy interface"]]
    IMPL -. implements .-> REPO
    IMPL -. implements .-> STRAT
    SVC --> MODEL["model/\nCopy, Loan, Member…"]
    MODEL --> EXC["exception/\nDomainException…"]`} />
</Figure>

**Single-file submissions.** Many platforms accept exactly one file. Then the layout becomes **sections inside one `public class Main`**, in the same order as the packages, each headed by a comment (`// ---- model/ ----`). Every class is a `static class` nested in `Main` — a plain nested class would need an enclosing `Main` instance for every `new`. Records, enums and interfaces are implicitly static. That is exactly the shape of the worked round in Chapter 12: every block on this page is a section of one `Main`.

**Multi-file submissions.** Use the package tree. Keep classes package-private where you can, and resist sub-packages inside the five above — depth is not organisation.

<Trace title="Which way the arrows point" steps={layout} takeaway="Model at the bottom, service in the middle depending on interfaces, Main on top wiring concretes. In a single file, the same sections in the same order, as static nested classes." />

**Catch the trap:** *"Layers are over-engineering in 90 minutes."* Five folders are not layers of indirection; they are a table of contents. What *is* over-engineering is a `LibraryServiceImpl` behind a `LibraryService` interface with one implementation, or a DTO that copies an entity field for field.

<h2 id="skeleton">Chapter 7 · The skeleton you can type with your eyes closed</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 7" title="The skeleton" mood="dawn">

Wren keeps a small card in her wallet. It is soft at the edges from being taken out. On it, in tiny handwriting: *Repository. InMemory. DomainException. NotFound. Ids. Clock.*

*"Every round, I type these first,"* she says. *"Same code, every time. Ten minutes, and I've never once had to think about them during a round. All my thinking goes into the problem."*

</Scene>

Some code is the same in every machine-coding round: a repository, an in-memory implementation, a base domain exception, an id source, a controllable clock. **Memorise it**, so that at minute 20 you type it while your mind is on the domain. Here is the skeleton, exactly as it opens the worked round in Chapter 12.

**The repository boundary and the exceptions:**

```java group=round
import java.time.*;
import java.time.temporal.ChronoUnit;

// ---- repository/ ----
interface Repository<ID, T> {
    T save(T entity);
    Optional<T> findById(ID id);
    List<T> findAll();
}

// ---- exception/ ----
abstract static class DomainException extends RuntimeException {   // "a business rule said no"
    DomainException(String message) { super(message); }
}

static class NotFoundException extends DomainException {
    NotFoundException(String kind, Object id) { super(kind + " " + id + " not found"); }
}
```

**Your turn.** Write the in-memory implementation — once, generically, so it serves every entity in the round.

<HintLadder hints={hints.repo} labels={turn}>

```java group=round
static class InMemoryRepository<ID, T> implements Repository<ID, T> {
    private final Map<ID, T> store = new ConcurrentHashMap<>();
    private final Function<T, ID> idOf;                        // Book::isbn, Copy::id, ...

    InMemoryRepository(Function<T, ID> idOf) { this.idOf = idOf; }

    public T save(T entity) {
        store.put(idOf.apply(entity), entity);
        return entity;
    }

    public Optional<T> findById(ID id) {
        if (id == null) return Optional.empty();               // ConcurrentHashMap rejects null keys
        return Optional.ofNullable(store.get(id));
    }

    public List<T> findAll() { return List.copyOf(store.values()); }
}
```

The `Function<T, ID>` is the trick: no `Identifiable` interface on every entity, just a method reference at wiring time — `new InMemoryRepository<>(Copy::id)`. `findAll` returns a copy, so a caller can't corrupt the store by mutating the list. The null guard matters: `ConcurrentHashMap.get(null)` throws `NullPointerException`, and Oona will pass a null id.

</HintLadder>

**Ids and time — the two nondeterministic things:**

```java group=round
// ---- infrastructure: ids and time ----
static class Ids {                                             // C-001, C-002, M-001, ...
    private final Map<String, AtomicLong> counters = new ConcurrentHashMap<>();
    String next(String prefix) {
        long n = counters.computeIfAbsent(prefix, p -> new AtomicLong()).incrementAndGet();

    }

static class ManualClock extends Clock {                       // a clock that tests and demos can move
    private Instant now;
    private final ZoneId zone;
    ManualClock(Instant start, ZoneId zone) { this.now = start; this.zone = zone; }

    void advanceDays(long days) { now = now.plus(Duration.ofDays(days)); }

}
```

Sequential ids make the demo output **predictable** — the grader can read `C-001` and follow it; `UUID`s turn every line into noise. (Zero-padding keeps them sorting correctly up to 999, which is plenty for a demo.) `ManualClock` lets the demo jump twenty days forward to show a late fee; production would wire `Clock.systemDefaultZone()`, and the service never knows the difference because it only ever calls `LocalDate.now(clock)`.

**The conventions that earn points as you go** — none take more than seconds:

- **In-memory repository = a `Map` behind an interface.** Swappable, and nobody asked for JPA.
- **Domain exceptions, not booleans and nulls.** `NoCopyAvailableException` carries what and why; a `false` return forces callers to guess. Unchecked, extending a base `DomainException`, message includes the ids.
- **Enums for closed sets** (`Tier`, `VehicleType`, `TicketStatus`) — with behaviour on them where it's intrinsic (`SpotType.fits(VehicleType)`).
- **Ids as `String` now, typed wrappers if time allows** — mention the upgrade (`record CopyId(String value)`) even if you skip it.
- **No `null` returns from finders** — `Optional<T> findById(...)`; reserve exceptions for "must exist", which the *service* decides.
- **Money is a `long` of the smallest unit** (or `BigDecimal`), never `double`.

**Catch the trap:** *"I'll make the repository specific to each entity."* `CopyRepository`, `MemberRepository`, `LoanRepository`, each with the same three methods, is ten minutes of copy-paste. Write the generic one once; add a specific finder (an index by isbn, say) only when a query genuinely needs it.

<h2 id="concurrency">Chapter 9 · Two phones, one charger</h2>

<Scene chapter="Chapter 9" title="Two phones, one charger" mood="storm">

Copperline's new app lets drivers book an EV charger slot. At 17:59 on a Friday, two drivers book charger 3 for 18:00 from two phones. Both get a green tick.

At 18:00, two cars arrive at one charger. One driver films the other. Bram forwards the video to the Gearhouse without a message.

Oona reproduces it in an afternoon with two threads and a latch. The code is four lines long, and the map it uses is a `ConcurrentHashMap`. *"It's thread-safe,"* the author says. *"It says so in the name."*

Wren reads the four lines. *"Each call is thread-safe. Is the decision?"*

</Scene>

Most machine-coding rounds are single-threaded by agreement — but **"what if two requests hit this at the same time?"** is a common late question, and some problems (booking, inventory, rate limiting) put it in scope from the start. You need three tools, and a sentence that picks between them.

**First, find the race.** A race lives wherever code **checks, then acts on what it checked**, and another thread can act in between. The mess:

```java fragment
if (!slots.containsKey(slot)) {        // thread A: free. thread B: free.
    slots.put(slot, driver);           // A writes. B overwrites. Both were told "booked".
    return true;
}
return false;
```

`ConcurrentHashMap` makes `containsKey` and `put` each safe on their own. It cannot make the *pair* atomic. Concurrency answers start with **"which decision must be atomic, and over which resource?"**

**Tool 1 · One atomic map operation, when the decision is about one key.** `putIfAbsent`, `computeIfAbsent`, `compute`, `merge`, `replace(k, old, new)` and `remove(k, v)` each check and act as one step:

```java group=locks
// Tool 1: ConcurrentHashMap's atomic operations — one key, one decision
static class SlotBook {
    private final ConcurrentHashMap<String, String> holderBySlot = new ConcurrentHashMap<>();

    boolean claim(String slot, String driver) {
        return holderBySlot.putIfAbsent(slot, driver) == null;   // null means nobody was there: you won
    }
    boolean release(String slot, String driver) {
        return holderBySlot.remove(slot, driver);                // only the holder can release
    }
    Optional<String> holder(String slot) { return Optional.ofNullable(holderBySlot.get(slot)); }
}
```

**Tool 2 · A lock per resource, when the decision reads and writes more than one thing.** A transfer reads one balance and writes two. Lock each account, not the world — and when you need two locks, **always take them in a fixed global order**, or two opposite transfers deadlock:

```java group=locks
// Tool 2: a lock per resource — when one decision spans several reads and writes
static class Wallets {
    private final Map<String, Long> balances = new ConcurrentHashMap<>();
    private final Map<String, ReentrantLock> locks = new ConcurrentHashMap<>();

    void open(String account, long amount) { balances.put(account, amount); }
    long balance(String account) { return balances.getOrDefault(account, 0L); }

    void debit(String account, long amount) {
        ReentrantLock lock = lockFor(account);
        lock.lock();
        try {
            long have = balance(account);
            if (have < amount) throw new IllegalStateException("insufficient funds in " + account);
            balances.put(account, have - amount);
        } finally {
            lock.unlock();
        }
    }

    void transfer(String from, String to, long amount) {
        if (from.equals(to)) throw new IllegalArgumentException("cannot transfer to the same account");
        boolean fromFirst = from.compareTo(to) < 0;               // a global order: by account id
        ReentrantLock first = lockFor(fromFirst ? from : to);
        ReentrantLock second = lockFor(fromFirst ? to : from);
        first.lock();
        try {
            second.lock();
            try {
                long have = balance(from);
                if (have < amount) throw new IllegalStateException("insufficient funds in " + from);
                balances.put(from, have - amount);
                balances.put(to, balance(to) + amount);
            } finally {
                second.unlock();
            }
        } finally {
            first.unlock();
        }
    }

    private ReentrantLock lockFor(String account) {
        return locks.computeIfAbsent(account, a -> new ReentrantLock());   // atomic: one lock per account, ever
    }
}
```

(For `debit` alone, `balances.compute(account, …)` would also be atomic; the explicit lock earns its place in `transfer`, where one decision spans two keys.) `lockFor` uses `computeIfAbsent` precisely because creating the lock is itself a check-then-act — two threads must never get two different locks for the same account.

**Tool 3 · An optimistic version check, when conflicts are rare.** Don't lock at all. Read a value *with its version*, compute the new value, and write only if the version hasn't moved; if it has, someone else won — re-read and retry. This is what a database's `UPDATE … WHERE id = ? AND version = ?` (and JPA's `@Version`) does.

**Your turn.** Build a versioned store whose `update` fails when someone else wrote first, and a retrying helper on top.

<HintLadder hints={hints.versioned} labels={turn}>

```java group=locks
// Tool 3: optimistic version check — read, compute, write only if nobody else wrote first
record Versioned<T>(T value, long version) {}

static class VersionedStore<T> {
    private final ConcurrentHashMap<String, Versioned<T>> rows = new ConcurrentHashMap<>();

    void insert(String id, T value) {
        if (rows.putIfAbsent(id, new Versioned<>(value, 0)) != null)
            throw new IllegalStateException(id + " already exists");
    }

    Versioned<T> read(String id) {
        Versioned<T> row = rows.get(id);
        if (row == null) throw new NoSuchElementException(id);
        return row;
    }

    /** Writes only if the row is still at expectedVersion. False means another writer won: re-read and retry. */
    boolean update(String id, long expectedVersion, T newValue) {
        Versioned<T> current = read(id);
        if (current.version() != expectedVersion) return false;
        return rows.replace(id, current, new Versioned<>(newValue, expectedVersion + 1));   // compare-and-swap
    }

    T updateWithRetry(String id, UnaryOperator<T> change) {
        while (true) {
            Versioned<T> seen = read(id);
            T next = change.apply(seen.value());
            if (update(id, seen.version(), next)) return next;
        }
    }
}
```

`replace(id, current, next)` swaps only if the row still equals what you read — compare and swap in one atomic step, so even two writers who both pass the version check can't both win. The checker runs eight threads, 500 increments each, through `updateWithRetry` and gets exactly 4000 with version 4000: no lost updates, and no thread ever waited on a lock.

</HintLadder>

**Choosing between them — the sentence to say:**

| Situation | Tool | Say |
|---|---|---|
| One key, one decision (claim a seat, a slot, a username) | Atomic map op | *"putIfAbsent is the check and the write in one step."* |
| A decision across several fields or keys | Per-resource lock, fixed order | *"I lock both accounts in id order, so two opposite transfers can't deadlock."* |
| Conflicts rare, work cheap to redo | Optimistic version | *"No one waits; the rare loser re-reads and retries."* |
| In doubt, five minutes left | One lock around the mutating operations | *"Coarse and correct first. Per-resource striping if throughput matters."* |

The last row is not a cop-out. **Correct and coarse beats fine and wrong**, and the worked round in Chapter 12 does exactly this — one new class, one lock — when the Interviewer asks at minute 85.

Two things to mention even if you don't code them: **visibility** (a plain field written by one thread may never be seen by another unless both go through the same lock, a `volatile`, or a concurrent collection) and **the safest shared state is immutable** — build maps in constructors, `final`, `Map.copyOf`. The War Room has a real case of the first: <a href={`${BASE}lld/war/inventory/#2-a-lazily-built-strategy-map--data-race`}>a lazily built strategy map</a> on a non-volatile field, where another thread could see a half-built `HashMap`. The fix there was to stop being lazy. The <a href={`${BASE}lld/lessons/concurrency-for-lld/`}>concurrency for LLD lesson</a> goes deeper on all of this.

<Trace title="Find the race" steps={concurrency} takeaway="Find the check-then-act. One key: an atomic map operation. Several resources: per-resource locks in a fixed order. Rare conflicts: an optimistic version check. Out of time: one coarse lock, said out loud." />

**Catch the trap:** *"I used ConcurrentHashMap, so it's thread-safe."* The collection is. Your *decision* — read the count, compare with the limit, then write — is not, unless it is one atomic call or runs under a lock.

```java group=round
// ---- tests without JUnit: two helpers and a counter ----
static int passed = 0;

static void check(boolean condition, String name) {
    if (!condition) throw new AssertionError("FAILED: " + name);
    passed++;
}

static void expectThrows(Class<? extends RuntimeException> type, Runnable action, String name) {
    try {
        action.run();
    } catch (RuntimeException e) {
        if (type.isInstance(e)) { passed++; return; }
        throw new AssertionError("FAILED: " + name + " threw " + e);
    }
    throw new AssertionError("FAILED: " + name + " did not throw " + type.getSimpleName());
}
```

```java group=round
// ---- model/ ----
enum Tier { STANDARD, PREMIUM }

record Book(String isbn, String title) {}

record Member(String id, String name, Tier tier) {}

static class Copy {                                           // entity: owns its availability
    private final String id;
    private final String isbn;
    private boolean onLoan = false;

    Copy(String id, String isbn) {
        this.id = id;
        this.isbn = isbn;
    }

    void checkOut() {
        if (onLoan) throw new IllegalStateException("copy " + id + " is already on loan");
        onLoan = true;
    }

    void checkIn() {
        if (!onLoan) throw new IllegalStateException("copy " + id + " is not on loan");
        onLoan = false;
    }

    boolean isAvailable() { return !onLoan; }
    String id() { return id; }
    String isbn() { return isbn; }
}

static class Loan {                                           // entity: owns its dates
    private final String id, copyId, memberId;
    private final LocalDate borrowedOn, dueOn;
    private LocalDate returnedOn;                             // null while the book is out

    Loan(String id, String copyId, String memberId, LocalDate borrowedOn, LocalDate dueOn) {
        if (dueOn.isBefore(borrowedOn)) throw new IllegalArgumentException("due before borrowed");
        this.id = id;
        this.copyId = copyId;
        this.memberId = memberId;
        this.borrowedOn = borrowedOn;
        this.dueOn = dueOn;
    }

    void close(LocalDate on) {
        if (returnedOn != null) throw new IllegalStateException("loan " + id + " is already closed");
        if (on.isBefore(borrowedOn)) throw new IllegalArgumentException("returned before borrowed");
        returnedOn = on;
    }

    boolean isActive() { return returnedOn == null; }
    boolean isOverdueOn(LocalDate day) { return isActive() && day.isAfter(dueOn); }
    long daysLateOn(LocalDate day) { return Math.max(0, ChronoUnit.DAYS.between(dueOn, day)); }

    String id() { return id; }
    String copyId() { return copyId; }
    String memberId() { return memberId; }
    LocalDate dueOn() { return dueOn; }
}

record Receipt(String loanId, String copyId, long daysLate, long fee) {}
```

Notice the entity guards throw `IllegalStateException`, not a domain exception. The service never lets a user reach them — it only picks available copies and only closes active loans — so if one fires, **it's a bug**, and a bug should crash loudly rather than print a polite rejection. interface LendingPolicy {
    int maxLoans();
    int loanDays();
    /** Never negative; never smaller for a later return. */
    long lateFee(long daysLate);
}

### 0:28 – 0:58 · The core flow

Borrow first, end to end; then return. Narrated while typing: *"Borrow checks the member, the book, the limit, then claims the lowest-numbered free copy — lowest so the demo is predictable."*

```java group=round
// ---- service/ ----
static class LibraryService {
    private final Repository<String, Book> books;
    private final Repository<String, Copy> copies;
    private final Repository<String, Member> members;
    private final Repository<String, Loan> loans;
    private final Map<Tier, LendingPolicy> policies;
    private final Ids ids;
    private final Clock clock;

    LibraryService(Repository<String, Book> books, Repository<String, Copy> copies,
                   Repository<String, Member> members, Repository<String, Loan> loans,
                   Map<Tier, LendingPolicy> policies, Ids ids, Clock clock) {
        this.books = books;
        this.copies = copies;
        this.members = members;
        this.loans = loans;
        this.policies = Map.copyOf(policies);
        this.ids = ids;
        this.clock = clock;
    }

    Book addBook(String isbn, String title) { return books.save(new Book(isbn, title)); }

    Copy addCopy(String isbn) {
        requireBook(isbn);
        return copies.save(new Copy(ids.next("C"), isbn));
    }

    Member register(String name, Tier tier) { return members.save(new Member(ids.next("M"), name, tier)); }

    Loan borrow(String memberId, String isbn) {
        Member member = requireMember(memberId);
        requireBook(isbn);
        LendingPolicy policy = policyFor(member);
        if (activeLoans(memberId).size() >= policy.maxLoans())
            throw new LoanLimitException(memberId, policy.maxLoans());
        Copy copy = copies.findAll().stream()
                .filter(c -> c.isbn().equals(isbn) && c.isAvailable())
                .min(Comparator.comparing(Copy::id))
                .orElseThrow(() -> new NoCopyAvailableException(isbn));
        LocalDate today = LocalDate.now(clock);
        copy.checkOut();
        return loans.save(new Loan(ids.next("L"), copy.id(), memberId, today, today.plusDays(policy.loanDays())));
    }

    Receipt returnCopy(String copyId) {
        Copy copy = copies.findById(copyId).orElseThrow(() -> new NotFoundException("copy", copyId));
        Loan loan = loans.findAll().stream()
                .filter(l -> l.copyId().equals(copyId) && l.isActive())
                .findFirst()
                .orElseThrow(() -> new NotOnLoanException(copyId));
        LocalDate today = LocalDate.now(clock);
        loan.close(today);
        copy.checkIn();
        long daysLate = loan.daysLateOn(today);
        long fee = policyFor(requireMember(loan.memberId())).lateFee(daysLate);
        return new Receipt(loan.id(), copyId, daysLate, fee);
    }

    List<Loan> activeLoans(String memberId) {
        return loans.findAll().stream()
                .filter(l -> l.memberId().equals(memberId) && l.isActive())
                .sorted(Comparator.comparing(Loan::id))
                .toList();
    }

    long availableCopies(String isbn) {
        return copies.findAll().stream().filter(c -> c.isbn().equals(isbn) && c.isAvailable()).count();
    }

    List<Loan> overdue() {                                       // the "should", landed at 0:56
        LocalDate today = LocalDate.now(clock);
        return loans.findAll().stream()
                .filter(l -> l.isOverdueOn(today))
                .sorted(Comparator.comparing(Loan::id))
                .toList();
    }

    private Member requireMember(String id) {
        return members.findById(id).orElseThrow(() -> new NotFoundException("member", id));
    }

    private void requireBook(String isbn) {
        books.findById(isbn).orElseThrow(() -> new NotFoundException("book", isbn));
    }

    private LendingPolicy policyFor(Member member) {
        LendingPolicy policy = policies.get(member.tier());
        if (policy == null) throw new IllegalStateException("no lending policy for " + member.tier());
        return policy;
    }
}

// ---- Main: the composition root ----
static Map<Tier, LendingPolicy> launchPolicies() {
    LendingPolicy standard = new FlatFeePolicy(3, 14, 10);
    return Map.of(Tier.STANDARD, standard, Tier.PREMIUM, standard);   // "treat them the same for now"
}

static LibraryService newLibrary(Map<Tier, LendingPolicy> policies, Clock clock) {
    return new LibraryService(
            new InMemoryRepository<>(Book::isbn), new InMemoryRepository<>(Copy::id),
            new InMemoryRepository<>(Member::id), new InMemoryRepository<>(Loan::id),
            policies, new Ids(), clock);
}
```

Things worth saying out loud while you write it:

- **The service is thin.** It finds things, checks the one rule that spans entities (the loan limit), and tells the entities what to do. Copy and Loan guard themselves.
- **Every rejection is a domain exception with the id in it.** No `null`, no `false`.
- **`findAll().stream().filter(...)` is fine at this size.** *"With a million loans I'd keep an index of active loans by member and by copy inside the repository — same interface."*
- **The due date is fixed at borrow time** from the member's policy; the fee is computed at return. If a policy changes mid-loan, existing due dates don't move. Say it — it's the kind of subtle decision graders like to hear named.
- **`Map.copyOf`** makes the policy table immutable once the service is built, and a missing tier fails loudly instead of charging nothing.

### 0:84 · "What about two kiosks?"

The Interviewer leans back. *"Lantern Lane has two kiosks. What if both borrow the last copy at the same moment?"*

*"Borrow is check-then-act: it counts the member's loans, finds a free copy, then marks it. Two kiosks can both pass the checks. The repositories are concurrent maps, so each read and write is safe, but the decision isn't, and `Copy.onLoan` is a plain field. With five minutes left I'll put one lock around the operations — correct now; if throughput mattered, I'd lock per title and per member, in a fixed order."*

```java group=round
// ---- added at 0:85: two kiosks, one lock ----
static class ConcurrentLibrary {                               // same operations, one lock around each
    private final LibraryService library;
    private final ReentrantLock lock = new ReentrantLock();

    ConcurrentLibrary(LibraryService library) { this.library = library; }

    Loan borrow(String memberId, String isbn) { return locked(() -> library.borrow(memberId, isbn)); }
    Receipt returnCopy(String copyId) { return locked(() -> library.returnCopy(copyId)); }
    long availableCopies(String isbn) { return locked(() -> library.availableCopies(isbn)); }
    List<Loan> activeLoans(String memberId) { return locked(() -> library.activeLoans(memberId)); }

    private <T> T locked(Supplier<T> action) {
        lock.lock();
        try {
            return action.get();
        } finally {
            lock.unlock();
        }
    }
}
```

Reads go through the lock too: a plain `boolean` written under a lock is only guaranteed visible to threads that take the same lock. Setup (`addBook`, `register`) happens before the kiosks open, so it stays on the unwrapped service. The checker races 16 threads at the last copy, 200 times: exactly one loan, fifteen clean `NoCopyAvailableException`s, every time. It also sends twelve concurrent borrows from one standard member and gets exactly three loans on three different copies, and eight concurrent returns of one copy and gets exactly one receipt.

**Five follow-ups you will get:**

<Reveal q="1 · Why didn't you use a database?">

"It's an in-memory round, so I put storage behind a `Repository` interface with a `ConcurrentHashMap` implementation. A JPA or JDBC repository would be a second implementation of the same interface — the service wouldn't change. Wiring a database would have cost twenty minutes nobody asked for."

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="2 · Why does everything throw exceptions instead of returning booleans?">

"A boolean loses the reason and a null crashes somewhere else. Each rejection is a domain exception with the id in the message, all extending one `DomainException` — so the demo, or an API layer later, can catch business rejections in one place, while real bugs like a broken entity invariant still crash as `IllegalStateException`."

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="3 · How would you make this thread-safe properly — not one big lock?">

"Borrow decides over two resources: the member's loan count and the title's copies. I'd keep a lock per member and a lock per isbn, and always take them in the same order — member, then title — so two borrows can't deadlock. Return touches one copy and its member, same order. If contention were low, an optimistic version on each copy would also work: claim with compare-and-swap, retry on conflict. And I'd make sure every read of a copy's status goes through the same locks, for visibility."

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="4 · How would you add holds — a queue of members waiting for a title?">

"A `Hold` entity and a per-isbn queue. The interesting part is the trigger: when a copy is returned, the next member in the queue should get it. I wouldn't put that inside `returnCopy`; I'd have the service publish a 'copy returned' event to listeners — Observer — and a `HoldQueue` listener reserves the copy and notifies the member. Then the next reaction, analytics or email, is another listener, not another branch."

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="5 · What would you do differently with another hour?">

"Typed ids instead of strings (`record CopyId(String value)`), so a member id can't be passed where a copy id belongs. An index of active loans by member and by copy instead of scanning. Holds via the observer. JUnit tests with the same cases as `selfTest`, plus a contract test that every `LendingPolicy` keeps 'never negative, never decreasing'. And per-resource locks instead of the global one."

</Reveal>

<h2 id="beyond">Beyond this page</h2>

**Further study**

- Martin Fowler, *Refactoring* (2nd edition) — Speculative Generality, Shotgun Surgery and Divergent Change: the smells graders are looking for in your class list.
- Martin Fowler, [YAGNI](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/Yagni.html) — the cost of building presumptive features, in the vocabulary interviewers use.
- Eric Evans, *Domain-Driven Design* — entities, value objects, repositories and services: the vocabulary behind Chapters 4 and 7.
- Brian Goetz et al., *Java Concurrency in Practice* — Chapter 2 (atomicity and check-then-act), Chapter 3 (visibility), Chapter 10 (lock ordering and deadlock).
- [ConcurrentHashMap (Java 17 API)](https://docs.oracle.com/en/java/javase/17/docs/api/java.base/java/util/concurrent/ConcurrentHashMap.html) — which operations are atomic (`putIfAbsent`, `compute`, `merge`, `replace`, `remove(k, v)`) and which combinations are not.
- [java.time.Clock](https://docs.oracle.com/en/java/javase/17/docs/api/java.base/java/time/Clock.html) — the JDK's seam for time, with `fixed` and `offset` for tests.
- Joshua Bloch, *Effective Java* (3rd edition) — Item 69 onwards on exceptions ("use exceptions for exceptional conditions", "favor the use of standard exceptions", "include failure-capture information in detail messages").
- [Optimistic concurrency control (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimistic_concurrency_control) — the read–validate–write cycle behind version checks.

**Look these up yourself**

- **Walking skeleton** (Alistair Cockburn) — the smallest end-to-end slice that runs; the core-flow box is a walking skeleton in miniature.
- **Typed ids / value objects for identifiers** — why `CopyId` instead of `String` prevents a whole class of mix-ups, and what it costs in a round.
- **Lock striping** — how `ConcurrentHashMap` historically and Guava's `Striped` today trade one lock per key for a fixed pool of locks.
- **Domain events and the transactional outbox** — the production form of "the service publishes, listeners react", with the failure modes Observer hides in-process.
- **Property-based testing** (jqwik, QuickCheck) — generating hundreds of inputs to check a contract like "never negative, never decreasing", as this page's checker does by hand.
- **Testing without mocks / nullable infrastructure** — designs where in-memory implementations double as test fakes, exactly like the repository here.
- **`StampedLock` and `ReadWriteLock`** — when reads vastly outnumber writes and one exclusive lock is too coarse.
