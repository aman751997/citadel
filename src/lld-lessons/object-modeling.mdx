<h2 id="prologue">The brief and the four questions</h2>

### Concept

Object modelling is deciding what the things are, what must always be true about each of them, and which object keeps it true. This chapter sets up the brief that every later chapter builds on, and the four questions you ask about every class.

### Problem

A brief arrives as a page of prose. The fastest start is to turn every noun into a class and begin typing. That gives you classes with no rules, and the rules that matter (for example, "two bookings can never share a bay") have no owner. The grader reads exactly that: who owns each rule.

Here is the page. Every chapter of this lesson comes back to it.

> **Copperline Service Workshop.** Drivers book a service bay for a time slot, in 30-minute steps, between 09:00 and 18:00 Copperline time. A booking is held for 15 minutes until it's paid; then it's confirmed. Two bookings can never share a bay at the same time. Each booking can include several jobs: oil change ₹1,499 (30 min), tyre rotation ₹799 (30 min), brake check ₹999 (60 min, needs a bay with a lift). Bays B1 and B3 have lifts; B3 also has an EV charger. Gold-card customers get 10% off every job. Drivers can cancel for free up to 24 hours before; after that they pay 20%. On the day, the mechanic checks the car in by its number plate and marks the job done. If the car doesn't turn up, it's a no-show. Mechanics are assigned to bookings, one lead and any number of assistants.

<h3 id="what-is-graded">What the round is really testing</h3>

A machine-coding or LLD round is **not** a design-pattern spelling bee. The grader reads your code for four signals, in roughly this order:

| Signal | What it looks like in code | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| **Correct object boundaries** | Classes that own their data and their rules; no God class | Highest |
| **Invariants enforced** | Illegal states can't be written, or are refused at the door | High |
| **Extensibility along the stated axis** | The follow-up feature lands without editing existing classes | High |
| **Pattern vocabulary** | The right name for what you already built | Lowest |

Patterns come *last* and weigh least. Get the first three right and the patterns fall out on their own. That's why this lesson comes before any of them, and why the <a href={`${BASE}lld/lessons/solid/`}>SOLID lesson</a> that follows it keeps pointing back here.

**Object modelling is the act of deciding what the things are, what must always be true about each of them, and which object is responsible for keeping it true.** Everything else — methods, arrows, patterns — follows from those three decisions. Four questions do most of the work, and you'll ask them about every class in this lesson:

1. **Entity or value?** Does it have an identity that survives change, or is it just its data?
2. **What is its invariant?** What must be true of it for its whole life?
3. **Who may change it?** Which methods, and only those, can move it from one valid state to another?
4. **How does it relate to its neighbours?** Is-a under every operation, or has-a? Owned, shared, or just referred to by id?

### When to use

Do this at the start of every machine-coding or LLD round, in the first ten minutes, before any code. It also works as a review habit on code you did not write: for each class, ask the four questions.

### Trade-offs and traps

Modelling up front has a cost: ten minutes of talking before any code. Spend them, but stop at the point where each rule has an owner. Do not draw every class, and do not name a pattern before a requirement forces one.

### Interview summary

For each class I ask: entity or value, what is its invariant, who may change it, and how does it relate to its neighbours. I decide who owns each rule before I write code, and I add patterns only when a requirement forces one."

<h2 id="nouns">Chapter 1: Nouns, verbs and first-draft classes</h2>

### Concept

Noun and verb extraction means underlining the nouns in the requirements to find candidate classes, and the verbs to find candidate methods. It is a vocabulary exercise, and the first draft of a model, not the model.

### Problem

Highlight every noun in the brief and you get twenty-three candidates, including `Minute`, `Percent`, `Step` and `Copperline`. Some are synonyms (driver and customer), some are attributes, some are states, and some are units. Meanwhile the most important sentence, "two bookings can never share a bay at the same time", contains no new noun at all. If you turn every noun into a class, nothing owns that rule.

### Better design

**Noun and verb extraction** is the oldest trick in object design: underline the nouns in the requirements to find candidate classes, and the verbs to find candidate methods. Russell Abbott described it in 1983, and every LLD tutorial still starts with it. It is a good start. **It is only ever a first draft**, because English is not a design:

- **Synonyms.** "Driver" and "customer" are the same person seen from two places. Two classes would give you two phone numbers to update.
- **Nouns that are attributes.** "Price" isn't a thing with a life of its own; it is a number attached to a job in a price list. "Minute" and "percent" are units.
- **Nouns that are states.** "No-show" and "confirmed" are stages in a booking's life, not classes.
- **Nouns that are closed sets.** The three jobs and the two bay capabilities will never be extended at runtime. They're enums.
- **Verbs whose subject is the wrong owner.** "Drivers book a bay" reads as `Driver.book(bay)`. But the rule that a booking must not clash needs to see *every other booking for that bay*, and a driver can't see those.
- **Missing nouns.** The most important classes are often never written down. "Two bookings can never share a bay" implies something that holds all of a bay's bookings and checks them. The brief never names it. Neither does it name "time slot" as a thing with rules, or the price a customer *agreed to*, as distinct from today's price.

So run the extraction, then sort every noun into one of six bins:

| Noun from the brief | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| driver, customer | **Entity** `Customer` (one class) | Synonyms; identity survives a change of tier |
| bay | **Entity** `Bay` | B1 stays B1 even if it gets a lift fitted |
| booking | **Entity** `Booking` | Same booking from held to completed |
| mechanic | **Entity** `Mechanic` | Same person after a rename or a new skill |
| time slot | **Value** `TimeSlot` | Two slots with the same start and end are the same slot |
| number plate | **Value** `Plate` | "ka-01 zz 0001" and "KA01ZZ0001" are the same plate |
| price, fee | **Value** `Money` | Nobody asks *which* ₹1,499 |
| oil change, tyre rotation, brake check | **Enum** `Job` | A closed set, each with a duration and needs |
| lift, EV charger | **Enum** `Capability` | A closed set |
| held, confirmed, no-show… | **Enum** `BookingStatus` | States of a booking, not things |
| gold card | **Enum** `Tier` | A role a customer has, not a kind of customer (Chapter 10) |
| 09:00–18:00, 30-minute steps | **Value** `OpeningHours` | A rule with data |
| *(never named)* the bookings of one bay | **Entity** `BaySchedule` | The owner of "never share a bay" (Chapter 12) |
| Copperline, minute, percent, step | **Not a class** | Context, units, parameters |

**Verbs go to the object that has the data the rule needs.** Craig Larman's name for this is *Information Expert*: assign a responsibility to the class that has the information to carry it out. "Book" ends up on the bay's schedule. "Cancel", "check in" and "mark done" end up on the booking, because each needs only the booking's own state. "Get 10% off" ends up on the tier.

The closed sets can be written down already. An enum in Java is a full class: it can carry data and behaviour, and the compiler knows every value:

```java group=workshop
import java.time.*;

enum Capability { LIFT, EV_CHARGER }

enum Job {
    OIL_CHANGE(30),
    TYRE_ROTATION(30),
    BRAKE_CHECK(60, Capability.LIFT);

    private final Duration duration;
    private final Set<Capability> needs;

    Job(int minutes, Capability... needs) {
        this.duration = Duration.ofMinutes(minutes);
        this.needs = Set.of(needs);              // immutable; empty for jobs that need nothing special
    }

    Duration duration() { return duration; }
    Set<Capability> needs() { return needs; }
}
```

Notice that `Job` says *what it needs* (`Set.of(LIFT)`), not "brake checks need lifts" written as an `if` somewhere else. When the workshop adds an EV top-up next year, it is one new constant that needs `EV_CHARGER`, and no other code has to learn about it.

<h2 id="values">Chapter 2: Value objects</h2>

### Concept

A value object is an object that is its data. Two values with the same fields are interchangeable, it never changes, and its constructor refuses bad data. In Java the tool is a `record`.

### Problem

A gate operator types `KA01ZZ0001`. The car is in the database and in the lookup map, and the system says "unknown car". The `Plate` class overrides `equals` but not `hashCode`, so a `HashMap` never reaches `equals` for a separately built but equal plate. The same family of bug appears when a plate is typed as `ka-01 zz 0001`: two spellings, two keys.

### Bad design

A hand-written value class that overrides `equals` but not `hashCode`:

```java id=plateMess
class Plate {
    private final String value;
    Plate(String value) { this.value = value; }

    @Override public boolean equals(Object o) {
        return o instanceof Plate other && value.equals(other.value);
    }
    // no hashCode(): Object's identity-based hash is still in force
}
```

`HashMap.get` doesn't start with `equals`. It computes the key's `hashCode`, goes to that bucket, and only then compares entries — and it compares the stored hash before it calls `equals`. Two separately built plates keep `Object`'s identity-based hash codes, which almost always differ, so the lookup never reaches `equals`. The checker builds the two plates, confirms `equals` is true, and confirms `get` returns `null` (and that a `HashSet` happily holds both "equal" plates).

The rule is written into `Object.hashCode`'s contract: **if two objects are equal according to `equals`, they must have the same `hashCode`.** Override one, override both — Joshua Bloch's *Effective Java* gives each rule its own item (10 and 11).

### Better design

**A value object is an object that *is* its data.** `Money(₹500)`, a `TimeSlot`, a `Plate`: you never ask *which* ₹500. The properties that follow from that one idea:

1. **Equality by value.** Two values with the same fields are interchangeable. `equals` and `hashCode` compare fields — all of them, together.
2. **Immutable.** You don't change ₹500 into ₹600; you compute a new `Money`. No setters, ever. A value can then be shared, cached, used as a map key, and passed between threads without copying.
3. **Self-validating.** The constructor refuses bad data, so every value that exists is valid. A `TimeSlot` whose end is before its start can't be built.
4. **Behaviour that belongs to the concept.** A slot knows whether it overlaps another; money knows how to add itself. These are side-effect free: they return new values.
5. **One canonical form.** If two spellings mean the same thing, the constructor turns them into one, so that equality is honest.

**Java's `record` is the value-object tool.** It gives you `private final` fields, accessors, and `equals`, `hashCode` and `toString` generated from all the components — consistently, so the contract can't be broken. A *compact constructor* runs your validation before the fields are assigned, and may even replace a parameter:

```java group=workshop
record BayId(String value) {
    BayId { if (value == null || value.isBlank()) throw new IllegalArgumentException("blank bay id"); }
}
record BookingId(String value) {
    BookingId { if (value == null || value.isBlank()) throw new IllegalArgumentException("blank booking id"); }
}
record CustomerId(String value) {
    CustomerId { if (value == null || value.isBlank()) throw new IllegalArgumentException("blank customer id"); }
}
record MechanicId(String value) {
    MechanicId { if (value == null || value.isBlank()) throw new IllegalArgumentException("blank mechanic id"); }
}

record Plate(String value) {
    Plate {
        if (value == null) throw new IllegalArgumentException("plate required");
        value = value.replaceAll("[\\s-]", "").toUpperCase(Locale.ROOT);   // one canonical spelling
        if (!value.matches("[A-Z0-9]{4,10}")) throw new IllegalArgumentException("not a plate: " + value);
    }
}
```

`new Plate("ka-01 zz 0001")` and `new Plate("KA01ZZ0001")` are now equal, with equal hash codes, because the compact constructor stores one canonical spelling. That fixes the gate lookup *and* the mechanic who types plates in lower case (Chapter 8 relies on it). The ids are values too: a `BookingId` is a string with a rule and a type of its own, and Chapter 5 shows what the type buys.

<Reveal q="Records are immutable, so a record with a List component is safe to share. True?">

Only shallowly. A record's fields are `final`, which stops a field from pointing at a different list — not the list from changing. If the caller passes in an `ArrayList` and later adds to it, the record changes with it, and so does its `hashCode`. The fix is to copy mutable components in the compact constructor: `list = List.copyOf(list);` or `map = Map.copyOf(map);`. Those return unmodifiable copies, so neither the caller nor anyone who reads the accessor can change them. Chapter 6's `PriceList` does exactly this, and the checker proves that mutating the caller's map afterwards changes nothing.

</Reveal>

**Your turn.** The brief says "for a time slot". Write `TimeSlot`: two moments, a rule that must hold, a factory that takes a start and a length, and the question the bay's schedule will ask of it a thousand times a day — *do these two slots overlap?* (Use `Instant` for the moments; Chapter 5 explains why.)

<HintLadder hints={hints.timeSlot} labels={turn}>

```java group=workshop
record TimeSlot(Instant start, Instant end) {
    TimeSlot {
        if (start == null || end == null) throw new IllegalArgumentException("start and end required");
        if (!end.isAfter(start)) throw new IllegalArgumentException("a slot must end after it starts");
    }

    static TimeSlot of(Instant start, Duration length) { return new TimeSlot(start, start.plus(length)); }

    Duration length() { return Duration.between(start, end); }

    boolean overlaps(TimeSlot other) {               // half-open: [start, end)
        return start.isBefore(other.end) && other.start.isBefore(end);
    }
}
```

**Half-open slots** — the start is included, the end is not — make back-to-back bookings work without fudging: 10:00–11:00 and 11:00–11:30 don't overlap, because neither contains the instant 11:00 twice. The overlap test is the standard one for intervals: two intervals overlap exactly when each starts before the other ends. The checker compares it against a brute-force minute-by-minute oracle on every pair of slots inside a 20-minute window (over 40,000 pairs).

</HintLadder>

### When to use

Use a value object for anything that is its data: amounts, slots, plates, ids, opening hours, coordinates. If you would ever ask "which one", it is not a value.

### Trade-offs and traps

**Common slip:** *"I made it a record, so it's a value object."* A record with a mutable `List` component, or one that accepts `"ka01zz0001"` and `"KA01ZZ0001"` as different plates, is a record that is still lying about equality. The record writes `equals`; you still decide what equal *means*.

### Interview summary

> "Plate, TimeSlot and Money are value objects — records, immutable, validated in the compact constructor. Plate normalises its spelling so equality is honest, and anything with a collection inside copies it."

<h2 id="entities">Chapter 3: Entities and identity</h2>

### Concept

An entity is an object whose identity matters more than its current data. A booking that is paid, moved or cancelled is still that booking. Its equality comes from an id that never changes, not from fields that do.

### Problem

A booking is added to a `Set` while held. It is paid and its status becomes `CONFIRMED`. The report asks the set whether it contains the booking: false. `remove` finds nothing and `size` is 1. The class generates `equals` and `hashCode` from every field (as Lombok's `@Data` does), so the hash changed when the status did, and the set is looking in the wrong bucket.

### Bad design

Equality generated over every field, the way Lombok's `@Data` does it, on a class whose fields change:

```java id=dataMess
class ServiceTicket {                    // equals and hashCode over every field, like @Data
    final String id;
    String status;

    ServiceTicket(String id, String status) {
        this.id = id;
        this.status = status;
    }

    @Override public boolean equals(Object o) {
        return o instanceof ServiceTicket t && id.equals(t.id) && status.equals(t.status);
    }
    @Override public int hashCode() { return Objects.hash(id, status); }
}

boolean stillFindable() {
    Set<ServiceTicket> open = new HashSet<>();
    ServiceTicket t = new ServiceTicket("BK-1", "HELD");
    open.add(t);
    t.status = "CONFIRMED";              // the same ticket, one field later
    return open.contains(t);             // false
}
```

The set filed the ticket under the hash of `("BK-1", "HELD")`. After the change, `contains` looks under the hash of `("BK-1", "CONFIRMED")`, finds nothing there, and says no. The checker confirms all three symptoms: `contains` is false, `remove` is false, and `size` is still 1.

### Better design

**An entity is an object whose identity matters more than its current data.** A booking that gets paid, moved or cancelled is *still that booking*. Customers ask for it by its id; the history of changes belongs to it. Its attributes are not what make it itself.

| Ask of the concept | Entity | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Does it matter *which one* it is? | Yes: "booking BK-4" | No: "₹1,499" |
| Can it change and stay the same thing? | Yes | No; a change is a new value |
| Equality is by… | Identity (an id, or the object reference) | All of its fields |
| Do you track its history, or its life? | Yes: created, changed, finished | No |
| Typical Java | A `final class` with an id and guarded methods | A `record` |

Whether a concept is an entity depends on the domain, not on the noun. At a concert with assigned seating, seat 14F is an entity — it is sold once, to one person. At a general-admission concert, a seat is a value: any free one will do.

**Equality for entities has two honest options.** Either leave `equals` and `hashCode` alone — reference identity — and make sure only one object per id ever exists in memory (a repository or identity map hands out the same instance). Or define equality by the id, and the id alone. **Never by fields that can change.** And the id itself must never change: assign it once, in the constructor, and make the field `final`.

```java group=workshop
final class Mechanic {
    private final MechanicId id;                 // identity: fixed at birth
    private String name;                         // state: free to change
    private final Set<Job> skills = EnumSet.noneOf(Job.class);

    Mechanic(MechanicId id, String name) {
        if (id == null) throw new IllegalArgumentException("id required");
        this.id = id;
        rename(name);
    }

    void rename(String name) {
        if (name == null || name.isBlank()) throw new IllegalArgumentException("name required");
        this.name = name.strip();
    }

    void certify(Job job) { skills.add(Objects.requireNonNull(job, "job")); }
    boolean canDo(Job job) { return skills.contains(job); }

    MechanicId id() { return id; }
    String name() { return name; }

    @Override public boolean equals(Object o) { return o instanceof Mechanic m && id.equals(m.id); }
    @Override public int hashCode() { return id.hashCode(); }
}
```

Ravi can be renamed and certified for brake checks, and he stays equal to every other `Mechanic` object with id `M-7`, with the same hash code throughout. (`EnumSet` is the set to reach for when the elements are enum constants: it's a bit vector underneath, compact and fast.) The class is `final`, and that's why its constructor may safely call `rename` — Chapter 10 shows what goes wrong when a constructor calls a method that a subclass can override.

**Identity and lifecycle travel together.** An entity is *born* (with an id), *changes* through its own methods, and *ends* — completed, cancelled, archived — but in most business domains it is never truly deleted, because something else refers to its id. Three questions to answer for every entity you draw:

- **Who issues the id?** Never the caller's imagination, and never `new Random()` buried in a constructor. An id generator is a dependency, like a clock (Chapter 5).
- **What are its states, and which moves between them are legal?** For `Booking`, that's an enum and a transition table (Chapter 8).
- **When is it finished?** Terminal states should be recognisable from the type, not from a comment.

<Reveal q="Can an entity be a Java record?">

Not comfortably. A record's `equals` compares every component, which is value semantics; and record fields are final, so every change has to build a new record with the same id. You *can* override `equals` and `hashCode` in a record to compare only the id, but at that point you are fighting the tool. Records for values, `final` classes with an id and guarded methods for entities — that split is what interviewers expect to see.

<h2 id="money">Chapter 4: Money</h2>

### Concept

Money is a value object: an exact amount plus a currency, with one named rounding rule. Hold it as a `long` of minor units (paise), or as `BigDecimal`, never as `double`.

### Problem

A customer buys a tyre valve for 1.10 and a cap for 2.20, and pays 3.30. The kiosk refuses it: in `double`, `1.10 + 2.20` is `3.3000000000000003`. Ten 10-paise coupons add up to `0.9999999999999999`. Rounding after every step hides the problem until the one step where someone forgets.

### Bad design

Money in `double`s:

```java id=doubleMess
double add(double a, double b) { return a + b; }

double sum(double[] amounts) {
    double total = 0;
    for (double a : amounts) total += a;
    return total;
}
```

`add(1.10, 2.20)` is **3.3000000000000003**, so it isn't equal to `3.30`. Add ten 10-paise coupons with `sum` and you get **0.9999999999999999**. The checker asserts both, to the last digit.

**Why:** a `double` stores a binary fraction (IEEE 754, with a 53-bit significand). One-tenth in binary is `0.000110011001100…`, repeating forever, like one-third in decimal. The `double` keeps the nearest value it can, which is very slightly off; `1.10` and `2.20` are each slightly off, and the errors don't cancel. **This is not a precision problem you can fix with more digits; it is a representation problem.** Rounding to two places after every step hides it until the one step where you forget. The one-sentence proof interviewers want: *"0.1 has no exact binary representation."*

### Better design

**Two correct ways to hold money in Java:**

| | `long` minor units (paise) | `BigDecimal` |
|---|---|---|
| Exact for `+`, `−`, `×` an integer | Yes | Yes |
| Division, percentages, tax rates | You choose and write the rounding rule | Explicit `scale` and `RoundingMode` on every division |
| Speed and memory | A primitive; very fast | An object; slower |
| Traps | Overflow (use `Math.addExact`); forgetting the currency's minor digits | `equals` compares scale; `new BigDecimal(0.1)`; non-terminating `divide` throws |
| Good for | Prices, wallets, ledgers in one currency | Interest, tax rates and FX with many decimals |

Either way, wrap it. A bare `long` in a method signature doesn't say paise or rupees, or which currency, and `long fee, long count` can be swapped silently. That's what a `Money` value object is for (Martin Fowler's *Patterns of Enterprise Application Architecture* catalogues it as the Money pattern).

`BigDecimal`'s traps are worth seeing once, because each has failed a real code review:

```java id=decimalTraps
import java.math.*;

String fromDouble()        { return new BigDecimal(0.1).toString(); }    // the double's exact binary value
String fromString()        { return new BigDecimal("0.1").toString(); }  // the decimal you meant
boolean equalsSaysSame()   { return new BigDecimal("2.0").equals(new BigDecimal("2.00")); }
int compareSaysSame()      { return new BigDecimal("2.0").compareTo(new BigDecimal("2.00")); }
BigDecimal oneThird()      { return BigDecimal.ONE.divide(new BigDecimal("3")); }
BigDecimal oneThirdTo2dp() { return BigDecimal.ONE.divide(new BigDecimal("3"), 2, RoundingMode.HALF_UP); }
```

The checker records what each returns: `fromDouble()` is `0.1000000000000000055511151231257827021181583404541015625` — the double was already wrong before `BigDecimal` saw it, so build from strings, or use `BigDecimal.valueOf(double)`, which goes through `Double.toString`. `fromString()` is `0.1`. `equalsSaysSame()` is **false**, because `equals` compares the scale (one decimal place against two); `compareSaysSame()` is 0. `oneThird()` throws `ArithmeticException` (non-terminating decimal expansion), and `oneThirdTo2dp()` is `0.33`.

**The workshop's `Money`** keeps paise in a `long` and carries its currency, so two amounts in different currencies can never be added by accident:

```java group=workshop
import java.math.BigDecimal;

record Money(long minor, Currency currency) {     // minor units: paise for INR
    Money {
        if (currency == null) throw new IllegalArgumentException("currency required");
        if (minor < 0) throw new IllegalArgumentException("negative amount: " + minor);
    }

    static Money of(String amount, String currencyCode) {
        Currency currency = Currency.getInstance(currencyCode);
        BigDecimal exact = new BigDecimal(amount).setScale(currency.getDefaultFractionDigits());  // throws if it would round
        return new Money(exact.unscaledValue().longValueExact(), currency);
    }

    static Money zero(Currency currency) { return new Money(0, currency); }

    Money plus(Money other)  { return new Money(Math.addExact(minor, sameCurrency(other).minor), currency); }
    Money minus(Money other) { return new Money(Math.subtractExact(minor, sameCurrency(other).minor), currency); }
    Money times(long count)  { return new Money(Math.multiplyExact(minor, count), currency); }

    /** percent% of this amount, rounded half up to the nearest minor unit. */
    Money percent(long percent) {
        if (percent < 0) throw new IllegalArgumentException("negative percent");
        return new Money((Math.multiplyExact(minor, percent) + 50) / 100, currency);
    }

    private Money sameCurrency(Money other) {
        if (!currency.equals(other.currency))
            throw new IllegalArgumentException("currency mismatch: " + currency + " vs " + other.currency);
        return other;
    }

    @Override public String toString() {
        return currency.getCurrencyCode() + " " + BigDecimal.valueOf(minor, currency.getDefaultFractionDigits()).toPlainString();
    }
}
```

The decisions, one line each:

- **Parsing is exact or loud.** `Money.of("1499", "INR")` is 149,900 paise and prints as `INR 1499.00`. `Money.of("0.005", "INR")` throws, because `setScale(2)` without a rounding mode refuses to round. The number of minor digits comes from `java.util.Currency`: 2 for INR, 0 for JPY, 3 for BHD.
- **Equality is by amount.** `Money.of("1.0", "INR")` equals `Money.of("1.00", "INR")` — both are 100 paise — unlike the `BigDecimal`s they came from.
- **Overflow throws** (`Math.addExact` and friends) instead of wrapping around to a negative number.
- **The rounding rule has a name and one home.** `percent` rounds half up to the paisa: `(minor × percent + 50) / 100` in integer arithmetic. The checker compares it against `BigDecimal` with `RoundingMode.HALF_UP` for 2,000 random amounts and percentages. Rounding is a business rule — the finance team chooses it, not the JDK — so it lives in exactly one method.
- **In this domain a price is never negative**, so `Money` refuses negatives and `minus` throws rather than go below zero. A ledger that records refunds and debts would allow negative amounts and model the sign explicitly; that's a domain decision, and you should say which one you made.

<Reveal q="Why does Money refuse to add INR to USD instead of converting?">

Because conversion needs a rate, a time and a rounding rule, and none of them belong to an amount of money. `plus` that silently converted would hide a business decision inside an arithmetic operator. If the workshop ever takes dollars, an `ExchangeRates` service converts explicitly — `rates.convert(amount, USD, at)` — and the result is an ordinary `Money` in the target currency.

</Reveal>

class BillSplitter {
    /** parts shares that differ by at most one minor unit and add up exactly to total. */
    static List<Money> split(Money total, int parts) {
        if (parts <= 0) throw new IllegalArgumentException("parts must be positive");
        long base = total.minor() / parts, leftover = total.minor() % parts;
        List<Money> shares = new ArrayList<>();
        for (int i = 0; i < parts; i++)
            shares.add(new Money(base + (i < leftover ? 1 : 0), total.currency()));
        return shares;
    }
}

The checker splits 2,000 random amounts into 1 to 12 parts and checks that the shares add up exactly, differ by at most one paisa, and that the bigger ones come first. (Fowler calls this operation *allocate*; a real one often splits by ratios, such as 70/30, using the same leftover trick.)

<h2 id="time-and-ids">Chapter 5: Time, clocks and ids</h2>

### Concept

Store moments as `Instant`s, keep human rules (opening hours, "same time tomorrow") in local types with one explicit `ZoneId`, and convert only at the edges. Treat "now" and id generation as dependencies you inject.

### Problem

A customer books 10:00 and the confirmation says 15:30: the booking stored a zone-less `LocalDateTime` and another service formatted it as if it were UTC. Separately, the free-cancellation test passes in the morning and fails in the evening build, because the rule calls `LocalDateTime.now()` itself and nobody can control that clock.

### Bad design

This version stores a wall-clock time with no zone and asks the real clock for "now" from inside a business rule:

```java fragment
class BookingDesk {
    LocalDateTime start;                                       // 10:00 — in which zone?
    boolean canCancelFree() {
        return LocalDateTime.now().isBefore(start.minusHours(24));   // the server's zone, and a clock nobody can control
    }
}
```

### Better design

**`java.time` gives you a type for each question. Use the one that matches what you mean:**

| Question | Type | In the workshop |
|---|---|---|
| *When exactly did it happen, or will it?* A point on the timeline, the same everywhere | `Instant` | Slot start and end, hold expiry, check-in time |
| *What does the wall clock say, where?* | `ZonedDateTime` (an `Instant` seen through a `ZoneId`) | Showing a slot to a person; converting the brief's "10:00 Copperline time" |
| *Which day, which time of day?* No zone, so not a moment | `LocalDate`, `LocalTime` | Opening hours (09:00–18:00), "Tuesday" |
| *Which rules does a place follow?* | `ZoneId` (`Asia/Kolkata`) | The garage's zone, set once |
| *How long, exactly?* | `Duration` (seconds and nanos) | 15-minute hold, 60-minute brake check |
| *How long, on the calendar?* | `Period` (years, months, days) | "Same time tomorrow" |

**The rule: store moments as `Instant`s; keep human rules in local types with one explicit `ZoneId`; convert only at the edges** — when a person types a time in, and when you show one back. `LocalDateTime` on its own is not a moment: "10:00" is 04:30 UTC in Copperline and 10:00 UTC in London in winter. The checker confirms that 09:00 in `Asia/Kolkata` on Monday 16 March 2026 is `2026-03-16T03:30:00Z`.

**Days are not always 24 hours.** Zones with daylight saving have days of 23 and 25 hours. In `Europe/London`, Sunday 29 March 2026 is 23 hours long — at 01:00 GMT the clocks jump to 02:00 BST — and a local time of 01:30 that day doesn't exist (`ZonedDateTime` moves it forward to 02:30). The checker measures both. So `Duration.ofDays(1)` (exactly 24 hours) and `Period.ofDays(1)` (the same wall-clock time tomorrow) are different: 10:00 on Saturday the 28th plus a `Period` of one day is 10:00 on Sunday; plus a `Duration` of one day is 11:00. India has no daylight saving, which is why Copperline's numbers stay simple — and why "it works in India" is not a test.

**Now is a dependency.** A business rule that calls `now()` itself can't be tested at 08:20 unless you stay up for it. Inject a `java.time.Clock` (the <a href={`${BASE}lld/lessons/solid/`}>SOLID lesson's</a> Chapter 6 does this for checkout) and ask it with `clock.instant()`. In tests, use a clock you can move by hand:

```java group=workshop
interface BookingIds { BookingId next(); }

class SequentialBookingIds implements BookingIds {     // tests and the demo: BK-1, BK-2, ...
    private long last = 0;
    public BookingId next() { return new BookingId("BK-" + (++last)); }
}

class RandomBookingIds implements BookingIds {         // production: unguessable
    public BookingId next() { return new BookingId("BK-" + UUID.randomUUID()); }
}

final class ManualClock extends Clock {                 // a clock a test can move
    private Instant now;
    private final ZoneId zone;

    ManualClock(Instant start, ZoneId zone) {
        this.now = start;
        this.zone = zone;
    }

    void advance(Duration by) { now = now.plus(by); }
    void set(Instant at) { now = at; }

    @Override public Instant instant() { return now; }
    @Override public ZoneId getZone() { return zone; }
    @Override public Clock withZone(ZoneId other) { return new ManualClock(now, other); }
}
```

**Ids are part of the model too.** Three decisions:

- **Give each id its own type.** With `String customerId, String bayId`, the call `book(bayId, customerId)` compiles and corrupts data. With `CustomerId` and `BayId` it's a compile error, and each id validates itself once, in its own constructor. Passing raw `String`s and `long`s for domain concepts has a name: *primitive obsession*.
- **Decide who issues them.** `BookingIds` is a port, like `Clock`: sequential in tests (so a test can predict `BK-1`), random in production.
- **Pick the format for what it must do.** Sequential ids are short and readable, but guessable (anyone holding `BK-41` can try `BK-42`) and need one counter. Random UUIDs (version 4, 122 random bits) are unguessable and need no coordination, but don't sort by time. Time-ordered ids (Snowflake-style, or UUIDv7) sort by creation time, which suits database indexes; the <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/databases/#ids`}>databases lesson's ID chapter</a> sizes them. **Never promise gapless ids:** a refused booking still drew a number, as the demo in Chapter 13 shows.

**Your turn.** Write `OpeningHours`: 09:00 to 18:00 in the garage's zone, in 30-minute steps. It needs two methods: one that turns the garage's words ("Tuesday, 10:00, for an hour") into a `TimeSlot`, and one that says whether a slot is bookable.

<HintLadder hints={hints.admits} labels={turn}>

```java group=workshop
record OpeningHours(LocalTime opens, LocalTime closes, ZoneId zone, Duration step) {
    OpeningHours {
        if (opens == null || closes == null || zone == null || step == null) throw new IllegalArgumentException("all fields required");
        if (!closes.isAfter(opens)) throw new IllegalArgumentException("closes before it opens");
        if (step.isZero() || step.isNegative()) throw new IllegalArgumentException("step must be positive");
    }

    /** The garage's wall-clock words, turned into a moment on the timeline. */
    TimeSlot slot(LocalDate day, LocalTime from, Duration length) {
        return TimeSlot.of(ZonedDateTime.of(day, from, zone).toInstant(), length);
    }

    boolean admits(TimeSlot slot) {
        ZonedDateTime start = slot.start().atZone(zone), end = slot.end().atZone(zone);
        if (!start.toLocalDate().equals(end.toLocalDate())) return false;          // no overnight jobs
        LocalTime from = start.toLocalTime(), to = end.toLocalTime();
        if (from.isBefore(opens) || to.isAfter(closes)) return false;              // inside opening hours
        long grid = step.toNanos();
        return Duration.between(opens, from).toNanos() % grid == 0                 // starts on the grid
            && slot.length().toNanos() % grid == 0;                                // and lasts whole steps
    }
}
```

The conversion happens once, in `slot()`, with the zone the record carries; everything after that is `Instant`s. The checker runs `admits` on every start time of a day in 5-minute steps against every length from 5 minutes to 15 hours, and compares each answer with plain minute arithmetic (start ≥ 09:00, end ≤ 18:00, both on the 30-minute grid). It also rejects 09:00:30, which is inside the hours but off the grid.

<h2 id="invariants">Chapter 6: Invariants</h2>

### Concept

An invariant is a rule that must hold for an object's whole lifetime. The design question is not "is there a check somewhere?" but "can any code path produce an object that breaks it?" The answer should be no, because the object itself enforces it.

### Problem

A wallet is an object with a getter and a setter. The payment screen checks the balance before taking a fee. A late-fee job, written by someone else, does not. It reads the balance, subtracts 800 and writes it back, and the wallet shows -300. Every caller has to remember the rule, and one forgot.

### Bad design

An *anemic* object — a struct with getters and setters — and two callers who each have to remember the rule:

```java id=walletMess
class Wallet {                                   // anemic: the rule lives in the callers' heads
    private long balance;
    long getBalance() { return balance; }
    void setBalance(long balance) { this.balance = balance; }
}

void payForService(Wallet w, long amount) {      // one careful caller
    if (w.getBalance() >= amount) w.setBalance(w.getBalance() - amount);
}

void applyLateFee(Wallet w, long amount) {       // and one that forgot
    w.setBalance(w.getBalance() - amount);
}
```

With ₹500 in the wallet, `applyLateFee(w, 800)` leaves it at −300; the checker runs it. The anemic version compiles, runs, and passes the happy path — and every reviewer marks it down, because *every* caller must re-check the rule, and one of them won't.

**An invariant is a rule that must hold for an object's whole lifetime.** A slot's end is after its start. A wallet never goes negative. A booking's slot is long enough for its jobs. A bay holds at most one active booking at any instant. The design question is never "is there a check somewhere?" but **"can any code path produce an object that breaks it?"**

### Better design

The fix moves the rule into the object it protects:

```java group=wallet
class InsufficientFundsException extends RuntimeException {
    InsufficientFundsException(long balance, long amount) {
        super("balance " + balance + " cannot cover " + amount);
    }
}

class Wallet {                                   // rich: the object defends itself
    private long balance;                        // paise; starts at zero

    public void credit(long amount) {
        if (amount <= 0) throw new IllegalArgumentException("amount must be positive");
        balance = Math.addExact(balance, amount);
    }

    public void debit(long amount) {
        if (amount <= 0) throw new IllegalArgumentException("amount must be positive");
        if (amount > balance) throw new InsufficientFundsException(balance, amount);
        balance -= amount;
    }

    public long balance() { return balance; }
}
```

There is no setter, so there is no code path that can write a negative balance. The guards run **before** the one line that changes state, so a refused debit leaves the wallet exactly as it was (the checker confirms ₹500 stays ₹500 after a refused debit of ₹800). The rule of thumb: **behaviour lives with the data it guards.** If you find a service doing `if (wallet.getBalance() >= amount) wallet.setBalance(...)`, that logic is homesick.

**The enforcement ladder, cheapest first:**

1. **Types.** A `Plate` can't be confused with a `BayId`; an `enum` can't hold an unknown status; `Money` can't be negative. Checked by the compiler, for free.
2. **Constructor validation.** An object that constructed successfully is valid — forever, if it is immutable; valid so far, if not. **Constructed means valid** is the promise every later chapter (and the <a href={`${BASE}lld/lessons/creational-patterns/`}>creational patterns lesson's</a> builders) leans on.
3. **Factory methods.** A static method with a name — `Money.of("1499", "INR")`, `TimeSlot.of(start, length)`, `OpeningHours.slot(...)` — can parse, normalise and validate before construction, can say what it does, and can return a cached instance or a subtype. When every path *must* go through the factory, make the constructor private.
4. **Guarded mutators, no setters.** Private fields; every state change is a method named for the business action (`debit`, `confirm`, `checkIn`) that checks first and changes second.
5. **Make illegal states unrepresentable.** The strongest rung: design the types so the bad combination can't even be written down. The phrase is usually credited to Yaron Minsky.

That last rung is the one people skip. Model a booking's lifecycle with three flags:

```java fragment
class Booking {
    boolean paid, cancelled, checkedIn;      // 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 combinations
    Instant checkedInAt;                     // "null unless checkedIn": a rule only a comment enforces
}
```

Eight combinations can be written, and only five mean anything: held, paid, cancelled before paying, cancelled after paying, checked in. "Checked in but not paid", "cancelled and checked in", and "paid, cancelled and checked in" are representable, so sooner or later some code path will produce one. A single `status` enum with one constant per real state makes the illegal three impossible to express. (The checker enumerates all eight and counts the five.)

    F --> C["Constructor validation\nconstructed = valid"]

The same question — *what data does this rule need?* — tells you where every invariant goes.

</Reveal>

**Your turn.** The workshop's price list: every job has exactly one price, all in one currency, and when prices change on Monday, the old list must stay exactly as it was. Write `PriceList` as a record that can't be changed from outside, even by the caller who built it.

<HintLadder hints={hints.priceList} labels={turn}>

```java group=workshop
record PriceList(Map<Job, Money> prices) {
    PriceList {
        prices = Map.copyOf(prices);                         // a private, unmodifiable copy
        for (Job job : Job.values())
            if (!prices.containsKey(job)) throw new IllegalArgumentException("no price for " + job);
        if (prices.values().stream().map(Money::currency).distinct().count() > 1)
            throw new IllegalArgumentException("one currency per price list");
    }

    Money priceOf(Job job) { return prices.get(job); }

    PriceList with(Job job, Money price) {                   // a changed copy; this list never changes
        Map<Job, Money> next = new HashMap<>(prices);
        next.put(job, price);
        return new PriceList(next);
    }
}
```

`Map.copyOf` makes the record a true value: the checker changes the caller's map after construction and the price list doesn't move, and `prices().put(...)` throws `UnsupportedOperationException`. `with` is a *wither*: the immutable-object way to "change" something, returning a new value and leaving the old one alone. A missing job or a mixed-currency list is refused at birth.

</HintLadder>

<h2 id="tell-dont-ask">Chapter 7: Encapsulation and tell, don’t ask</h2>

### Concept

Encapsulation means an object's invariants can only change through methods that keep them. "Tell, don't ask": do not fetch an object's data, decide something with it and push the result back. Tell the object what you want and let it decide.

### Problem

A `Bay` has a getter that returns its live `Set` of capabilities. A helper class adds `"LIFT"` to it to make a brake check book. Bay B2 now claims a lift it has never had, and a mechanic arrives with tools to a ceiling with nothing on it. The getter handed a stranger the key, and every caller re-derives the booking rule for itself.

### Bad design

A getter that hands out the object's live internals, and callers who ask for data and make the decision themselves:

```java id=bayMess
class Bay {
    private final Set<String> capabilities = new HashSet<>();
    Bay(String... capabilities) { this.capabilities.addAll(List.of(capabilities)); }
    Set<String> getCapabilities() { return capabilities; }        // hands out the real set
}

boolean canBook(Bay bay, String job) {                              // every caller re-derives the rule
    return !job.equals("BRAKE_CHECK") || bay.getCapabilities().contains("LIFT");
}

void quickFix(Bay bay) { bay.getCapabilities().add("LIFT"); }       // "now it books"
```

For a bay built with no capabilities, `canBook(b2, "BRAKE_CHECK")` is false. After `quickFix(b2)` it's true, and the checker confirms it. The bay now claims a lift it doesn't have.

### Better design

**Encapsulation is not "private fields with public getters and setters".** A field with a public getter and setter is a public field with extra typing. Encapsulation means **the object's invariants can only be changed through methods that keep them**. Getters and setters for every field, generated by reflex, give that away.

**Tell, don't ask.** Don't ask an object for its data, make a decision with it and push the result back. Tell the object what you want and let it decide with its own rules. (Andy Hunt and Dave Thomas popularised the phrase; Martin Fowler's bliki entry discusses it and its limits.) Five working rules:

1. **No setters on anything with a rule.** Name the business action instead: `debit`, `confirm`, `upgradeTo`.
2. **Getters return values or read-only views — never the live collection.** `Set.copyOf`, `List.copyOf`, or `Collections.unmodifiableSet`.
3. **Ask, then act on the same object? Move the act into the object.** `if (bay.has(LIFT) || !job.needsLift())` written by callers becomes `bay.canDo(job)`.
4. **Queries are fine.** Screens, reports and tests need to *read*. `booking.total()` and `bay.capabilities()` are ordinary. The smell is a *decision* about an object's state made outside it.
5. **Talk to friends, not to friends of friends.** `shop.find(id).lines().get(0).agreedPrice().currency()` — a *train wreck* — couples the caller to four classes' shapes. The *Law of Demeter* says: call methods on yourself, your fields, your parameters and objects you create. When you need something four links away, ask the first link for it.

```java group=workshop
final class Bay {
    private final BayId id;
    private final Set<Capability> capabilities;

    Bay(BayId id, Set<Capability> capabilities) {
        if (id == null) throw new IllegalArgumentException("id required");
        this.id = id;
        this.capabilities = Set.copyOf(capabilities);        // the caller's set can't reach in
    }

    boolean canDo(Job job) { return capabilities.containsAll(job.needs()); }   // the bay decides
    Set<Capability> capabilities() { return capabilities; }  // read-only: Set.copyOf is unmodifiable
    BayId id() { return id; }
}
```

`canDo` reads the job's needs as data — "do I have everything this job needs?" — so a future job that needs an `EV_CHARGER` works without touching `Bay`. `capabilities()` is still a getter, and that's fine: it returns an unmodifiable set, so the checker's `bay.capabilities().add(LIFT)` throws instead of fitting a lift. `Bay` doesn't override `equals`: there is exactly one `Bay` object per bay, owned by the workshop, so reference identity is the honest equality (Chapter 3's first option).

    Note over C,B: Ask (bad)

    B-->>C: live Set
    C->>C: decide, even add LIFT

<h2 id="lifecycle">Chapter 8: Lifecycle as a state machine</h2>

### Concept

A lifecycle is a state machine. Put the legal edges on the status enum, give the entity one named method per transition, and make each method check the edge, then its own business condition, then change state. No status setter.

### Problem

A customer cancels a booking and sees "Cancelled". The next morning a mechanic waits for a car that is not coming: the booking says "Confirmed". The booking service only confirms held bookings, but a nightly importer called `setStatus` directly with whatever the spreadsheet said. Once a setter exists every caller is trusted, and one of them is wrong.

### Bad design

The rule lives in a service; the setter lets everyone else skip it:

```java id=statusMess
enum Status { HELD, CONFIRMED, CANCELLED }

class Booking {
    private Status status = Status.HELD;
    Status getStatus() { return status; }
    void setStatus(Status status) { this.status = status; }
}

class BookingService {                                // the rule lives here...
    void confirm(Booking b) {
        if (b.getStatus() != Status.HELD) throw new IllegalStateException("only a held booking can be confirmed");
        b.setStatus(Status.CONFIRMED);
    }
}

class NightlyImporter {                               // ...and nowhere here
    void apply(Booking b, String fromSpreadsheet) { b.setStatus(Status.valueOf(fromSpreadsheet)); }
}
```

The checker cancels a booking, runs the importer with `"CONFIRMED"`, and the cancelled booking is confirmed again. `BookingService` never got a say. **Once a setter exists, every caller is trusted, and one of them will betray you.**

### Better design

**The fix has two halves. The legal edges belong to the status type; the transitions belong to the entity.** First, an enum that knows its own state machine:

```java group=workshop
enum BookingStatus {
    HELD, CONFIRMED, CHECKED_IN, COMPLETED, CANCELLED, EXPIRED, NO_SHOW;

    Set<BookingStatus> next() {
        return switch (this) {
            case HELD       -> EnumSet.of(CONFIRMED, CANCELLED, EXPIRED);
            case CONFIRMED  -> EnumSet.of(CHECKED_IN, CANCELLED, NO_SHOW);
            case CHECKED_IN -> EnumSet.of(COMPLETED);
            case COMPLETED, CANCELLED, EXPIRED, NO_SHOW -> EnumSet.noneOf(BookingStatus.class);
        };
    }

    boolean canMoveTo(BookingStatus target) { return next().contains(target); }
    boolean isTerminal() { return next().isEmpty(); }
    boolean occupiesBay() { return this == HELD || this == CONFIRMED || this == CHECKED_IN; }
}
```

The `switch` is an expression, so the compiler checks it covers every constant: add a status and forget its edges, and the build fails. Terminal states return an empty set, so "is it finished?" is *derived* from the table rather than stored as a second fact that could disagree with it. `occupiesBay()` is behaviour on the enum: the bay's schedule will ask it which bookings still block a slot.

Then the entity. **No setter for the status, ever** — each legal move is a method named for what happens in the workshop, and each one does three things in order: check the edge, check its own condition, then change the state.

```java group=workshop
record BookingLine(Job job, Money agreedPrice) {          // the price is copied in when the booking is made
    BookingLine {
        if (job == null || agreedPrice == null) throw new IllegalArgumentException("job and price required");
    }
}

final class Booking {
    private final BookingId id;
    private final CustomerId customer;                      // another aggregate: held by id
    private final BayId bay;
    private final Plate plate;
    private final TimeSlot slot;
    private final List<BookingLine> lines;
    private final Instant heldUntil;
    private BookingStatus status = BookingStatus.HELD;      // no setter, ever

    Booking(BookingId id, CustomerId customer, BayId bay, Plate plate, TimeSlot slot,
            List<BookingLine> lines, Instant heldUntil) {
        if (id == null || customer == null || bay == null || plate == null || slot == null || heldUntil == null)
            throw new IllegalArgumentException("every field of a booking is required");
        this.id = id;
        this.customer = customer;
        this.bay = bay;
        this.plate = plate;
        this.slot = slot;
        this.heldUntil = heldUntil;
        this.lines = List.copyOf(lines);
        if (this.lines.isEmpty()) throw new IllegalArgumentException("a booking needs at least one job");
        Duration work = Duration.ZERO;
        for (BookingLine line : this.lines) work = work.plus(line.job().duration());
        if (slot.length().compareTo(work) < 0)
            throw new IllegalArgumentException("slot is " + slot.length().toMinutes() + " min; the jobs need " + work.toMinutes());
        total();                                             // throws if the lines mix currencies
    }

    void confirm(Instant at) {
        requireEdge(BookingStatus.CONFIRMED);
        if (!at.isBefore(heldUntil)) throw new IllegalStateException("hold on " + id.value() + " expired at " + heldUntil);
        status = BookingStatus.CONFIRMED;
    }

    void expire(Instant at) {
        requireEdge(BookingStatus.EXPIRED);
        if (at.isBefore(heldUntil)) throw new IllegalStateException("hold on " + id.value() + " runs until " + heldUntil);
        status = BookingStatus.EXPIRED;
    }

    void cancel(Instant at) {
        requireEdge(BookingStatus.CANCELLED);
        if (!at.isBefore(slot.start())) throw new IllegalStateException("too late to cancel " + id.value() + ": the slot has started");
        status = BookingStatus.CANCELLED;
    }

    void checkIn(Plate arriving, Instant at) {
        requireEdge(BookingStatus.CHECKED_IN);
        if (!arriving.equals(plate)) throw new IllegalArgumentException(arriving.value() + " is not the car booked on " + id.value());
        if (!at.isBefore(slot.end())) throw new IllegalStateException("the slot for " + id.value() + " is over");
        status = BookingStatus.CHECKED_IN;
    }

    void complete() {
        requireEdge(BookingStatus.COMPLETED);
        status = BookingStatus.COMPLETED;
    }

    void markNoShow(Instant at) {
        requireEdge(BookingStatus.NO_SHOW);
        if (at.isBefore(slot.start())) throw new IllegalStateException("the slot for " + id.value() + " has not started");
        status = BookingStatus.NO_SHOW;
    }

    private void requireEdge(BookingStatus target) {
        if (!status.canMoveTo(target))
            throw new IllegalStateException(id.value() + ": " + status + " -> " + target + " is not allowed");
    }

    Money total() {
        Money sum = Money.zero(lines.get(0).agreedPrice().currency());
        for (BookingLine line : lines) sum = sum.plus(line.agreedPrice());
        return sum;
    }

    boolean occupies(TimeSlot other) { return status.occupiesBay() && slot.overlaps(other); }

    BookingId id() { return id; }
    CustomerId customer() { return customer; }
    BayId bay() { return bay; }
    Plate plate() { return plate; }
    TimeSlot slot() { return slot; }
    List<BookingLine> lines() { return lines; }             // unmodifiable: List.copyOf
    Instant heldUntil() { return heldUntil; }
    BookingStatus status() { return status; }

    @Override public boolean equals(Object o) { return o instanceof Booking b && id.equals(b.id); }
    @Override public int hashCode() { return id.hashCode(); }
}
```

Read it as a list of promises, each of which the checker tests:

- **Born valid.** At least one job, a slot long enough for all of them (a brake check refuses a 30-minute slot), one currency. A `Booking` that exists satisfies all three.
- **Every edge goes through the table.** The checker drives a booking into each of the seven states and fires all six transitions at it: 42 combinations. Exactly the seven edges in the enum succeed; the other 35 throw `IllegalStateException` and leave the status unchanged.
- **Each transition guards its own business condition.** Confirm only before the hold runs out. Expire only after it has. Cancel only before the slot starts. Check in only with the booked car's plate — compared as a `Plate` value, so `"ka-01-zz-0002"` matches `KA01ZZ0002` — and before the slot ends. Mark a no-show only once the slot has started.
- **A refused transition changes nothing**, because every check runs before the one assignment.
- **Equality is by id** (Chapter 3), `lines()` is unmodifiable, and the customer is held by id (Chapter 12 explains why).

**Enums with behaviour are one of Java's best modelling tools.** An enum constant is a singleton object: it can have fields (`Job`'s duration), methods (`BookingStatus.next()`), and even constant-specific method bodies. `EnumSet` and `EnumMap` are the fast, compact collections for them. **Enum table or State pattern?** The table is right when only the **edges** vary between states. When each state also **behaves** differently — a `CONFIRMED` booking sends reminders, a `CHECKED_IN` one starts a job timer — promote to one class per state (the State pattern, in the <a href={`${BASE}lld/lessons/behavioral-patterns/`}>behavioral patterns lesson</a>), and keep the table as the guard. <h2 id="relationships">Chapter 9: Four kinds of arrow</h2>

### Concept

Four relationships carry most of the meaning in a class diagram: association (knows about), aggregation (groups parts that live on their own), composition (owns parts that die with it) and dependency (uses briefly). The question that picks the arrow: what happens to the other end when this object dies?

### Problem

You draw the model and every arrow looks alike. Is Booking to BookingLine the same kind of line as Booking to Customer? If you cannot say what a diamond promises about lifetime and ownership, the diagram communicates nothing, and the code is probably unclear too.

### Better design

UML gives you a handful of arrows for relationships. In an interview, four of them carry almost all the meaning — plus the two for types. **What matters is what each one promises about lifetime and knowledge, and what it looks like in Java:**

| Relationship | It means | Lifetime | In Java | Mermaid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Association** | A lasting "knows about" | Independent | A field — often just the other aggregate's id | `A --> B` |
| **Aggregation** | Whole and part; the parts exist on their own and may be shared | Parts outlive the whole | A field holding objects passed in from outside | `A o-- B` |
| **Composition** | Whole and part; exclusive ownership | Parts die with the whole | The whole creates or copies the parts, never hands out a mutable reference | `A *-- B` |
| **Dependency** | Uses, briefly | None | A parameter, a local variable, a return type | `A ..> B` |
| Inheritance | Is a kind of (class) | — | `extends` | `B <\|-- A` |
| Realisation | Implements (interface) | — | `implements` | `I <\|.. A` |

In the workshop's own code:

```java fragment
// Composition: the schedule creates its map, adds bookings itself, and never hands out the map.
final class BaySchedule { private final Map<BookingId, Booking> bookings = new LinkedHashMap<>(); }

// Aggregation: a crew holds mechanics who were hired before it and stay after it.
final class Crew { private final List<Mechanic> members; Crew(List<Mechanic> m) { members = List.copyOf(m); } }

// Association, by id: a booking knows its customer, but doesn't own or load it.
final class Booking { private final CustomerId customer; }

// Dependency: the policy uses a booking for one call and keeps nothing.
record CancellationPolicy(Duration freeUntilBefore, long percent) { Money feeFor(Booking booking, Instant at) { /* ... */ } }
```

**Composition is a promise you keep, not a keyword.** Java has no syntax for "owns". The promise holds only if the whole builds (or defensively copies) its parts and never lets a mutable reference escape: `Booking` copies its lines with `List.copyOf`; `BaySchedule` returns `List.copyOf(bookings.values())`, never the map.

**Aggregation is the weakest arrow.** In Java it looks exactly like an association, and the difference is only intent. Martin Fowler's *UML Distilled* advises leaving it out of your own diagrams for that reason. In an interview, use it when "these parts outlive the whole" is a point worth making; otherwise a plain association is clearer.

**Multiplicity and direction carry meaning too.** `"1" *-- "many"` says a schedule has many bookings and each booking belongs to exactly one schedule. An arrowhead says which side can navigate to the other. **Default to one-way**: every two-way relationship is two references that must agree, and Chapter 11 shows what happens when they don't.

<h2 id="inheritance">Chapter 10: Inheritance or composition</h2>

### Concept

Prefer composition: model a variation as a field (a set of capabilities, a tier enum) rather than a subclass. Inherit only when the subtype is substitutable, is that kind forever, varies in behaviour, and the parent was designed for extension.

### Problem

A lobby board shows "EV bay B3 (0 kW)" for a 22 kW charger: the base constructor calls an overridable method, which runs before the subclass's field is assigned. A second report says that when a customer was upgraded to gold the system made a second copy of him: `GoldCustomer extends Customer` cannot be reached by upgrading an existing object. Both bugs come from inheritance used where a field would do.

### Bad design

A base class whose constructor calls a method a subclass overrides:

```java id=bayTreeMess
class Bay {
    private final String label;
    Bay(String code) { this.label = describe(code); }      // calls a method a subclass can override
    String describe(String code) { return "Bay " + code; }
    String label() { return label; }
}

class EvBay extends Bay {
    private final int kilowatts;
    EvBay(String code, int kilowatts) {
        super(code);                                        // runs Bay's constructor first...
        this.kilowatts = kilowatts;                         // ...and only then sets this
    }
    @Override String describe(String code) { return "EV bay " + code + " (" + kilowatts + " kW)"; }
}
```

`new EvBay("B3", 22).label()` is **"EV bay B3 (0 kW)"**, and the checker confirms it. Java runs the superclass constructor before the subclass's field assignments; the call to `describe` inside it is virtual, so it reaches `EvBay`'s override, which reads `kilowatts` while it still holds its default, 0. Even a `final` field can be observed before it's assigned this way. *Effective Java* Item 19 puts the rule bluntly: **constructors must not invoke overridable methods.**

### Better design

This is the **fragile base class problem** in its constructor form. The <a href={`${BASE}lld/lessons/solid/`}>SOLID lesson</a>'s Chapter 4 shows the other form — a subclass depending on how its parent's methods call each other (`addAll` calling `add`). Both have the same cause: **a subclass is coupled to its parent's internals**, including the order in which the parent does things. The narrow fix is to stop the constructor calling anything overridable:

```java id=bayTreeFixed
class Bay {
    private final String code;
    Bay(String code) { this.code = code; }                  // the constructor only stores
    String code() { return code; }
    String label() { return "Bay " + code; }               // computed when asked, on a finished object
}

class EvBay extends Bay {
    private final int kilowatts;
    EvBay(String code, int kilowatts) {
        super(code);
        this.kilowatts = kilowatts;
    }
    @Override String label() { return "EV bay " + code() + " (" + kilowatts + " kW)"; }
}
```

Now the label reads **"EV bay B3 (22 kW)"**. That is also why the workshop's `Mechanic` and `Customer` are `final` classes: their constructors call their own methods (`rename`), which is safe only when no subclass can override them.

**The deeper fix is a modelling question: should there be a tree at all?** A lift bay, an EV bay, a bay with a lift *and* a charger: as subclasses that's `LiftBay`, `EvBay`, `EvLiftBay`, and a class for every combination of the next capability too. As composition it's one `Bay` with a `Set<Capability>` — which is exactly what Chapter 7's `Bay` is, and why `canDo` reads the job's needs as data. The same failure shows up in Parking Lot rounds: `ElectricSUV extends SUV extends Car extends Vehicle` dies when the interviewer asks for an electric *bike*, because the follow-up cuts across the tree. **A `VehicleType` enum plus composed capabilities beats a class tree whose branches you guessed wrong. Deep hierarchies are guesses about the future; composition defers the guess.**

**Four is-a tests.** Inheritance passes only when all four do:

1. **Substitutable under every operation the parent supports.** That's Liskov. `Square extends Rectangle` fails it: `setWidth(5); setHeight(4)` gives 16 on a square where every caller expects 20 (the SOLID lesson's <a href={`${BASE}lld/lessons/solid/#substitutable`}>Chapter 3</a> runs it). The fix there was modelling the real variation — immutable `Rect` and `Square` records behind a `Shape` interface — not cleverer inheritance.
2. **Is-a forever.** An object can't change its class. If the "kind" can change during the object's life — standard to gold, active to suspended — it's a role or a state, so make it a field.
3. **The parent was designed for extension.** Documented hooks, no surprising self-calls, no overridable calls in constructors. A parent that wasn't should be `final`, and *Effective Java* Item 19 says exactly that: design and document for inheritance, or else prohibit it.
4. **The subtype varies in behaviour, not just data.** A bike that pays 10 an hour and a car that pays 20 differ in a number, which is a constructor argument, not a subclass.

Test 2 is the second bug. With `GoldCustomer extends Customer`, upgrading Kofi means building a *new* object with Kofi's id; every booking, session and cache that held the old one now holds a stale Kofi:

```java fragment
class GoldCustomer extends Customer {
    @Override Money price(Money list) { return list.minus(list.percent(10)); }
}
Customer kofi = customers.find(new CustomerId("C-2"));   // created years ago, as a Customer
// He completes his fifth service. There is no way to turn this object into a GoldCustomer.
// The only option is a new GoldCustomer with the same id: two Kofis in memory.
```

The role is a field; the behaviour that varies by role lives on an enum:

```java group=workshop
enum Tier {
    STANDARD(0), GOLD(10);

    private final long percentOff;
    Tier(long percentOff) { this.percentOff = percentOff; }

    Money price(Money listPrice) { return listPrice.minus(listPrice.percent(percentOff)); }
}

final class Customer {
    private final CustomerId id;
    private final String name;
    private Tier tier = Tier.STANDARD;                       // a role that changes: a field, not a subclass

    Customer(CustomerId id, String name) {
        if (id == null) throw new IllegalArgumentException("id required");
        if (name == null || name.isBlank()) throw new IllegalArgumentException("name required");
        this.id = id;
        this.name = name.strip();
    }

    void upgradeTo(Tier next) {
        if (next.compareTo(tier) <= 0) throw new IllegalStateException(id.value() + " is already " + tier);
        tier = next;
    }

    CustomerId id() { return id; }
    String name() { return name; }
    Tier tier() { return tier; }

    @Override public boolean equals(Object o) { return o instanceof Customer c && id.equals(c.id); }
    @Override public int hashCode() { return id.hashCode(); }
}
```

Kofi is now the same object before and after his upgrade. A gold brake check costs ₹999.00 − 10% = **₹899.10**; a gold oil change costs **₹1,349.10**. Both are in the checker. `upgradeTo` refuses sideways and downward moves, which says, in code, that this desk only promotes.

**Interface or abstract class?** When you do need a type hierarchy, choose the tool deliberately:

| | `interface` | `abstract class` |
|---|---|---|
| Contract | A pure capability: "can price a job" | A shared skeleton: "is 80% of a job pricer" |
| State | None (constants only) | Fields allowed |
| How many? | A class implements many | A class extends exactly one |
| Use it for | A **seam** callers depend on | A **partial implementation** shared by close siblings |

Default to interfaces for every boundary between components: that is the seam a Strategy or an Adapter later plugs into. Reach for an abstract class only when siblings genuinely share code *and* state — Template Method territory. The War Room's <a href={`${BASE}lld/war/patterns-why/#7-template-method--forced-by-module-dependency-direction`}>Template Method dossier</a> shows one that was forced by module dependency direction rather than chosen by taste. And when the hierarchy is a closed set of data shapes, Java 17's `sealed` interfaces with record implementations give you a hierarchy the compiler can check exhaustively.

### When to use

Use inheritance for substitutable permanent subtypes and for skeletons built for extension (Template Method, sealed hierarchies). Use interfaces for seams. Use fields for roles and capabilities.

<h2 id="many-to-many">Chapter 11: Many-to-many</h2>

### Concept

Keep a many-to-many relationship in one place. Go one-way when you can, make the link an object when the pair has data of its own, and give its owner one index per direction (the in-memory version of a join table with a reverse index).

### Problem

A work order keeps a list of its crew and each mechanic keeps a list of their jobs. `assign` updates one list. A mechanic opens the app and sees nothing for today, while the manager sees him as the lead on a booking. No language keeps two collections in sync, and removal is where the second bug usually lives.

### Bad design

A many-to-many relationship stored as a collection on *both* sides, updated on one:

```java id=crewMess
class Mechanic {
    final String name;
    final List<WorkOrder> jobs = new ArrayList<>();
    Mechanic(String name) { this.name = name; }
}

class WorkOrder {
    final String id;
    final List<Mechanic> crew = new ArrayList<>();
    WorkOrder(String id) { this.id = id; }
    void assign(Mechanic m) { crew.add(m); }        // updates one side only
}
```

After `order.assign(ravi)`, the order's crew contains Ravi and `ravi.jobs` is empty. The checker confirms it. **No language keeps two collections in sync. Two references that must agree are two places to forget**, including on removal, which is where the second bug usually lives.

### Better design

The <a href={`${BASE}sd/lessons/databases/#many-to-many`}>databases lesson's Chapter 3</a> solves it for tables: one row per pair in a join table, a primary key for one direction, a reverse index for the other, and the relationship's own facts on the row. **Objects have the same three shapes, and you choose by asking which questions the system asks, and whether the relationship has data of its own.**

| Shape | In Java | Use it when | Table twin |
|---|---|---|---|
| **One-way collection** | `Mechanic` has `EnumSet<Job> skills` | One direction is asked; the other is rare and can be computed | A column you scan for the reverse query |
| **Two-way collections** | Lists on both sides, updated together | Both directions are asked often and the relationship has no data | Two foreign-key lists that must agree (avoid) |
| **Link object** | `Assignment(booking, mechanic, role)` held by one owner, indexed both ways | The relationship has its own data (a role, a price, a date) or its own lifecycle | Join table + reverse index |

**When the relationship has data, it is an object.** A mechanic's *role* isn't a property of Ravi (he leads one job and assists on another) or of the booking (it has one lead and several assistants). It's a property of the pair. So the pair becomes a record, and one class owns all of them and keeps one index per question:

```java group=workshop
enum Role { LEAD, ASSIST }

record Assignment(BookingId booking, MechanicId mechanic, Role role) {   // the relationship, with its own data
    Assignment {
        if (booking == null || mechanic == null || role == null) throw new IllegalArgumentException("all fields required");
    }
}

final class Roster {                     // owns every assignment; one index per question
    private final Map<BookingId, Map<MechanicId, Assignment>> byBooking = new HashMap<>();
    private final Map<MechanicId, Map<BookingId, Assignment>> byMechanic = new HashMap<>();

    void assign(Assignment a) {
        Map<MechanicId, Assignment> crew = byBooking.getOrDefault(a.booking(), Map.of());
        if (crew.containsKey(a.mechanic()))
            throw new IllegalStateException(a.mechanic().value() + " is already on " + a.booking().value());
        if (a.role() == Role.LEAD && crew.values().stream().anyMatch(x -> x.role() == Role.LEAD))
            throw new IllegalStateException(a.booking().value() + " already has a lead");
        byBooking.computeIfAbsent(a.booking(), k -> new LinkedHashMap<>()).put(a.mechanic(), a);
        byMechanic.computeIfAbsent(a.mechanic(), k -> new LinkedHashMap<>()).put(a.booking(), a);
    }

    void unassign(BookingId booking, MechanicId mechanic) {
        Map<MechanicId, Assignment> crew = byBooking.get(booking);
        if (crew == null || !crew.containsKey(mechanic))
            throw new IllegalStateException(mechanic.value() + " is not on " + booking.value());
        crew.remove(mechanic);
        if (crew.isEmpty()) byBooking.remove(booking);
        Map<BookingId, Assignment> jobs = byMechanic.get(mechanic);
        jobs.remove(booking);
        if (jobs.isEmpty()) byMechanic.remove(mechanic);
    }

    List<Assignment> crewOf(BookingId booking) { return List.copyOf(byBooking.getOrDefault(booking, Map.of()).values()); }
    List<Assignment> jobsOf(MechanicId mechanic) { return List.copyOf(byMechanic.getOrDefault(mechanic, Map.of()).values()); }
}
```

`Roster` is the only code that writes either map, and both writes happen in the same method, so the two directions can't disagree — `assign` and `unassign` keep them in step, including the clean-up of empty entries. "One lead per booking" is checked where all of a booking's assignments are visible. Both questions are one map lookup. The checker runs 3,000 random assigns and unassigns against a plain list of assignments and checks both directions after every step.

**Where a link lives depends on what it must stay consistent with.** The workshop has two many-to-many links, and they live in different places on purpose:

- **`BookingLine`** links bookings and jobs, with the agreed price on the link. It must agree with the booking's total, and it means nothing without the booking, so **the booking owns its lines** (composition, Chapter 9).
- **`Assignment`** links bookings and mechanics. Neither side owns the other, and changing a crew mustn't lock a booking, so **assignments hold both ids and live in their own `Roster`**.

<h2 id="aggregates">Chapter 12: Aggregates and consistency boundaries</h2>

### Concept

An aggregate is a cluster of objects changed as one unit, with one entity, the root, as the only door. The invariant draws the boundary: the smallest cluster that can see everything the rule mentions.

### Problem

Two cars arrive at bay B1 at the same time, each with a confirmation. One was booked on the website, which checks for clashes before saving. The other was booked by phone and loaded by a nightly import that writes rows without checking. Even through the website, two concurrent requests can both see a free slot and both save. The check lives in a service, so other doors skip it and concurrency beats it.

### Bad design

The overlap check lives in a service, as an ask-then-act, and a second code path skips it:

```java fragment
class BookingService {
    void book(Booking candidate) {
        for (Booking other : repository.findByBay(candidate.bay()))     // ask...
            if (other.occupies(candidate.slot())) throw new IllegalStateException("taken");
        repository.save(candidate);                                     // ...then act
    }
}
class AdminImport {
    void load(List<Booking> rows) { rows.forEach(repository::save); }    // a second door, with no check
}
```

The check can be skipped by any other door, and under concurrency it can be beaten even through this door: two requests both ask, both see a free slot, and both save.

### Better design

**An aggregate is a cluster of objects that is changed as one unit, with one entity — the root — as its only door.** The term comes from Eric Evans's *Domain-Driven Design*. Outside code may hold a reference to the root and nothing else inside; every change goes through the root's methods; and the root makes sure every invariant inside the boundary holds after each change.

**The invariant draws the boundary.** "No two active bookings share a bay at the same time" talks about all the bookings of *one bay*. So:

| Boundary | Can it see what the rule needs? | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Each `Booking` | No: one booking can't see its siblings | — |
| **One schedule per bay** | **Yes: exactly the bookings the rule talks about** | **Bookings for different bays never contend** |
| The whole `Workshop` | Yes | Every booking in every bay waits on one lock |

(You can go smaller still: slots can't cross midnight — `OpeningHours` refuses them — so *one bay, one day* is also a valid boundary. Say so if the interviewer pushes on contention.)

Vaughn Vernon's rules of thumb for aggregates are the ones interviewers expect at this depth:

1. **Protect real invariants inside a consistency boundary.** The boundary exists for a rule, not for tidiness.
2. **Design small aggregates.** Big ones contend, load slowly, and fail together.
3. **Reference other aggregates by identity.** `Booking` holds a `CustomerId`, not a `Customer`, so loading a booking doesn't drag a customer along, and no booking method can change a customer.
4. **Use eventual consistency outside the boundary.** A rule that spans two aggregates is enforced by a follow-up step, not one transaction (Extensions 2 and 3).

In a database the same boundary becomes **one transaction per aggregate**: a row lock on the bay, or a version column checked on save (optimistic locking). In a 90-minute round, saying "this is my consistency boundary, and in production it's one transaction or one lock" earns the point; the <a href={`${BASE}lld/lessons/concurrency-for-lld/`}>concurrency lesson</a> builds the lock. This model is deliberately single-threaded.

```java group=workshop
final class BaySchedule {                                      // aggregate root: one per bay
    private final Bay bay;
    private final Map<BookingId, Booking> bookings = new LinkedHashMap<>();

    BaySchedule(Bay bay) {
        if (bay == null) throw new IllegalArgumentException("bay required");
        this.bay = bay;
    }

    /** The only way into the schedule. Checks every rule that needs the other bookings. */
    void hold(Booking candidate, Instant now) {
        if (!candidate.bay().equals(bay.id()))
            throw new IllegalArgumentException(candidate.id().value() + " is for another bay");
        if (candidate.status() != BookingStatus.HELD)
            throw new IllegalArgumentException("only a fresh hold can join a schedule");
        if (bookings.containsKey(candidate.id()))
            throw new IllegalStateException("duplicate booking id " + candidate.id().value());
        for (BookingLine line : candidate.lines())
            if (!bay.canDo(line.job())) throw new IllegalArgumentException(bay.id().value() + " cannot do " + line.job());
        expireHolds(now);                                       // stale holds stop blocking the slot
        for (Booking other : bookings.values())
            if (other.occupies(candidate.slot()))
                throw new IllegalStateException(bay.id().value() + " is taken: overlaps " + other.id().value());
        bookings.put(candidate.id(), candidate);
    }

    void confirm(BookingId id, Instant at)              { get(id).confirm(at); }
    void cancel(BookingId id, Instant at)               { get(id).cancel(at); }
    void checkIn(BookingId id, Plate plate, Instant at) { get(id).checkIn(plate, at); }
    void complete(BookingId id)                         { get(id).complete(); }
    void markNoShow(BookingId id, Instant at)           { get(id).markNoShow(at); }

    int expireHolds(Instant now) {
        int expired = 0;
        for (Booking b : bookings.values())
            if (b.status() == BookingStatus.HELD && !now.isBefore(b.heldUntil())) {
                b.expire(now);
                expired++;
            }
        return expired;
    }

    Optional<Booking> find(BookingId id) { return Optional.ofNullable(bookings.get(id)); }
    List<Booking> bookings() { return List.copyOf(bookings.values()); }
    Bay bay() { return bay; }

    private Booking get(BookingId id) {
        Booking b = bookings.get(id);
        if (b == null) throw new IllegalArgumentException("no booking " + id.value() + " on " + bay.id().value());
        return b;
    }
}
```

**The division of labour is the whole lesson in miniature.** `Booking` checks everything it can see alone (its slot fits its jobs; its transitions are legal). `BaySchedule` checks everything that needs the siblings (no clash; the bay can do the jobs; no duplicate id) and sweeps stale holds before checking, so an expired hold never blocks a new booking. There is one door — `hold` — and no other way to add a booking. The checker throws 2,000 random holds, confirmations, cancellations and clock jumps at one schedule, and after every step asserts that no two bookings that occupy the bay overlap, and that each hold was accepted exactly when a brute-force check said it should be.

<h2 id="whole-model">Chapter 13: Brief to running code</h2>

### Concept

The application service wires the model together without business rules of its own: it translates outside words (a day and time in Copperline, a list of jobs) into model types, asks the right aggregate to act, and owns the dependencies (clock, id generator). The composition root is where every concrete choice is named.

### Problem

With all the pieces built, two things are left: the cancellation-fee policy, and a way to prove the whole model works. Without a scripted day that includes the things that go wrong (a clash, an expired hold, a late cancellation), you do not know whether every sentence of the brief has an owner.

### Better design

Two pieces are left: the policy for cancellation fees, and the **application service** that wires the model together. The service makes no business decisions of its own. It translates the outside world's words (a day and a time in Copperline, a list of jobs) into the model's types, asks the right aggregate to act, and owns the dependencies (the clock and the id generator).

```java group=workshop
record CancellationPolicy(Duration freeUntilBefore, long percent) {
    CancellationPolicy {
        if (freeUntilBefore == null || freeUntilBefore.isNegative()) throw new IllegalArgumentException("bad free window");
        if (percent < 0 || percent > 100) throw new IllegalArgumentException("percent must be 0..100");
    }

    Money feeFor(Booking booking, Instant at) {
        Money total = booking.total();
        if (booking.status() == BookingStatus.HELD) return Money.zero(total.currency());       // nothing paid yet
        boolean early = !at.isAfter(booking.slot().start().minus(freeUntilBefore));            // "up to 24 hours before" includes 24
        return early ? Money.zero(total.currency()) : total.percent(percent);
    }
}

final class Workshop {                                   // application service: translation, wiring and sequence; no rules of its own
    private final Map<BayId, BaySchedule> schedules = new LinkedHashMap<>();
    private final OpeningHours hours;
    private final CancellationPolicy cancellation;
    private final Duration holdFor;
    private final BookingIds ids;
    private final Clock clock;
    private PriceList prices;                            // replaced as a whole, never edited in place

    Workshop(List<Bay> bays, OpeningHours hours, PriceList prices, CancellationPolicy cancellation,
             Duration holdFor, BookingIds ids, Clock clock) {
        for (Bay bay : bays)
            if (schedules.putIfAbsent(bay.id(), new BaySchedule(bay)) != null)
                throw new IllegalArgumentException("duplicate bay " + bay.id().value());
        this.hours = hours;
        this.prices = prices;
        this.cancellation = cancellation;
        this.holdFor = holdFor;
        this.ids = ids;
        this.clock = clock;
    }

    Booking book(Customer customer, Plate plate, BayId bayId, LocalDate day, LocalTime from, Duration length, List<Job> jobs) {
        BaySchedule schedule = schedule(bayId);
        TimeSlot slot = hours.slot(day, from, length);
        if (!hours.admits(slot)) throw new IllegalArgumentException("outside opening hours or off the grid");
        Instant now = clock.instant();
        if (!slot.start().isAfter(now)) throw new IllegalArgumentException("that slot has already started");
        List<BookingLine> lines = new ArrayList<>();
        for (Job job : jobs) lines.add(new BookingLine(job, customer.tier().price(prices.priceOf(job))));   // price agreed now
        Booking booking = new Booking(ids.next(), customer.id(), bayId, plate, slot, lines, now.plus(holdFor));
        schedule.hold(booking, now);
        return booking;
    }

    void pay(BookingId id)                  { scheduleOf(id).confirm(id, clock.instant()); }   // the charge itself goes through a PaymentGateway port
    void checkIn(BookingId id, Plate plate) { scheduleOf(id).checkIn(id, plate, clock.instant()); }
    void complete(BookingId id)             { scheduleOf(id).complete(id); }
    void markNoShow(BookingId id)           { scheduleOf(id).markNoShow(id, clock.instant()); }

    Money cancel(BookingId id) {
        Booking booking = find(id);
        Instant now = clock.instant();
        Money fee = cancellation.feeFor(booking, now);   // priced before the status changes
        scheduleOf(id).cancel(id, now);
        return fee;
    }

    void changePrices(PriceList next) {
        if (next == null) throw new IllegalArgumentException("prices required");
        prices = next;
    }
    PriceList prices() { return prices; }

    Booking find(BookingId id) {
        for (BaySchedule s : schedules.values()) {
            Optional<Booking> b = s.find(id);
            if (b.isPresent()) return b.get();
        }
        throw new IllegalArgumentException("no booking " + id.value());
    }

    private BaySchedule scheduleOf(BookingId id) { return schedule(find(id).bay()); }

    private BaySchedule schedule(BayId id) {
        BaySchedule s = schedules.get(id);
        if (s == null) throw new IllegalArgumentException("no bay " + id.value());
        return s;
    }
}
```

The **composition root** is where all the concrete choices meet: three bays, Copperline's hours, today's prices, a 24-hour free window, a 15-minute hold, sequential ids and whatever clock the caller provides. Then a whole day, scripted:

A service that starts making business decisions (pricing, clash checks) has become a God class: move the rule to the object that owns the data. <h2 id="sq-prices">Extension 1: Monday’s price rise</h2>

**The new sentence:** *"From Monday, oil changes are ₹1,699. Bookings already made keep their price."*

**Where it lands:** nowhere new — and that's the point. Because `BookingLine` copied the agreed price when the booking was made, the price list and the booking are separate facts. Changing the list is a new `PriceList` value swapped into the workshop; no existing booking can see it.

<Reveal q="Why not keep only the job on the line and look the price up from the current PriceList whenever total() is called?">

Then every booking's total would change whenever the price list changed: Priya's confirmed ₹1,499 oil change would silently become ₹1,699 on Monday. "The price agreed" and "today's price" are different facts with different lifetimes, so they get different homes. This is the same reason an order line in any shop stores the unit price — and the same reason the many-to-many link in Chapter 11 carries its own data.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.prices} labels={turn}>

```java group=workshop
List<String> mondayPriceRise() {
    LocalDate monday = LocalDate.of(2026, 3, 16), nextWeek = monday.plusDays(8);
    Workshop shop = copperline(new ManualClock(at(monday, 8, 0), IST));
    Customer priya = new Customer(new CustomerId("C-1"), "Priya");
    Plate car = new Plate("KA01ZZ0001");
    Duration half = Duration.ofMinutes(30);

    Booking before = shop.book(priya, car, new BayId("B1"), nextWeek, LocalTime.of(10, 0), half, List.of(Job.OIL_CHANGE));
    shop.changePrices(shop.prices().with(Job.OIL_CHANGE, Money.of("1699", "INR")));
    Booking after = shop.book(priya, car, new BayId("B1"), nextWeek, LocalTime.of(11, 0), half, List.of(Job.OIL_CHANGE));

}
```

The checker gets `["INR 1499.00", "INR 1699.00"]`: the booking made before the change keeps ₹1,499.00, the one made after pays ₹1,699.00, and the old `PriceList` value is unchanged too.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** the owner adds *"…but gold customers who booked before Monday get the new, lower price if we ever cut prices."* Which object now needs to know about price changes after booking — and does that make the line's price an entity, or still a value?

<h2 id="sq-pair">Extension 2: Two cars, side by side</h2>

**The new sentence:** *"Fleet customers can book two cars into two bays at the same time — both or neither."*

**Where it lands:** across two aggregates. Each `BaySchedule` guards its own bay, and neither can see the other. "Both or neither" can't be one transaction without merging the schedules into one big aggregate. So it becomes a small **process**: hold the first, try the second, and if the second refuses, **compensate** by cancelling the first. A held booking cancels free, so the compensation is cheap and safe.

<Reveal q="When would you merge the two schedules into one aggregate instead?">

When the cross-bay rule is the *common* case rather than the exception — for example, if most bookings needed two bays at once. Then the "both or neither" rule is a true invariant of a bigger unit, and you'd pay for the bigger boundary (more contention) to keep it in one step. For an occasional fleet booking, a two-step process with compensation keeps every everyday booking fast. The War Room's <a href={`${BASE}lld/war/patterns-why/#11-saga-compensation--transactional-cant-reach-outside-the-database`}>saga compensation dossier</a> is the production version: a transaction can't reach outside its boundary, so the code tracks what it created and undoes it.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.pair} labels={turn}>

```java group=workshop
final class PairedHold {                                  // both bays or neither, across two aggregates
    private final Workshop shop;
    PairedHold(Workshop shop) { this.shop = shop; }

    List<Booking> holdBoth(Customer customer, Plate firstCar, BayId firstBay, Plate secondCar, BayId secondBay,
                           LocalDate day, LocalTime from, Duration length, List<Job> jobs) {
        Booking first = shop.book(customer, firstCar, firstBay, day, from, length, jobs);
        try {
            Booking second = shop.book(customer, secondCar, secondBay, day, from, length, jobs);
            return List.of(first, second);
        } catch (RuntimeException refused) {
            shop.cancel(first.id());                      // compensate: give the first bay back
            throw refused;
        }
    }
}
```

A pair of brake checks in B1 and B3 holds both. A pair in B1 and B2 fails — B2 has no lift — and the checker confirms that the B1 hold was cancelled (fee ₹0), the original "B2 cannot do BRAKE_CHECK" error reached the caller, and the B1 slot can be booked again straight away.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** between the first hold and its compensation, the B1 slot is briefly unavailable to everyone else. Is that acceptable for Copperline? What would make it unacceptable — and what would you change in the process (not in `BaySchedule`)?

<h2 id="sq-loyalty">Extension 3: Five services and you’re gold</h2>

**The new sentence:** *"After five completed services, a customer becomes gold automatically."*

**Where it lands:** another cross-aggregate rule. The fact ("this booking completed") happens in a `BaySchedule`; the reaction ("upgrade the customer") happens to a `Customer`. Reference by id, eventual consistency: the booking completes in its own step, and a **follow-up** reacts to it. Because follow-ups in real systems get delivered twice (a retried message, a re-run job), **the reaction must be idempotent**: telling it about the same booking twice must count once.

<Reveal q="Why not have Booking.complete() call customer.upgradeTo(GOLD) when the count reaches five?">

Because `Booking` holds a `CustomerId`, not a `Customer`, on purpose. If completing a booking reached into the customer, one method would change two aggregates, loading a booking would drag the customer along, and the booking would need to know the loyalty rule — a second reason to change. Keeping the reaction outside lets marketing change "five" to "four" without touching the lifecycle of a booking.

</Reveal>

<HintLadder hints={hints.loyalty} labels={turn}>

```java group=workshop
final class LoyaltyDesk {                                 // reacts after a booking completes; safe to tell twice
    private final int threshold;
    private final Map<CustomerId, Set<BookingId>> completed = new HashMap<>();

    LoyaltyDesk(int threshold) {
        if (threshold <= 0) throw new IllegalArgumentException("threshold must be positive");
        this.threshold = threshold;
    }

    void onCompleted(Booking booking, Customer customer) {
        if (booking.status() != BookingStatus.COMPLETED) throw new IllegalArgumentException(booking.id().value() + " is not completed");
        if (!booking.customer().equals(customer.id())) throw new IllegalArgumentException(booking.id().value() + " belongs to someone else");
        Set<BookingId> seen = completed.computeIfAbsent(customer.id(), k -> new HashSet<>());
        if (seen.add(booking.id()) && seen.size() >= threshold && customer.tier() == Tier.STANDARD)
            customer.upgradeTo(Tier.GOLD);
    }

    int completedCount(CustomerId id) { return completed.getOrDefault(id, Set.of()).size(); }
}
```

The checker completes six real bookings through the workshop and tells the desk about each one twice. The count tracks distinct bookings (6, not 12), the customer becomes GOLD exactly at the fifth, and the sixth doesn't try to upgrade again. A booking that is only confirmed is refused, and so is someone else's.

</HintLadder>

**Transfer test:** a completed booking is later refunded and reversed. Does the customer lose a completed service, and possibly their gold tier? Which object would you have to change, and does `Customer.upgradeTo`'s "promotions only" rule still hold?

<h2 id="interview-script">Say it in the room</h2>

Before any talk of patterns, interviewers usually ask: walk me through how you modelled this. Open with what must always be true, then give the boundaries.

**The 90-second answer** (adapt the nouns):

> "I started from the brief's nouns and sorted them. Booking, Bay, Customer and Mechanic are entities: equality by a typed id, the id is final, and state changes only through methods. TimeSlot, Plate and Money are value objects: records, immutable, validated in the compact constructor. Money is paise in a long with a currency, never a double, and percentages round half up in one place. Time is Instants with one ZoneId for the opening hours, and an injected Clock so I can test hold expiry. The booking lifecycle is an enum with its legal edges; Booking has no status setter, just confirm, cancel, checkIn, complete, expire and markNoShow, each checking the edge and its own condition before changing anything. Each rule lives with the data it needs: a booking checks that its slot fits its jobs; the no-overlap rule needs every booking of a bay, so each bay has a BaySchedule aggregate whose hold() is the only door, and in production that's one transaction. Bookings hold the customer's id, not the customer, and copy the agreed price onto their lines. Gold is a Tier field, not a subclass, because customers get upgraded. Mechanics-to-bookings is many-to-many with a role, so it's an Assignment record in a Roster indexed both ways."

**Five follow-ups you will get:**

<Reveal q="1 · Why isn't Booking a record? It's mostly data.">

Because it's an entity: it changes (held, confirmed, checked in) and stays the same booking. A record's `equals` compares every component, which would make a confirmed booking unequal to its held self — Chapter 3's vanishing-booking bug — and a record's fields are final, so every transition would have to build a new object with the same id. Records are for values: `TimeSlot`, `Money`, `Plate`, `BookingLine`.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="2 · Where exactly is 'no double booking' enforced, and what happens under concurrency?">

In `BaySchedule.hold`, the aggregate root for one bay: it sweeps expired holds, checks every occupying booking for overlap, and adds the new one, all in one step, and it's the only way to add a booking. Under concurrency, the boundary is what you lock: in memory, a lock per schedule; in a database, one transaction per schedule with a row lock or a version column. Bookings for different bays never contend. A check in a service ("is it free? then save") fails under concurrency, because two requests can both see the slot as free.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="3 · Why long paise instead of BigDecimal?">

All the workshop's arithmetic is adding, subtracting, multiplying by counts and taking whole percentages of whole-rupee prices. A long of paise does all of that exactly, it's fast, and the one rounding rule (half up, in `percent`) lives in one method. I'd switch to `BigDecimal` for tax rates or FX with many decimal places. Either way it's wrapped in `Money` with its currency — never a bare number and never a `double`.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="4 · How do you model many-to-many in objects?">

First ask which directions are queried and whether the relationship has data. One direction only: a collection on one side (`Mechanic`'s `EnumSet` of skills), and compute the reverse when asked. Data on the relationship: make it an object (`Assignment` with a role, `BookingLine` with the agreed price), give it one owner, and keep one index per direction — a join table with a reverse index, in memory. Avoid two collections that must be kept in sync by hand.

</Reveal>

<Reveal q="5 · Isn't BaySchedule plus Workshop plus Booking over-engineered for 90 minutes?">

Each one has a reason I can name. `Booking` owns rules that need only itself. `BaySchedule` exists because one rule needs every booking of a bay, and without it the overlap check ends up in a service where other code can skip it. `Workshop` only translates and wires — wall-clock times into Instants, ids, the clock — and has no business rules. There are no interfaces except where something stands in: `BookingIds` and `Clock`, for tests. If the interviewer says it's one bay, I'd fold the schedule into the workshop and say so.

</Reveal>

<h2 id="cheat-sheet">Cheat sheet</h2>

| When you see… | Model it as… | The promise to keep |
|---|---|---|
| A noun with a history: "the same booking, now paid" | **Entity**: `final class`, typed id, guarded methods | Equality by id (or reference); the id never changes |
| A noun that is its data: an amount, a slot, a plate | **Value object**: a `record` | Immutable; validated in the compact constructor; canonical form; copy mutable components |
| A closed set: statuses, job types, tiers | **Enum with behaviour** | Data and rules live on the constants; `EnumSet`/`EnumMap` |
| Money | `Money(long minor, Currency)` or `BigDecimal` | Never `double`; one rounding rule; allocate leftovers |
| "When" | `Instant`; local types + one `ZoneId` for human rules; an injected `Clock` | Convert at the edges; days aren't always 24 hours |
| An id | A record per kind of id, from an injected generator | No swapped arguments; never promise gapless ids |
| A rule needing only one object's data | That object's constructor or method | Constructed means valid; check before change |
| A status with legal and illegal moves | Enum edges + one named method per transition | No setter, ever; refused moves change nothing |
| `getX()` then `setX()` on the same object | **Tell, don't ask**: move the decision inside | Getters return values or read-only views |
| A lasting reference / owned parts / shared parts / a parameter | Association / composition / aggregation / dependency | Ask what happens to the other end when this one dies |
| "A kind of", but it can change, or only data differs | A field (role, tier, capability set) | Inheritance only for substitutable, permanent, behaviour-varying subtypes |
| A constructor calling an overridable method | Make the class `final`, or stop the call | Effective Java Item 19 |
| Many-to-many | One-way collection, or a link object with one owner and an index per direction | Never two hand-synced collections |
| A rule spanning several entities | An **aggregate** whose root is the only door | Smallest boundary that sees the whole rule; others by id |
| A rule spanning aggregates | A follow-up step: compensation, or an idempotent reaction | One transaction per aggregate |

## Five mistakes to leave behind

1. **`@Data` (or equals over every field) on an entity.** The first hash collection that sees it change loses it.
2. **Money in `double`.** 1.10 + 2.20 is 3.3000000000000003. Paise in a `long`, or `BigDecimal` from strings, inside `Money`.
3. **A setter for the status.** Every caller is then trusted with the state machine, and the nightly importer will betray you.
4. **A subclass for a role.** `GoldCustomer` can't be reached by upgrading a `Customer`. Roles and capabilities are fields.
5. **The check outside the boundary.** "Is it free? Then save" in a service: other doors skip it, and concurrent requests beat it. The root checks and changes in one step.

**Practice drill: brief to model.** Take a one-page brief (a car wash, a library, a gym, a clinic) and give yourself ten minutes to produce three things: the noun table with a verdict for each, the invariants with their owners, and the arrows with a reason for each. Then ask yourself three "one small change" questions and say which object absorbs each before you type. Do one rep on a many-to-many with data on the relationship (students and courses with grades; playlists and songs with positions), in objects *and* in tables, because it is a common weak spot.

**Look these up yourself**

- **Sealed interfaces with record implementations** — states that carry their own data (a `CheckedIn` state that holds the check-in time), so "null unless checked in" disappears.
- **Identity Map and Unit of Work** (Fowler, *PoEAA*) — how persistence layers keep one object per id in memory, which makes reference equality safe for entities.
- **Optimistic locking with a version column** (JPA's `@Version`) — the production form of "one aggregate, one transaction".
- **Domain events** — how "a booking completed" travels to the loyalty desk in a real system, and why consumers must be idempotent.
- **Primitive obsession** — the code smell that typed ids, `Money` and `Plate` cure.
- **Event sourcing** — storing the transitions instead of the current status, so history can't be corrupted by a backwards move.
- **UUIDv7 (RFC 9562)** — time-ordered UUIDs that index well, against random version 4.
- **JPA `@Embeddable`** — how value objects are stored as columns of their entity's table rather than as tables of their own.
